# Frameworks: Naval Ravikant

25 frameworks, roughly most-used first (the seat's core leverage and equity frameworks lead), built from 19 extract batches over 86 usable sources (2005 to 2026). "Sources" counts distinct sources across all batches where the idea is used (ids listed). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short descriptive name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from his stated thresholds, not quotes. The seat's question (leverage and equity you own versus rented time) leans hardest on 1, 2, 3, 4, and 11.

---

## 1. Forms of leverage: labor, capital, code and media (and now AI)
- **What it is:** Leverage is a force multiplier on your judgment. Labor (people working for you) is the oldest and worst form: costly to manage and fought over. Capital multiplies a single decision but someone has to grant it to you. Code and media (software, writing, podcasts, video, anything with no marginal cost of replication) need nobody's permission and are where new fortunes are made. He has said the same thing since 2009: the internet hands one person more leverage than any earlier generation had (S018), and the computer is the first tool since the stone axe that levers up an individual without permission (S055). In 2025 he adds AI and agents as the newest layer (S034).
- **Parts:** Name the form of leverage the plan uses; prefer permissionless (code, media) over permissioned (labor, capital); use the minimum labor needed to unlock the others; look for leverage already embedded in the system (APIs, cloud, platforms) before building or hiring (S088); a tech company stacks all of them.
- **When to use:** Any plan that grows by adding hours or people; a service business versus a product; whether to hire or build; how a solo founder should scale.
- **How to score:** 5 if output scales through code or media at near-zero marginal cost and the founder's hours are not the bottleneck; 3 if capital or a small team does the multiplying; 1 if revenue rises only with more hours or more people. His numbers for how cheap leverage got (2009): "What used to cost $1M-$2M to set up, now costs $10K." (S018)
- **Quote:** "labor is the shittiest form of leverage" (S091) Also: "The computer is the first tool since maybe the stone axe, that an individual can use to gain massive leverage, without permission from anyone else." (S055) And: "there's just so much embedded leverage in the system now that a single person with a lever can truly move the earth" (S088)
- **Sources:** 13 (S001, S002, S017, S018, S032, S034, S041, S046, S055, S071, S081, S088, S091)

## 2. Own equity, do not rent your time (the claims hierarchy)
- **What it is:** You cannot get rich while income stops when you stop working. Every business has four sets of claimants: customers get product, creditors get fixed interest, employees get salary and security, and owners get nothing guaranteed but all the upside (S091). Equity can be a company stake, an investment, IP, or a personal brand that accrues to you (S017). Taking accountability is an equity position in your own work.
- **Parts:** Is the founder an owner, investor, or brand holder, or only paid for time; renting time is acceptable early, only while learning and saving, and preferably in a field where apprenticeship is the only way to learn (S041); in pre-traction companies the first engineers are late founders and get founder equity (S019); align partners through shared ownership rather than terms.
- **When to use:** Fee versus salary versus revenue share versus ownership; pricing a service business; paying early hires, partners, and advisors; a consultant deciding whether to productize.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder owns an asset that earns without them (product, IP, company stake, brand); 3 if income mixes fees with equity or the rented time is a deliberate apprenticeship with savings; 1 if income stops when they stop working and nothing is being learned or owned.
- **Quote:** "you must own a piece of a business if you want a path to financial freedom there's literally no other way" (S091) Also: "the owners actually don't get anything guaranteed ... but they get all the upside" (S091) And, his tweet as read back by the host: "You will need to rent your time to get started. This is only acceptable when you are learning and saving." (tweet read by Nivi, S041)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S017, S019, S024, S032, S041, S063, S089, S091)

## 3. Specific knowledge, accountability, leverage (productize yourself)
- **What it is:** The ingredients of wealth. Specific knowledge is what you know that cannot be trained, or you could be replaced. Accountability puts your name on the outcome and the risk. Leverage scales it; judgment steers it. "Productize yourself" is his two-word summary: turn what only you do into a product you own.
- **Parts:** Specific knowledge feels like play to you and looks like work to others (S091), is found through genuine curiosity and by acting in hard situations (S029), and is usually a skill stack of two or three things (S034). Timely knowledge tracks the frontier and expires; timeless knowledge (persuasion) always applies, so stack both (S041). The sequence: accountability is permissionless and comes first, specific knowledge follows from taking on what others cannot do, judgment takes years, and leverage is granted after judgment is shown (S041). By 2023 he adds that anything an AI can spit back has no scarcity premium; the monetizable kind sits at the edge of what humanity knows (S084).
- **When to use:** Founder-market fit; a creator or consultant deciding whether to productize; personal brand decisions; "why you" questions from investors.
- **How to score:** Ask why this founder, at scale. 5 if they have a rare skill stack, sell under their own name, and scale it with code or media; 3 if two of the three; 1 if the work is trainable, commoditized, or something a model can already do.
- **Quote:** "Productize has specific knowledge and leverage. Yourself has uniqueness and accountability." (S001) Also: "you want to productize yourself into a business, and then you want to own that business." (S063) And: "you end up selling a product that is simply an extension of who you uniquely are and nobody can beat you at that because it's just you being you" (S091)
- **Sources:** 12 (S001, S005, S017, S029, S030, S034, S041, S043, S062, S063, S084, S091)

## 4. Judgment in an age of infinite leverage
- **What it is:** When capital, code, media, and teams multiply every decision, being right a little more often beats working more. The ideal is one good call a year multiplied by leverage. Judgment is built by doing, honest reflection, and iteration (not repetition) until it becomes taste you cannot articulate (S032).
- **Parts:** Decision quality over effort; a calm, clear mind as the precondition (S033, S036); the arc from explicit reasoning to subconscious judgment to taste (S032); prepare before the decision arrives so the subconscious can move fast (S025); judgment takes five to 15 years to verify in early-stage investing (S025) and about ten (S056), so self-reported judgment counts for little.
- **When to use:** Whether to add effort or think harder; hiring a leader; any high-stakes call that capital or code will multiply; whether the founder's week is spent on decisions or on tasks.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder slowed down to think on a high-leverage call and has a track record that shows judgment; 1 if they are grinding hours on a decision their leverage will amplify, or deciding while anxious or under strong desire for an outcome (S031).
- **Quote:** "In an age of infinite leverage, judgment becomes the most important skill." (S001) Also: "The ideal would be to make money with your mind, not with your time." (S063) And: "the quality of your decision- making dominates everything" (S033)
- **Sources:** 10 (S001, S025, S031, S032, S033, S035, S036, S041, S056, S063)

## 5. Power law, asymmetric bets, and no ruin
- **What it is:** Returns are nonlinear. The number one winner outweighs all the rest combined, so take bets with capped downside and open upside, take many of them, and never let one bet wipe you out. Ruin is the one outcome that ends the game, and in business it usually comes from cutting corners, not betting too much.
- **Parts:** Capped downside, 10x to 1,000x upside (Bitcoin as "an asymmetric bet", S083); count of swings over size of swings (S045); treat seed companies as cheap experiments priced for a 1-in-10 hit rate (S088); hold winners and do not sell early (S025, S080); never invest money you are not OK losing (S025); stay out of jail; be optimistic in general and skeptical of each specific bet (S092).
- **When to use:** A safe small win versus a long shot; how to spread effort across projects; personal guarantees, big debt, shortcuts on law or ethics; whether a product line can ever be the big one.
- **How to score:** 5 if loss is capped and survivable and upside is 100x or more; 3 if upside is around 10x; 1 if upside is linear (2x or 3x) or one bad outcome wipes out the founder, the company, or their name. Portfolio guidance: nine zeros and one 1,000x beats ten 2x to 3x outcomes (S025).
- **Quote:** "Angel investing is a game of exceptional outcomes; it's not a game of averages." (S025) Also: "Your outcome scales more with the number of bets than the size of the bets." (S045) And: "The number one way people get ruined in modern business is not by betting too much; it's by cutting corners and doing unethical or downright illegal things." (S001)
- **Sources:** 12 (S001, S011, S018, S025, S031, S033, S044, S045, S080, S083, S088, S092)

## 6. Intelligence, energy, integrity *(Buffett's test, his most-repeated people screen)*
- **What it is:** The three-part checklist for any partner, cofounder, hire, or founder he backs. Intelligence is depth of understanding of the specific problem, not years of experience (S062). Energy is the stamina for a brutally hard job. Integrity is what someone does despite the money, and it is the hardest to read because it shows only over time and under high stakes.
- **Parts:** All three, no trade-offs; read integrity from actions, especially how someone treats third parties and their enemies (S074, S063); someone who talks a lot about their own honesty is a warning sign (S031); niceness is easy to fake at low stakes (S025); prefer partners who are irrationally ethical (S059).
- **When to use:** Any hire, cofounder, partner, or investor; deciding whom to back; a smart, driven candidate with a doubtful record.
- **How to score:** 5 if the person clears all three with evidence from shared work; 1 if any one leg is missing. His shorthand for the failure case is the smart, hardworking crook. If a founder wrongs another shareholder to please you, score integrity at 1: "if they can do it to them they can do it to me" (S074).
- **Quote:** "pick ones that have high intelligence, high energy, and high integrity, I find that's the three-part checklist that you cannot compromise on." (S001) Also: "if you have someone who's high intelligence and high energy but a low Integrity what you've got is a hardworking smart Crook" (S074) And: "the old Warren Buffet line is intelligence energy and integrity yeah uh and you can't give up in any of the three" (S080)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S025, S031, S056, S057, S059, S062, S074, S080)

## 7. Long-term games with long-term people (compound interest)
- **What it is:** Every return in money, relationships, and learning comes from compound interest, and it arrives at the end of the curve. Trust only builds in repeated games, so pick an industry and people that will be around for decades and play cooperatively.
- **Parts:** Stay where your network lives (each industry hop resets trust to zero) (S004); ask of any partner whether you will know them in ten years (S088); be wary of people who overoptimize for the moment (S088); leave something on the table in a negotiation (S088); bake the pie rather than cut it; the journey must be enjoyable or you burn out and sell before the compounding arrives (S089).
- **When to use:** Partnerships, deal terms, pivots into a new industry, whether to squeeze a counterparty, whether to sell early.
- **How to score:** 5 if the move builds trust with people the founder will deal with for years and keeps them in the game long enough to compound; 1 if it wins this deal at the cost of reputation or resets the network. He calls long-term mentality "probably the single most important indicator of an entrepreneur success" (S087).
- **Quote:** "Essentially, all the benefits in life come from compound interests. Whether it's in relationships, or making money, or in learning." (S004) Also: "In a longterm game, it's positive sum. We're all baking the pie together." (S004) And: "money has karma, too. And uh if you treat it badly, it will treat you badly." (S088)
- **Sources:** 10 (S001, S004, S016, S031, S063, S069, S074, S087, S088, S089)

## 8. Network effects, zero marginal cost, scale economies *(unnamed checklist)*
- **What it is:** The business-model version of leverage. The three travel together and produce winner-take-all markets: one or a few head players, a long tail of individuals, and a hollowed-out middle. Craigslist was his early example: near-zero cost plus network effects and critical mass per category made "a supercharged monopoly in the making" (S046).
- **Parts:** Do customers add value to each other; does the next unit cost nothing; does unit cost fall with volume; are you number one in the category or a niche you keep redefining; is adoption risk unquantifiable before launch, which means data will not convince investors and you may need to bootstrap (S053). Once a subsidized rival wins a network-effects market, it owns the market and can raise prices (S070).
- **When to use:** Choosing a model or market; entering a crowded category; a mid-sized player that relies on geography or regulation; a 5 to 20 person software company in an enterprise niche (S002).
- **How to score:** 5 if all three are present and the company can be first in its category or niche; 3 if one or two; 1 if it is a mid-sized player with linear costs in a market heading to one winner. Being number two in a network-effects market scores low.
- **Quote:** "when I win I own the whole market and I can raise prices and if you try to start up a competitor then it's too late I've got Network effects" (S070) Also: "Craigslist is a dot-org no more. It's a supercharged monopoly in the making." (S046) And, in 2026: "There is no demand for average." (S002)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S002, S006, S018, S046, S053, S070, S076, S087)

## 9. Wealth, money, status, and knowledge
- **What it is:** Wealth is assets that earn while you sleep; money is how wealth moves; status is rank. Wealth creation is positive sum; status is zero sum and always combative. From 2021 he increasingly uses David Deutsch's definition: wealth is the set of physical transformations you can bring about, and knowledge, not capital, is most of it (S072, S080, S084).
- **Parts:** Ask whether the plan builds assets and knowledge or buys rank; pursue wealth indirectly through creating value; overt status chasing signals low status (S029); fame chased for its own sake is hollow, earned fame is fine (S030); the company's real asset is the knowledge in its people (S032).
- **When to use:** Spend on image (office, events, awards, press) versus assets; fame-first content versus productized value; a founder who wants recognition.
- **How to score:** 5 if the move adds an asset that earns or knowledge the company owns; 1 if it buys rank, attention, or an award with no asset behind it.
- **Quote:** "Wealth is assets that earn while you sleep; it's the factory of robots cranking out things." (S001) Also: "knowledge is the big multiplier. It's not the capital." (S032) And: "wealth is a byproduct of knowledge" (S080)
- **View change:** In 2019 his definition was assets that earn while you sleep (S001). In 2026 he says that definition was narrow and driven by his own wish to make money, and he now prefers Deutsch's (S032). The newer one governs, though the old one still runs the seat's question.
- **Sources:** 10 (S001, S014, S015, S029, S030, S032, S036, S072, S080, S084)

## 10. Inputs versus outputs
- **What it is:** Pick work where hours in do not map to results out. In idea domains the output gap between people is 100x to 1,000x, and choosing the right thing to work on is an infinite difference. Companies that cannot measure outputs measure inputs instead, so make your outputs visible.
- **Parts:** Is the founder's best hour worth 100 of their average; are people paid and judged on results; does the team work in focused bursts rather than performed 80-hour weeks (S052); pay twice and expect ten times the output (S034).
- **When to use:** Compensation design, performance reviews, hiring, and the founder's own week.
- **How to score:** 5 if pay and progress are tied to outputs that leverage multiplies; 1 if work is billed, judged, or bragged about by hours or days.
- **Quote:** "First, measure outputs, not inputs, in yourself and your organization." (S052) Also: "judgment and accountability matter much more." (S041) And: "human creativity is nonlinear. Idea space is a nonlinear domain where the right idea can be worth almost an infinite amount, and the wrong idea, not so much." (S084)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S003, S012, S034, S041, S052, S072, S083, S084)

## 11. Be number one at something; keep redefining what you do
- **What it is:** Escape competition through authenticity. Competing means copying, and copying means converging where you have no edge. Instead, keep changing the objective until it matches your specific knowledge, position, and interests and you are number one at it, even in a niche. Founder, product, and market have to fit at once (S043).
- **Parts:** Is the founder the best in the world at this exact thing; if not, can the definition narrow until they are; are they copying a rival; is the product an extension of who they are.
- **When to use:** Positioning, crowded markets, a founder who watches competitors, choosing a niche.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder is plausibly number one in a niche defined by their own skill stack; 3 if they are top tier in a broad field; 1 if the plan is a copy of a rival's.
- **Quote:** "You want to be the best in the world at what you do, but keep redefining what you do until that's true." (S029) Also: "If you really want to get paid in this world, you want to be number one at whatever you do. It can be niche" (S043) And: "Escape competition through authenticity." (S001)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S002, S017, S029, S030, S043, S091)

## 12. Minimum-sized group (small is the future) *(his phrase in S011)*
- **What it is:** Use the smallest group of high-trust, highly competent people that can do the job. Large groups seek consensus, not truth, and never admit failure. Information technology lowers transaction costs, so the efficient size of a firm keeps shrinking (S017, S088), and AI pushes it further: one- and two-person software companies can now reach millions of users (S006).
- **Parts:** A fully interconnected graph with one light hub who holds the whole picture, which only works if every node is very capable (S009); no Slack, maker's time; one person holds the product in their head; add structure only when size forces it; expect more founders and very small teams, not fewer jobs (S003).
- **When to use:** Growing headcount, adding managers, process, or internal tools; deciding whether a hire or an agent should do the work.
- **How to score:** 5 if output grows without new layers and every person can navigate the flat graph; 1 if structure, managers, or headcount are added ahead of need.
- **Quote:** "So you want to be in the minimum-sized group to do the scope of the job that you set out to do." (S011) Also: "the actual efficient size of a company is shrinking very rapidly." (S088) And: "You truly can have one-person, two-person software companies now that can scale to millions upon millions of users and make billions upon billions of dollars." (S006)
- **Sources:** 9 (S003, S006, S007, S009, S011, S013, S017, S032, S088)

## 13. Feedback from reality, through iterations
- **What it is:** Only nature (physics) and free markets give feedback you cannot fool. Praise, awards, critics, peers, and groups give feedback shaped by the wish to get along. Mastery comes from the number of loops against that real feedback, not hours: do it, reflect honestly, change it, try again. Structures that cannot lose money cannot learn.
- **Parts:** Is the test against customers paying or physics working; how many real iterations a month; is the scorecard written by people with nothing at stake; cut losers fast and learn from them (S030, S088); principles only make sense once you have specifics to attach them to (S029, S032).
- **When to use:** Validating demand; reading a board's or community's approval; a founder planning instead of shipping; for-profit versus non-profit structure for a mission.
- **How to score:** 5 if the evidence is money paid or a system that works, gathered in many fast loops; 1 if it is applause, awards, a deck, or a group's own report.
- **Quote:** "nature and free markets are the gold standards" (S092) Also: "Iteration means you do something, then you honestly reflect upon the outcome, you make a change, and you try again." (S032) And: "If you want to change the world to a better place, the best way to do it is a for-profit because for-profits have to take feedback from reality." (S013)
- **Sources:** 11 (S007, S013, S029, S030, S032, S034, S037, S038, S072, S088, S092)

## 14. AI as leverage: smartest model, waste tokens, humans as verifiers *(2025 to 2026 cluster)*
- **What it is:** His current operating stance on AI. Always use the most intelligent model, because errors are invisible and compound across loops and every answer gets multiplied by leverage. Judge only your time and the final output, never token cost. Do not learn tricks; the model adapts to you faster than you adapt to it (S002). People shift from producing to verifying and standing behind the work. AI does not replace engineers; it lets engineers replace everyone else (S032).
- **Parts:** Smartest model always; run important queries through several models and cross-check (S002); throw several models at a problem and waste tokens to save time (S012); a human with taste and knowledge of the layer below directs the model (S006); use AI for retrieval and code, but write human-facing work yourself and compress any AI output before a person reads it (S035).
- **When to use:** Tooling and model spend; whether a task should still be done by a person; staffing around agents; any founder who saves on models while spending their own hours.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder buys the best intelligence available and spends their own time only on taste, verification, and judgment; 1 if they economize on model cost while burning their own hours, or hand the final call at the edge to a model.
- **Quote:** "Just waste tokens, save time. Don't look at the tokens either as inputs or outputs. Just look at your time, and look at the final output." (S003) Also, he will pay more for intelligence and is not cost sensitive on judgment, because the leverage makes it worth it (inferred from S035). And: "Humans are becoming verifiers." (S003)
- **View change:** In 2016 he called general AI far off (S016) and in 2019 said it was nowhere near achievable in our lifetimes (S017). He admits he was behind the curve from 2020 to 2022 (S035) and calls December 2025 the practical unlock for founders (S035). The 2026 view governs; he still says human plus AI beats pure AI for longer than people think (S003).
- **Sources:** 9 (S002, S003, S006, S008, S012, S016, S017, S032, S035)

## 15. Where the moat moved *(unnamed; 2025 to 2026)*
- **What it is:** Pure software is now easy to build, so it is no longer a moat or a venture bet. Value moves to hardware, network effects, model training, and design. China commoditizes software through open source to win on hardware (S035).
- **Parts:** Can an agent build this from a spec; does the business have hardware, network effects, proprietary training, or design that resists copying; does deep tech's lower competition justify its lower odds and harder hiring (S034).
- **When to use:** Pricing or funding a software product; whether software is the core asset; a SaaS founder in a saturated category.
- **How to score:** 5 if the asset is hard to copy even with agents (network, hardware, models, taste); 1 if it is a feature an agent could reproduce from a description.
- **Quote:** "pure software is uninvestable." (S006) Also: "If you're a venture investor now, you're looking for hardware, you're looking for network effects, you're looking for AI models." (S006) He also argues AI has now eaten software the way software ate the world, so software is commoditized (inferred from S035).
- **View change:** In 2009 he called web leverage "obscene" because products had no marginal cost (S018), and in 2019 coding was how to command the robot armies (S001). By 2026-04-28 pure software is uninvestable (S006). The newer view governs, and he still calls software engineers among the most leveraged people on earth (S002).
- **Sources:** 7 (S002, S003, S006, S012, S018, S034, S035)

## 16. Play that looks like work (zone of genius)
- **What it is:** Find what feels like play to you and looks like work to others. Someone who loves the work outlasts someone who does not, so money arrives as a byproduct. Spend all your time in the thing you are world-class at; whatever you hate, someone else is world-class at, which makes it a recruiting problem, not an execution problem (S089).
- **Parts:** Would the founder do it unpaid and already does it daily; do others notice the output unprompted (S034); is it one overwhelming desire rather than several (S093); is the work a detour to something else; burnout counts as evidence the fit is wrong (S007, S034).
- **When to use:** Picking a business or a marketing medium; a founder stuck in a role they hate; whether to step down as CEO.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder would do it unpaid and spends the hard parts on someone else's zone of genius; 1 if it was chosen only for money, or the founder is grinding through work they dislike.
- **Quote:** "it should feel like play to you but look like work to others. That's really what you want to be doing." (S032) Also: "it really ends up being a recruiting problem, not a execution problem." (S089) And, on what drives him now: "to me creating business is play" (S017)
- **Sources:** 9 (S007, S017, S030, S032, S034, S058, S089, S091, S093)

## 17. Curate people (geniuses only)
- **What it is:** The first people are the company's DNA, so the founder recruits personally, holds an uncompromising bar, and fires to keep it. Look for self-motivated, low-ego people who solve problems without being told: barrels who set direction, not bullets who take orders (S034). Measure someone by how much of your attention they use per unit of output (S080).
- **Parts:** Collect geniuses even without a slot; the first engineers are late founders with founder equity (S019); recruit founders whose category failed through no fault of their own and be generous with equity (S089); pay twice and expect ten times the output; hire slowly, fire fast (S034); do not keep a mediocre, reliable performer (S052) or a good person who makes you miserable (S089).
- **When to use:** Any hire; outsourcing recruiting; equity for early hires; a team that has stopped impressing new candidates.
- **How to score:** 5 if the person is a self-directed barrel the founder recruited directly; 1 if hired to fill a slot, needs to be told what to do, or is high ego. Guidance from S007: you are lucky to hire one genius a month.
- **Quote:** "Collect geniuses. Warehouse them. You'll never regret it." (S007) Also: "Your first two engineers? They're just late founders. Treat them as such." (S019) And: "Nothing damages a startup like a mediocre and reliable performer." (S052)
- **Sources:** 7 (S007, S011, S019, S034, S052, S080, S089)

## 18. Search, then commit; no by default *(unnamed decision heuristics)*
- **What it is:** Life is a search function. Early, say yes widely to find the right work, people, and place, because who, what, and where decide most outcomes. Once you find the fit, switch to exploitation: say no by default and compound. For any single choice: if you cannot decide, the answer is no; if two paths look equal, take the one that hurts more in the short term; prefer the choice that leaves you calmer later.
- **Parts:** Spend deciding time in proportion to the commitment (a four-year decision deserves a year) (S030); not a hell yes means no (S016); if you are not willing to do it now, do not commit to it for later (S031); you can always change it, accept it, or leave it (S062, S074).
- **When to use:** A founder adding a second business; new offers, partnerships, speaking slots; whether to keep testing or commit.
- **How to score:** An early founder sampling widely scores fine; a founder with traction who keeps opening new fronts scores low. A yes given to avoid disappointing someone scores 1.
- **Quote:** "Life is a search function." (S034) Also: "if you can't decide, the answer is no" (S030) And, repeating his trainer Jerzy Gregorek's saying: "easy choices hard life hard choices easy life" (S031)
- **Sources:** 8 (S016, S030, S031, S032, S034, S062, S074, S093)

## 19. Contracts, optionality, and the walk-away deal
- **What it is:** A contract is two parties constraining their future in exchange for working together, a stag hunt for a bigger shared prize. Options are valuable and the world is large, so sign only if the deal is among your best paths. Compromise is the enemy of a great business, so walk from suboptimal deals.
- **Parts:** Judge money by its terms, not its source (S022); write the individual partner's name into the term sheet (S049); do not take a valuation above a third of a modest exit, or investors can block the exit (S049); clean terms (1x preference, no veto on arm's-length deals, no option pool shuffle) (S051); an NDA filters out all but desperate investors (S061); fight only if letting it go would cost real sleep.
- **When to use:** Term sheets, partnerships, exclusivity, board seats, preferred stock, vetoes, and disputes.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder has a real alternative and the deal keeps their options and control; 1 if they are signing under time pressure into terms they cannot unwind.
- **Quote:** "You're closing down your options. And the world is a big place. Optionality is very powerful." (S011) Also: "I've just learned over the years that compromise is the enemy of building a great business." (S011) And: "The individual VC partner on your Board matters a lot more than the firm's name does, so write the partner's name into the termsheet." (S049)
- **Sources:** 6 (S011, S022, S049, S051, S061, S088)

## 20. When and how to raise (how funding markets behave)
- **What it is:** Private investors herd rationally, because decisions are serial and information is private (S020). In any region the safest companies get funded first and seed money comes last, so frontier founders bootstrap to real returns or move (S021). VC was a bundle of advice, control, and money that is coming apart (S022, S049). Many good businesses do not need funding at all, and adding it can ruin them (S088).
- **Parts:** No raise before product-market fit (S057); be exceptional in at least one of traction, team, product, social proof, or pitch (S065); raise when your own excitement clears a threshold, not on a calendar (S011); keep valuation discipline, since what wipes out portfolios is raising too much at too high a price later (S080); downstream financing risk is the main risk an early investor carries (S021).
- **When to use:** Whether to raise at all; which investor type to take; where to base a company; how much and at what price.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder can fund growth from revenue, or raises after fit from a position of strength on clean terms; 1 if the plan needs several future rounds in a market without them, or the money will force behavior the business does not need.
- **Quote:** "Iterate like crazy until you find product/market fit . If you don't find it, do not raise money, do not pass go. Start over." (S057) Also: "there are a lot of great businesses that can be built without funding but the moment you add funding it ruins them" (S088) And: "It is rational for private investors to move in herds." (S020)
- **View change:** From 2005 to 2019 he built funding infrastructure and taught founders to raise (S049, S021, S024). By 2025 to 2026 he is skeptical of most VCs (S007) and points out that the greatest companies were self-financed or broke out before they needed much venture money (S032). The newer view governs for his own companies.
- **Sources:** 11 (S007, S011, S020, S021, S022, S032, S049, S057, S065, S080, S088)

## 21. Capital, judgment, dealflow (non-consensus and right)
- **What it is:** His angel-investing model. You need capital, judgment, and dealflow; dealflow is not access, and access comes from a brand founders trust. The money is made when you are right and the room disagrees. The best deals look strange, and each is allowed one fatal flaw that lets conventional investors pass (S025).
- **Parts:** Start in your own network; hunting for deals lowers returns (S024); first-time founders take market risk, repeat founders take execution risk (S025); never forward a deal you are not investing in (S056); a hackable consumer space with no prototype is a poor sign (S056); great founders are not that coachable (S025).
- **When to use:** Angel bets, choosing which opportunities to back, judging a weird idea the room dislikes.
- **How to score:** Guidance from S024: if you do more than one in ten deals you look at, you are too optimistic. Score 5 for a non-consensus bet with one flaw and a great founder; 1 for a consensus bet in a hot market found through press.
- **Quote:** "The three things it takes to get into investing are capital, judgment and dealflow." (S024) Also: "In this industry, you get paid for being right when everybody else is wrong." (S062) And: "If you do more than one out of every 10 deals that you look at, you're probably being too optimistic." (S024)
- **Sources:** 6 (S024, S025, S044, S056, S062, S080)

## 22. Aspirational hourly rate and low burn rate
- **What it is:** Two numbers that guard the founder's time and freedom. Pick an hourly rate that feels absurdly high and never spend time below it; outsource or drop any task that costs less. Keep your burn rate low; driving it toward zero is one of his paths to being retired, next to passive income that covers it (S017, paraphrase).
- **Parts:** Set the rate; hire out anything cheaper; skip non-transactional meetings, conferences, and business travel (S001, S017, S083); keep an empty calendar because a busy one kills creativity (S089); start contact in writing and escalate only on interest (S023).
- **When to use:** The founder's calendar; doing work a hire could do; meetings, travel, small savings; a founder deciding whether they can afford to take the leap.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder spends their week on work worth the rate and a burn rate low enough to wait for compounding; 1 if they do tasks they could buy for a tenth of it, or have a burn rate that forces them back to renting time.
- **Quote:** "If you can outsource something ... for less than your hourly rate, outsource it or don't do it." (S001) Also: "keep your burn rate low" (S017) And: "a busy calendar is the death of creativity." (S089)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S016, S017, S023, S030, S083, S089)

## 23. Build hard things people clearly want (technical risk over market risk) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Prefer high technical risk with low market risk: if you can deliver it, people will want it. Before product-market fit comes passion-market fit: the internet is efficiently arbitraged, so you need an almost irrational obsession with a market to find the precise fit (S058).
- **Parts:** If it works, is demand obvious; has the founder been obsessed with this market for a long time; is there a prototype; for consumer products, is there traction before money (S087); is there an unsolved problem the team cannot even theorize a fix for, which will not get easier later (S080).
- **When to use:** Choosing what to build; a clever product with unclear demand; a founder chasing a trend.
- **How to score:** 5 if demand is certain and the hard part is building; 1 if it is easy to build and nobody knows whether anyone wants it, or the founder is trend chasing.
- **Quote:** "you want to build something that if people get it if you can deliver it you know people will want it and it's just hard to build as opposed to you build it and you don't know if they want it" (S070) Also: "the only way you're likely to find product-market fit is if you're almost irrationally obsessed with the market" (S058)
- **Sources:** 5 (S057, S058, S070, S080, S087)

## 24. Learn to sell, learn to build (the founding pair)
- **What it is:** Two broad skills. Building covers design, development, manufacturing, and operations; selling covers marketing, recruiting, fundraising, and PR. The classic team is one world-class builder and one world-class seller; one person who does both can create industries.
- **Parts:** The ideal team is two founders with a history of working together, similar age and financial standing, and mutual respect (S059); aligned motives (S059); vesting set up before any conflict (S059); find cofounders through shared work, not networking (S034); start with building, add selling later because sales scale with reputation; writing is selling for technical people (S005).
- **When to use:** Founding team gaps; a technical founder avoiding sales; picking a cofounder; three or more founders.
- **How to score:** 5 if the team has a world-class builder and a world-class seller who have worked together; 1 if it has neither, or the founders want different things (product, money, fame).
- **Quote:** "I think the real giants in any field are the people who can both build and sell." (S005) Also: "The ideal founding team is two people, with a history of working together, of similar age and financial standing, with mutual respect." (S059) And: "You wouldn't marry someone you'd just met. Date first." (S059)
- **Sources:** 3 (S005, S034, S059)

## 25. Good products are hard to vary
- **What it is:** From Deutsch's test for good explanations. A design is right when the parts constrain each other so no detail can change without breaking the whole, and a hard-to-vary product reaches markets its makers never planned for. If you can still vary it materially, you have not found the design yet.
- **Parts:** Remove parts before you optimize (S028); you cannot make a product simple enough, and choices left in settings are an abdication (S007); one opinionated holder of the vision.
- **When to use:** Product scope, feature creep, multiple versions or tiers, design as a moat.
- **How to score:** 5 if every feature is load-bearing and rivals cannot materially improve it by varying it; 1 if parts could be swapped freely.
- **Quote:** "good products are very hard to vary. they do have a very unique set of interlocking parts that then allows them to be greater than the whole." (S032)
- **Sources:** 5 (S007, S028, S032, S037, S072)
