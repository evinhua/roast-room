# Frameworks: Sara Blakely

Built from 54 usable sources of 60 fetched (keynotes, long interviews, podcasts, live streams, a commencement address, and short web pieces, 2013 to 2026, about 331,000 words; see `sources.md`), merged from nine batches of verified notes covering 52 of them (S055 and S056 were read directly). 25 frameworks, roughly most-used first. "Sources" counts distinct sources where she uses the idea (ids listed). She names few of her tools, so most names are short descriptive ones marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from her own thresholds and habits, not quotes. Her later brand is Sneex (2024); captions spell it "sneaks", which stays only inside quotes.

## 1. Make it, sell it, build awareness (start small, think big, scale fast)
- **What it is:** Her whole early operating plan. Start with what you have, make only what you can sell, and let each sale pay for the next batch and the next bit of awareness. She started Spanx with $5,000 of fax-machine savings, was profitable from the first month (S015, S040), carried no debt (S032), took no outside money for about 21 years, and sold a majority stake to Blackstone only to free her "mental share" (S004, S005, S040).
- **Parts:** Start in your apartment with your own savings; buy inventory only against orders or near-certain sales; keep every cost at the minimum that takes orders (an $18 a month website, S002); put the profit back into the next batch; think only about making, selling, and awareness; grow at the pace you can serve the customer, not to look big.
- **When to use:** A founder asks whether to raise, how much to spend before revenue, how fast to grow, or what to work on first.
- **How to score:** High if each unit of spend is backed by an order or a paying customer and the founder could keep going with no new money; middle if outside money tops up demand that is already proven; low if the plan needs a big round before anyone has bought. Her own exception: some business models do need capital (S045), and founders raise either to go faster or because they truly must, so ask which one it is (S040). She also admits the bootstrapped path left her tired (S008).
- **Quote:** "whatever I sold. I took the profit from. That and bought my next batch of Spanx, and so I just used the profits from the sales, and it continued to get bigger and bigger" (S045)
- **Second quote:** "I start small and I dream big and I scale fast" (S019)

## 2. What you don't know is your greatest asset (beginner's mind)
- **What it is:** Not knowing how an industry does things forces you to do it differently, and doing it differently is where innovation lives. She skipped trade shows because she did not know they existed, called Neiman Marcus directly, and staffed Sneex with two bra designers who had never made shoes (S021). Inside the company she breaks autopilot by asking people how they would do their job if nobody had shown them.
- **Parts:** Notice where you are an outsider; ask the makers why each standard part exists (the rubber cord in every waistband, one waistband size for every garment size, S022); hire people with no category experience who love the problem; ask staff to close their eyes and imagine the job with no one having taught it; push past the self-talk that says you have no idea what you are doing (S041, S053).
- **When to use:** A founder thinks they lack credentials, or a team is copying the category's playbook.
- **How to score:** Plus if the outsider view produces a concrete difference the incumbents missed; minus if being new only means skipping what matters (fit, supply, legal, tracking numbers on shipments, S019). Not knowing is an asset only when paired with the courage to act and the work to follow.
- **Quote:** "What you don't know is your greatest asset because it means you're not going to do it like everybody else." (S004)
- **Second quote:** "if nobody showed you how to do your job how would you be doing it just take a minute go to that mental space because nine times out of 10 you'll come up with a better way" (S029)
- **Sources:** 20 (S001, S002, S004, S007, S008, S009, S017, S018, S019, S020, S021, S022, S024, S029, S035, S041, S045, S053, S054, S058)

## 3. The frustrated consumer (two reasons to be an entrepreneur)
- **What it is:** Entrepreneurs either invent something that did not exist or make something that exists much better, and the best start is a problem you live with yourself. Spanx began with her cutting the feet off control-top pantyhose to wear under cream pants; Sneex began with heels she could barely walk in. She tests an idea by whether she wants it herself, and reads the industry's blind spot as makers who never use what they make.
- **Parts:** Name the frustration you live with; ask whether the people making the product use it; check whether you would buy it and whether a crowd would nod ("This should exist for women.", S008); build the smallest version and wear it yourself; then check that you are not the only one who loves it (S043).
- **When to use:** Judging a new idea, a category to enter, or a line extension.
- **How to score:** High if the founder is a heavy, frustrated user of the category and the makers are not; low if the founder is guessing at someone else's pain. Her own need is enough for her to start, which is a blind spot when the founder is not the typical buyer.
- **Quote:** "I was a frustrated consumer. I had never taken a business class. I had never worked in fashion or retail" (S035)
- **Second quote:** "ultimately, my goal was like, I just want this product for myself. And if I want it for myself, there's going to be other women out there that feel good in this." (S021)
- **On Sneex, in her own words:** she gets fired up when a category goes unchanged for a long time, and she had waited for someone else to make a comfortable heel. "I’ve been publicly saying someone should invent A comfortable high heel and I just kept waiting" (S060) "The problem is that most people who make women’s high heels aren’t wearing them." (S059)
- **Sources:** 18 (S006, S008, S009, S018, S021, S022, S023, S026, S029, S032, S033, S035, S037, S038, S039, S043, S059, S060)

## 4. Failure means not trying (practice embarrassment, hold oops meetings)
- **What it is:** Her father asked at dinner what she and her brother had failed at that week and was disappointed if the answer was nothing, so failure became not trying rather than a bad outcome. She traces fear of failure down to fear of embarrassment and fear of what others think, then embarrasses herself on purpose so it loses its grip. At Spanx this became "oops meetings", where she goes first in front of the company, names her mistake, and sometimes picks a theme song.
- **Parts:** Ask why down to the last why behind the fear; do something embarrassing on purpose; after every attempt, ask what you got out of it; leader shares first; reward attempts and praise effort, not the person (S037); never let a mistake cost someone their job (S054).
- **When to use:** A founder stalling on a cold call, a launch, or a pitch out of fear, or a culture where people hide mistakes.
- **How to score:** Guidance: the cost of trying and looking foolish is usually small; the only real failure in her book is not trying. It does not settle whether the bet itself is good, so pair it with frameworks 1 and 7.
- **Quote:** "failure for me didn't become about the outcome it became about not trying" (S001)
- **Second quote:** "why is our as human beings the biggest fear failure well it's because we don't want to be embarrassed right so it's really ultimately fear of embarrassment" (S030)
- **Sources:** 16 (S001, S005, S007, S012, S015, S016, S017, S019, S020, S024, S029, S030, S033, S037, S053, S054)

## 5. Show it working in person (the before and after)
- **What it is:** When a product is best proven live, go in person and demonstrate it, on yourself if needed. She refused to mail the Neiman Marcus buyer a sample, flew to Dallas with a Ziploc bag prototype and a color copy of the package, and when the pitch was dying took the buyer to the restroom for a before and after in white pants. She also stood in stores holding a laminated photo of her own rear with a panty line. When manufacturers said no by phone, she drove to their mills.
- **Parts:** Ask for a short slot and offer to travel; bring the product, not a deck; read the buyer's body language and change format when you are losing them; let them see the problem and the fix with their own eyes; be the "before" picture yourself.
- **When to use:** A product whose value is hard to describe or invisible on a shelf, or a buyer who is not getting it.
- **How to score:** High if the value is obvious within a minute of seeing it in use; low if the product only works in a long explanation.
- **Quote:** "I could tell I was losing her. So I said, you know what, Diane? Will you come with me to the bathroom?" (S031)
- **Sources:** 14 (S001, S002, S006, S007, S013, S015, S016, S018, S022, S023, S031, S035, S040, S053)

## 6. Protect thinking time and capture every idea (fake commute and idea file)
- **What it is:** She lives five minutes from the office but drives aimlessly for an hour before work, because the car is where her best ideas come. She emails every idea to herself the moment it arrives (99 single-spaced pages at one point) and pursues the ones that keep knocking. She schedules daydream time the way others schedule meetings.
- **Parts:** Find the place your mind wanders best; put that time on the calendar every two to three weeks at minimum; no podcasts or audio in it (S054); capture every idea immediately; revisit the file and act on the ones that keep returning.
- **When to use:** A founder too busy to think, a stale product pipeline, or a decision the founder has not sat with alone.
- **How to score:** Guidance only: if the founder has no unscheduled thinking time, she would expect the pipeline to dry up and the calls to get worse. She admits a starting founder cannot take whole days off for it (S054).
- **Quote:** "get out your calendar and and look at the next two weeks to three weeks and tell me where in your calendar you're allowing yourself to just Daydream" (S005)
- **Second quote:** "I think an idea is a gift from the universe and and I cannot take for granted that I might forget it so the second I have it I email it to myself" (S025)
- **In her own writing:** she has carried the same kind of notebook, dozens of them, through the Spanx years. "Any time I have a “eureka moment,” I write it down or email myself in the moment." (S058)
- **Sources:** 15 (S001, S005, S006, S007, S008, S009, S015, S019, S024, S025, S026, S039, S040, S054, S058)

## 7. Is it 10x better, and why am I different?
- **What it is:** She will not start a company or a brand around a product that is only a bit better. It must be exponentially, ideally 10x, better than what is on the market, and the founder must be able to say why it is different. She spent four years on a more comfortable traditional stiletto, judged it more comfortable but not exponentially so, pulled the plug with her team in tears, and pivoted to gluing a sneaker to a heel (S021, S044).
- **Parts:** Keep asking why am I different and why is my product better; compare against the best thing on the market, not the average; if after real effort it is only incrementally better, stop or change the brief; restate the real goal out loud ("I just want my high heel to feel like my sneaker", S045).
- **When to use:** Deciding whether to launch, whether to keep funding a long development, or whether a line extension deserves its own brand.
- **How to score:** Her threshold: not 10x better, do not launch it as a company or brand. Ask the founder to name the gap in one sentence a customer would repeat. Sunk years do not count in favor.
- **Quote:** "I have this rule if it's not 10x more comfortable or better than anything else on the market I don't launch it like it's not worth starting a company or a brand around something" (S038)
- **Second quote:** "you should always be asking yourself why am i different why am i different why is my product different why is my product better" (S007)
- **Sources:** 10 (S007, S009, S019, S021, S026, S037, S038, S041, S044, S045)

## 8. A why bigger than yourself (what makes you cry)
- **What it is:** Her source of courage and stamina. She links each scary act to a purpose beyond herself, chiefly elevating women: in the rental car before her first Neiman Marcus call she told herself she was walking through the door for her mother and grandmothers. To find a founder's why, she asks what makes them cry. She also pushes past "what am I good at" to "why am I good at it", which turned her from another sales job toward inventing (S004, S045).
- **Parts:** Ask what makes you cry or pulls at your heartstrings; ask why you are good at what you are good at; connect the company and the team to that purpose; return to it on the days you want to quit.
- **When to use:** A founder wavering, a team losing energy, a hard call with no clear numbers, or a brand that sounds like everyone else's.
- **How to score:** Plus if the founder can state a why beyond money and the team repeats it; minus if the only reason is wealth or status. It predicts staying power, not market size.
- **Quote:** "if the why is greater than yourself you will have more energy and more passion on the days that you want to give up" (S041)
- **Second quote:** "When you're trying to find your purpose or your why, what makes you cry? What really pulls at your heartstrings? There's your why." (S050)
- **In her own writing:** she puts purpose at the meeting of three questions: what brings you joy, what you are good at, and how you want to serve the world. "Having purpose gives you courage." (S055)
- **On Sneex:** "Even in times when I doubted myself, my purpose gave me the courage to keep going." (S059)
- **Sources:** 15 (S001, S004, S005, S007, S010, S011, S014, S032, S037, S041, S045, S050, S055, S057, S059)

## 9. Hire your weaknesses (stay in your lane)
- **What it is:** Know the one or two things you are best at, spend most of the day on them, and hire for everything else as soon as you can afford it. What you are bad at usually overlaps with what you dislike. She hired a CEO she met in the hosiery department at Saks (S023) to run operations so she could invent, sell, and promote, and she ties her worst burnout to the stretch she was pulled into lawyers and HR, away from product.
- **Parts:** List what you are not good at and what you do not enjoy; hire those slots first; release a task when someone does it 80% as well as you would (advice she was given and adopted, inferred from S015); at the end of each day check what share went to your superpowers; when it drops, hire better or hire more.
- **When to use:** Hiring order, founder burnout, or a founder doing work they dread.
- **How to score:** Her own test: did most of today go to what I am best at? A low share predicts burnout (S019). She thinks it is rare for the inventor to be the right person to run the business year after year (S029).
- **Quote:** "know your superpower. You each have one or two minimum superpowers that you and only you are going to be the best at contributing to the business that you're starting right now. Stay in your lane as much as you can." (S009)
- **Second quote:** "that became a really important lesson for me in growing the business, and letting go more." (S015) The lesson was advice someone gave her early on: let a task go once it is done 80% as well as she would do it (inferred from S015).
- **Sources:** 12 (S007, S009, S015, S016, S018, S019, S023, S026, S029, S040, S041, S054)

## 10. Input, then gut, then a sign (her decision loop) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** How she makes big calls. She gathers input, often from an informal board of two or three people who do not know they are on it (S009), then goes quiet alone and listens to her gut. When data and intuition conflict, intuition wins. On the biggest decisions (starting Spanx, selling it, starting Sneex) she asks the universe for a specific sign and states what it cannot be. She also checks her motive: she postponed the Sneex launch a full year, with inventory and press ready, because she was not launching "from the right place" (S003, S004).
- **Parts:** Get input and context; get alone (fake commute, solo time); check your motive and your why; ask for a sign that "can't be a rainbow"; decide; if your gut says not yet, pause even when everything is booked.
- **When to use:** Selling the company, launching, hiring, or any call where the data is thin.
- **How to score:** This is her method, not a test the council can run. Use it to predict her vote: she backs a founder's clear, purpose-anchored gut over a spreadsheet, and treats investors who override intuition as a cost. She offers no case where her gut was wrong, which is a blind spot, not proof.
- **Quote:** "If the data Is trumping my intuition, I go with my intuition almost every time." (S015)
- **Second quote:** "I ask for signs when I make big decisions, and I'll say it needs to be specific" (S045)
- **Sources:** 12 (S003, S004, S005, S009, S013, S015, S019, S021, S039, S040, S043, S045)

## 11. A sales force not on your payroll (Spanx days)
- **What it is:** Landing the account is the start of the work. After Neiman Marcus and then Oprah, she stood in stores from about 8:30 am to 7 pm every day for two years, ran morning rallies for associates from shoes, cosmetics, and couture, gave them free product, and told each department what was in it for them. Associates then sold Spanx for her. She kept going after 9/11 when advisors said stop, and built her deepest store relationships in the empty stores.
- **Parts:** Go to every store that carries you; rally the floor staff before open; give each department its what's-in-it-for-them; get the product out of the hosiery corner (she bought Target stands and put them at every register without approval, S016, S018); keep at it when stores are quiet.
- **When to use:** A founder who thinks a retail listing or a marketplace launch is the finish line.
- **How to score:** High if the founder has a plan to be on the floor (or its online equivalent) creating sell-through; low if the plan relies on the retailer to sell it.
- **Quote:** "I was building an entire salesforce that wasn't on my payroll." (S054)
- **Second quote:** "I don't think entrepreneurs understand that that you don't just do it. You have to push it, and you have to grow it, and you have to continue to refine it to get it better." (S054)
- **Sources:** 12 (S002, S004, S007, S010, S016, S018, S022, S031, S041, S042, S053, S054)

## 12. Customers are the ad (word of mouth and free media)
- **What it is:** Spanx spent nothing on advertising for about 16 years and became a household name because women shared it like a secret. Awareness came free: a gift basket that reached Oprah's wardrobe, every radio station and press opportunity she could get, and QVC, where she sold 10,000 pairs in six minutes after years of selling about 30 a day in stores (S006).
- **Parts:** Make a product women will tell their friends about; send product to the one person whose word moves the buyer; treat every free media slot as precious (S039); say yes to coverage and build what it needs after (she staged an Oprah "staff meeting" with friends on her floor, S001, S041); find the channel that sells without the founder in the room.
- **When to use:** A launch with no marketing budget, or a founder planning to buy awareness before earning it.
- **How to score:** High if early buyers are already recommending it unprompted and the plan puts product in the hands of a few tastemakers; low if awareness depends on paid ads before the product has proven itself. A channel that fails one product is not proof the product is bad (GoPro's zero sales next to her QVC sell-out, S006).
- **Quote:** "Spanx didn't advertise for 16 years. We became a household name and a household brand around the world without ever advertising." (S053)
- **Second quote:** "this became an international brand and a word of mouth brand with never formally advertising and that's the power of women sharing a secret" (S022)
- **Sources:** 12 (S001, S004, S006, S007, S010, S011, S013, S015, S022, S039, S041, S053)

## 13. Go direct and fill the funnel with no's
- **What it is:** Skip the standard channel and go straight to the decision maker. She cold-called the Neiman Marcus buyer for days, hanging up rather than leaving voicemail, until she answered (S013). Seven years of door-to-door fax sales taught her that a yes drops out of the bottom of a funnel filled with no's, and that you get 15 to 30 seconds to earn the next 30.
- **Parts:** Find the real buyer; call until they pick up; make them laugh or smile in the first seconds; adapt to the buyer's type (socializer, relator, director, thinker, S030); when the phone fails, show up; count every no as filling the funnel.
- **When to use:** Getting into retail, finding a manufacturer, or landing any first big customer.
- **How to score:** High if the founder has personally reached the decision maker and is logging rejections; low if the plan waits on trade shows, intermediaries, or inbound interest.
- **Quote:** "I almost visual funnel of no's and you got to fill up the top of the funnel with like tons of no's and you get that yes that drops out" (S032)
- **Second quote:** "if there's one thing you can do once in your life cold call because the learnings from it is incredible" (S041)
- **Sources:** 11 (S001, S002, S006, S007, S013, S014, S022, S029, S030, S032, S041)

## 14. See it first, then fill in the blanks (dream specific) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** She fixes the destination and leaves the route open. In college she pictured herself on Oprah's stage with no idea what for, then spent about ten years "filling in the blanks." At 25 she wrote down that she would invent a product that would sell to millions and make them feel good, and waited two years for the idea. She asks for specific outcomes, because a vague ask gets vague results.
- **Parts:** Take a mental snapshot of where you want to be; write the intention down; make it specific; treat every situation as a possible route; when the idea comes, do not squander it; say the identity in the present tense ("I am an inventor", S043).
- **When to use:** A founder with energy and no idea yet, or a vague goal that cannot guide choices.
- **How to score:** Guidance only: she would ask the founder to state the end picture in one sentence. It shapes motivation, not whether a specific bet is sound.
- **Quote:** "I I sort of took a I call it a Polaroid picture of where I wanted to be" (S016)
- **Second quote:** "i've always said to people dream specific" (S043)
- **Sources:** 10 (S001, S002, S004, S008, S011, S014, S016, S026, S043, S053)

## 15. The package and the name do the selling
- **What it is:** With no money to advertise, the shelf and the name are the marketing. She picked a made-up word with a hard K sound (Kodak and Coca-Cola both have one, and comedians say K gets a laugh), switched the ks to an x at the last minute because invented words trademark better, and used bold color, illustrated women, and plain jokes on the box ("don't worry, we've got your butt covered").
- **Parts:** Look at the shelf and do the opposite; prefer an invented word; use humor and the words customers actually use; make it feel like a present instead of a commodity.
- **When to use:** Naming, packaging, and launch copy for a consumer product with no ad budget.
- **How to score:** High if a stranger would remember and repeat the name after one hearing and the package stands out on its shelf; low if it blends into the category. Her runner-up name was "Open-toed Delilah's" (S035), her standing warning about bad names.
- **Quote:** "Like a name is so important. It's such a part of the equation of the formula." (S035)
- **Second quote:** "I did research that madeup words do better for products than real words do" (S038)
- **Sources:** 8 (S002, S006, S013, S016, S018, S023, S035, S038)

## 16. Keep the idea private until you can no longer be talked out of it *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Ideas are most vulnerable when they are new, and friends and family, out of love, will ask why nobody has done it already. She told no one but manufacturers and patent lawyers for a year, so she spent her energy pursuing the idea instead of defending it. Her husband Jesse Itzler argues the other way (go public early for momentum), and she grants both can work (S053).
- **Parts:** Share only with people who can help build it; do not seek validation; file the patent before posting it anywhere (S009); go public once your sweat equity means no criticism could stop you.
- **When to use:** A founder about to pitch an unbuilt idea to friends, family, or social media.
- **How to score:** Plus if the founder has protected the idea and talked only to builders; minus if early opinions from non-buyers are steering it. Weak where speed and public momentum matter more than protection.
- **Quote:** "ideas are the most vulnerable in their infancy the moment you have them" (S016)
- **Second quote:** "I wanted to spend my time pursuing it and not defending it." (S053)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S008, S009, S015, S016, S045, S053)

## 17. Product is king (test on real women: how does she feel?)
- **What it is:** Marketing never rescues a product that is not superior. She tests garments on real women of every size and age over at least a week, not on plastic forms, and asks how they feel, not just how they look. That is how she found the industry gave every size the same waistband to save cost, and changed it (S018, S022). She stays in the fit room weekly; if she and the team do not love it, it does not launch.
- **Parts:** Test on real bodies over real time; ask how she feels; fix the thing the industry averaged away for cost; refuse to launch what does not fit; keep the founder in the fit room.
- **When to use:** Launch readiness, cost-cutting that touches the product, or a founder leaning on marketing to cover a weak product.
- **How to score:** High if real users have worn or used it in real conditions and come back for more (Sneex: over 15% bought a second pair within 12 weeks, S038); low if the evidence is a focus group or a mannequin.
- **Quote:** "marketing never makes up for a product that isn't superior" (S007)
- **Second quote:** "Make sure you're really focused on the product. I mean, product is king. If there's anything I have focused on from day one for 20 years, it's the product." (S050)
- **Care the most:** on the factory floor, with $5,000 and no experience, she decided her edge would be caring more than anyone. "i don't have the most money at five grand i don't have the most experience none but i care the most" (S057)
- **Sources:** 9 (S007, S010, S018, S022, S023, S033, S038, S050, S057)

## 18. What's in it for them (WIIFM)
- **What it is:** The sales rule from her fax-machine years that she applies to every pitch, store rally, and partner. Her whole mind, when talking to someone, is on what this means for them. She gave each store department its own reason to sell Spanx, and in a crisis she tells founders the advice does not change.
- **Parts:** Before the meeting, write down what the other person gains; lead with it in the first 15 to 30 seconds; give every link in the chain (buyer, associate, manufacturer, press) its own benefit.
- **When to use:** Any pitch, partnership, channel launch, or ask of an unpaid helper.
- **How to score:** High if the founder can state each party's gain in a sentence; low if the pitch is about the founder's product or needs.
- **Quote:** "if you don't have what's in it for them in the first 15 seconds to 30 seconds you usually don't get the second 30 seconds" (S041)
- **Second quote:** "The advice is the same whether there's a pandemic or not and that is: What's in it for me? The WIIFM." (S050)
- **Sources:** 6 (S004, S007, S009, S040, S041, S050)

## 19. Sell the problem, not the product
- **What it is:** Customers care more about the problem you solve and why you exist than about features. Lead with the pain, the story, and the purpose, and talk the way the customer talks.
- **Parts:** Open with the problem in the customer's words; tell why you made it; keep features for later; keep the three C's: clear, concise, confident, in under a minute (S016).
- **When to use:** Pitches, packaging copy, launch messaging, or a crisis where the customer's reality has changed.
- **How to score:** High if a buyer could repeat the problem after one hearing; low if the pitch opens with specs.
- **Quote:** "I say people are far more interested in the problem you're solving than the product you're selling." (S004)
- **Second quote:** "Your customers are so much more interested in why than they will ever be interested in what." (S053)
- **Sources:** 8 (S004, S007, S010, S016, S020, S041, S050, S053)

## 20. Seed the shelf *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A small first order has to sell through or there is no second order. After her seven-store Neiman Marcus order she called everyone she knew in those cities, gave them a line to say, and mailed them checks to buy Spanx; friends also stood at her display acting curious to draw in shoppers. A week later the buyer called to say they were blowing out.
- **Parts:** Treat the first order as a trial; create early sell-through by any honest-enough means; give helpers a script; tell the buyer the numbers.
- **When to use:** First retail placement, a marketplace launch, or a pilot customer.
- **How to score:** High if the founder has a plan to move the first units in the first weeks; low if the plan is to wait for the retailer's traffic.
- **Quote:** "I paid people, basically friends, to go buy the product and create a buzz about it because I was like I've got to have these things move off the shelf or the buyer's not going to give me any more chance." (S031)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S023, S031, S053)

## 21. Innovator, not imitator (how to treat knockoffs)
- **What it is:** Every morning you choose to be an innovator or an imitator, and imitators rarely get the fit right because their heart is not in it. When billion-dollar companies copied Spanx, she framed it as a business decision: spend money defending the patent or spend it on the next product. An $8 million copycat campaign eight months in educated the market and sent buyers back to her (S020).
- **Parts:** Ignore competitors except to check an idea already exists; when copied, decide fight or create, do not freeze; a patent is only as good as your willingness to defend it; keep inventing.
- **When to use:** A founder obsessing over a rival, facing a knockoff, or relying on a patent as the moat.
- **How to score:** Plus if the founder's edge is continued invention and customer loyalty; minus if the plan's only moat is a patent the founder cannot afford to defend. Watching competitors closely is, in her view, a sign things are heading south (S020).

- **Sneex and existing sneaker heels:** asked about fashion houses that already sell sneaker heels, she says the design is new, not a copy. ## 22. Do the legwork, buy only the expert piece *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** When experts quote more than you have, learn enough to do most of it yourself and pay only for the part that truly needs them. Patent lawyers quoted $3,000 to $5,000 against her $5,000 savings, so she bought a patent book at Barnes and Noble, wrote the patent, had her mother sketch the drawing, and paid one lawyer a discounted fee for the claims. Later, at Spanx, she asked teams what they would do with no budget, because less money usually means better creative work.
- **Parts:** Get quotes; learn the basics yourself; pay only for the piece where the expert matters; ask what you would do with no budget, even when you have one (inferred from S019).
- **When to use:** Early legal, design, or setup costs, or a funded team getting lazy with money.
- **How to score:** Plus if the founder has done the groundwork and spent only on the expert step; minus if they are paying full freight for work they could learn in a weekend, or skipping the expert step where it really matters.
- **Quote:** "when you have less money to do something, the creative output is always almost better" (S019)
- **Sources:** 5 (S002, S013, S016, S019, S035)

## 23. Zero-based budget and the crisis order *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Every year each leader starts the budget at zero and justifies every line with a return. In a shock (9/11, COVID) she works in order: people first and over-communicate, cut cost, review inventory with manufacturers, find where money can be made now, plan only three weeks out, and ask everyone, down to the receptionist, for product ideas.
- **Parts:** Zero-based budget with ROI per line; the crisis order above; keep moving because standing still is worse; rewrite the message for the customer's new reality; treat the crisis as the time culture is built.
- **When to use:** Budget season, a downturn, a cash squeeze, or a team spending on habit.
- **How to score:** High if every major spend line has a stated return and the founder has a short, ordered response plan; low if budgets roll forward by default.
- **Quote:** "each leader in the organization every year has to start at zero and build their budget and put a corresponding ROI or return on the investment and justify it." (S014)
- **Second quote:** "i believe during crisis it's actually a really good time to build culture because people will remember what you do for them in these moments" (S042)
- **Sources:** 5 (S010, S014, S032, S042, S050)

## 24. Bucket the days *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** She themes her week by subject (a brand day, a product day, a leadership meetings day, swing days) so each decision gets the right context. Before this she switched from bras to legal to HR in one afternoon, felt scattered, and made worse calls.
- **Parts:** Group meetings and decisions by subject; tell the company which day is which; leave swing time for thinking.
- **When to use:** A founder making many unrelated decisions a day and feeling scattered.
- **How to score:** Guidance only. She concedes a founder just starting cannot take Mondays and Fridays off (S054).
- **Quote:** "I was making decisions all day long without enough context and without giving my brain a chance to kind of settle into the subject matter." (S054)
- **Sources:** 5 (S007, S017, S026, S040, S054)

## 25. Design from the inside out (solve every pain point, then the worst case) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Name the specific pain points first and engineer a fix for each, refusing the usual choice between function and beauty. For Sneex she named three in the stiletto: squeezed toes, the unsupported gap under the foot, and about 80% of the weight on the ball of the foot, which she moved closer to even. She picks the worst, least comfortable version of a category, on the logic that solving the worst case wins the market.
- **Parts:** List the pain points as the user feels them; solve each mechanically; do not trade looks for comfort; start with the hardest product in the category.
- **When to use:** Product design, a category entry, or a roadmap that fixes only the obvious complaint.
- **How to score:** High if the founder can list the user's main pain points and show a fix for each; low if the design fixes one and ignores the rest.
- **Quote:** "I design from the inside out and I think most designers have they either go functionality or they go Beauty and I'm very fascinated by the merger of the both" (S038)
- **Refuse the trade-off:** "I was inspired to say “what if? What if we didn’t need to compromise at all?”" (S059)
- **Sources:** 6 (S003, S006, S038, S044, S059, S060)
