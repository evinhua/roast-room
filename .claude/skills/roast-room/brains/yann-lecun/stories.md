# Stories: Yann LeCun

20 stories and cases he reuses, with the lesson he draws. Built from the twenty extraction batches over S001 to S099. Source ids in parentheses; most-used first.

1. **The 17-year-old driver.** A teenager learns to drive in about 20 hours, yet millions of hours of driving data have not produced a level 5 self-driving car. He tells it in almost every talk since 2019. Lesson: learning fast from little data is the real test, and what is missing is a world model, not more data (S001, S003, S007, S008, S022, S032, S040, S046, S056, S066, S075, S085, S087, S099).
2. **Babies and impossible events.** Around nine or ten months, babies stare in surprise when an unsupported object does not fall; six-month-olds do not. V-JEPA, trained only on video, shows the same spike in prediction error when a ball vanishes mid-flight. Lesson: world models can be learned from watching, and surprise at impossible events is the test (S002, S008, S015, S025, S029, S032, S051, S052, S058, S081, S082, S085, S089, S092, S099).
3. **The four-year-old's data.** A child awake for 16,000 hours takes in about 10^14 bytes through vision, the same order as all the public text used to train the largest LLMs. Lesson: more text is not the bottleneck; the kind of data and the architecture are (S006, S011, S014, S015, S029, S031, S045, S061, S081, S082, S084). His figures vary between talks (10^14 bytes in most, 10^15 and "50 times more" in S031 and S061).
4. **Linux beat Sun and HP-UX.** In the 1990s proprietary Unix, Microsoft, and Sun ran internet servers; by the 2000s Linux, Apache, and MySQL ran nearly everything. He casts OpenAI and Anthropic as today's Sun and HP-UX. Lesson: infrastructure goes open, and the open side wins in the end (S001, S019, S032, S041, S057, S062, S064, S066, S087).
5. **Fifteen years of blurry video.** He spent about ten of fifteen years trying to predict video pixel by pixel and got only blurry frames, because the model averages all possible futures. He calls it "the same mistake that everybody is doing at the moment" (S045). Lesson: a decade of failure is data; predict in an abstract space instead, which led to JEPA (S009, S019, S031, S032, S034, S045, S052, S082, S084).
6. **Galactica, then ChatGPT.** Meta's open science model drew a wave of attacks on Twitter in late 2022 and its small team pulled the demo. Weeks later ChatGPT launched to wide praise. "we like we looked at each other and said like what what just happened?" (S013). Lesson: public reaction to AI is fickle and a big company with a reputation gets punished for an experimental demo that a startup would not (S010, S013, S023, S066).
7. **The Bell Labs check reader, won and lost.** Within months of joining Bell Labs in 1988 his ConvNet beat everything on the digit data, and by the mid-1990s it read 10 to 20 percent of US checks. The day the team gathered to celebrate, AT&T announced its breakup; research, engineering, and product went to different companies, and the patents went to NCR, which did not understand them. He kept working while hoping NCR's lawyers would not notice and "popped the champagne" (S005) when the patents expired in 2007. Lesson: a commercial win does not protect a project from the org chart, and locking up ideas slows everyone, including the inventor (S005, S016, S026, S027, S072, S092, S093, S094).
8. **Llama came from a pirate team of 12 in Paris.** A small FAIR Paris team built Llama 1 for its own research while the big official LLM effort was stopped. "the first Llama was actually a bit of a pirate project." (S051). He admits he had little to do with it technically (S051). Lesson: small, insulated teams with a long leash produce the breakthroughs (S009, S013, S014, S025, S051).
9. **From Llama 1's waiting list to Llama 2's open release.** Llama 1 went out to researchers only, and requests flooded in from startups, big companies, and NGOs: "to us it felt like um basically another internet." (S062). For months a group of about 40, including Zuckerberg, met weekly while legal and policy objected. He argued the risks were overblown and the upside was a whole industry, and Llama 2 went out with a commercial license after red teaming and a staged release. Lesson: watch the demand; an open platform can start an ecosystem a closed one never would (S001, S012, S039, S062, S064).
10. **Shut out by CVPR, then mainstream.** Around 2011 his lab's ConvNet beat every system for semantic segmentation, about 50 times faster, and all three reviewers rejected it. He wrote to the program chair and took it to ICML. By 2014 CVPR would not accept papers without neural nets. Earlier, shut out of the main conferences, he, Hinton, and Bengio ran their own "pirate" workshop and CIFAR summer schools. Lesson: a community that does not know the paradigm will dismiss strong evidence, so build your own forum and keep going (S005, S016, S077, S090, S094).
11. **Three conditions to join Facebook.** He told Zuckerberg he would stay in New York, keep his NYU job, and publish and open source everything. All three were accepted at once; Schroepfer said openness was already in the company's DNA. Lesson: set the culture you need at the point of hire (S005, S017, S018, S047, S067).
12. **The GenAI group under product pressure.** Meta pulled 60 to 70 researchers out of FAIR into a product-focused GenAI group in 2023. Cut off from long-term research, it did conservative work and lost key people, some of whom founded Mistral. He later called letting the robotics group go "a strategic mistake." (S040). Lesson: research needs a protected bridge into product, or the company misses the next wave (S001, S040).
13. **FAIR Paris versus DeepMind Paris.** FAIR Paris published openly and hosted resident PhD students, about a dozen graduating a year; it produced Llama 1 and alumni who started Mistral, and helped make Paris a top AI hub. DeepMind Paris kept secrets and could not host students, and seeded far less. Lesson: openness recruits and compounds; secrecy isolates (S009, S055, S080).
14. **Demos that did not become products.** IBM Watson was going to sit in every hospital; "That turned out to be really difficult." (S055), and IBM sold it for parts. Today's humanoid kung fu videos are planned in advance with handwritten models: "This is all precomputed. Okay?" (S058). Lesson: the gap from impressive demo to working product is where the money goes (S055, S058, S040).
15. **The paper, the wine, and the car wash.** He asked ChatGPT what happens when you hold a sheet of paper flat with two hands and let go with one; it answered fluently and got the physics exactly wrong. Video generators spill wine that floats in the air. Asked whether to walk to a car wash 100 meters away, most LLMs said walk, missing that the car has to go too. Lesson: text-trained systems give confident, wrong answers about the physical world, and a right answer may only mean the question was in the training data (S015, S023, S030).
16. **Hate speech removal went from about a quarter to 95 percent.** Self-supervised transformers raised Facebook's automatic hate speech removal from roughly 23 percent in 2017 to 95 percent by 2022. Lesson: AI is often the fix for a social harm, not the cause (S018, S033, S038, S063).
17. **Hinton's three interns.** Hinton sent three students to Microsoft, Google, and IBM, each told to replace one part of the speech recognizer, the acoustic model, and leave the rest alone. All three improved it, and within about 18 months every smartphone used deep learning for speech. Lesson: a narrow, testable wedge inside a customer's existing system can turn an industry faster than papers (S059, S077).
18. **The NYU MRI project.** An informal talk with NYU Radiology led to a FAIR collaboration to speed up MRI scans, with no healthcare plan on Meta's side. It was published and open sourced and ended up in nearly every MRI machine sold. Lesson: open research plus a real-world partner can turn a side project into global infrastructure (S006, S088).
19. **The Vapnik bets and "just run this."** At Bell Labs, Larry Jackel and Vladimir Vapnik bet on whether anyone would explain why big neural nets work and whether anyone would still use them; theory lost and the nets kept working. Later, people did not believe his team that stochastic gradient descent was far faster than SVMs until they posted a short C program saying just run it, and even then needed a theory paper. Lesson: results beat theory, but you have to make them easy to check (S034, S076, S090, S097).
20. **The postdoc who proved him wrong.** He had dismissed maximizing information content for decades on theoretical grounds. His postdoc Stephane Deny tried it anyway; it worked, and led to Barlow Twins, VICReg, and the JEPA family. Lesson: run the experiment you think will fail, and drop your own idea when a better one shows up (S045, S068).
