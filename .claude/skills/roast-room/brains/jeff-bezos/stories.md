# Stories: Jeff Bezos

The stories he reuses, with the lesson he draws, built from all twelve notes files over the 92-source corpus. One or two lines each, most-retold first, with every source that tells it.

1. **Leaving D. E. Shaw (1994).** His boss walked him through Central Park, said the online bookstore was a better idea for someone without a good job, and asked him to think for 48 hours. He projected himself to 80 and asked what he would regret. Lesson: on a personal leap, minimize regret, not short-term loss; "it would be a hundred percent chance of regret if I didn't try and basically a zero percent chance of regret if I tried and failed" (S071). (S004, S005, S020, S043, S071, S074, S082, S086, S108)

2. **Raising the first million.** About 60 meetings, 22 investors at roughly $50,000 each, the first question always "what's the internet?", and he told everyone, his parents included, there was a 70 percent chance they would lose it. His parents bet on him, not the internet. Lesson: expect many noes and state the honest odds. (S001, S014, S030, S041, S043, S089, S108)

3. **"Amazon.toast" (1997).** Barnes & Noble went online with about 30,000 employees and $3 billion in sales against Amazon's 125 people and $60 million. He told staff to be afraid of customers, not the competitor, "because they're never going to send us any money." (S047) He also matched their prices regardless of margin (S004). Lesson: under threat, double down on the customer. (S004, S011, S037, S041, S047, S048, S082)

4. **Auctions, then zShops, then Marketplace.** Two failed versions, each a year or more of real work, came before third-party listings on Amazon's own detail pages worked. Lesson: stubborn on the vision, flexible on the form. (S014, S062, S071, S074, S083, S086)

5. **The brewery's generator, and AWS.** A 300-year-old Luxembourg brewery once made its own power; "the fact that they generated their own electric power did not make their beer taste better" (S012). AWS began as Amazon's own internal pain at scale, then "why not sell it?" (S070). Experts called it a distraction, and incumbents ignored it for about seven years, which he calls huge luck. Lesson: rent undifferentiated heavy lifting, productize what you built for yourself, and use the quiet a weird idea buys. (S001, S010, S012, S028, S041, S044, S070, S083, S098)

6. **Packing tables, not knee pads.** Packing orders on a concrete floor in the first weeks, he proposed knee pads; the person next to him said packing tables, and productivity doubled the next day. Lesson: fix the cause, and the people closest to the work often see it first. (S043, S082, S089, S108)

7. **$113 to $6.** After the bubble the stock fell from $113 to about $6, while customers went from 14 million to 20 million and every internal metric improved. "so if the stock is the company somebody forgot to tell the customers" (S027). Lesson: the stock is not the company; watch fundamentals and keep runway. (S003, S008, S018, S023, S027)

8. **The Washington Post (2013).** Two gates: is it important, and is it hopeless? He paid about $250 million with no negotiation and no due diligence because Don Graham had told him every wart. The fix was one strategic shift, from a lot of money per reader on few readers to a little money on many. Years later he cut by data but spared investigative work. Lesson: take on a turnaround only if it matters, and know your real edge (the internet, not newspapers). (S003, S010, S018, S030, S033, S044, S098)

9. **Prime from a junior engineer.** An engineer proposed an all-you-can-eat shipping buffet; finance's model was horrifying and the heaviest users came first, but the trend lines were good. He later told leaders Prime would reach 100 million members and they thought he was nuts (S006). Lesson: some bets are judgment calls the math cannot settle. (S006, S011, S044, S088)

10. **Third-party sellers on Amazon's own pages (2000).** Letting sellers compete next to Amazon's own offers alarmed many smart Amazonians and took hundreds of escalated decisions. Third-party units grew from 6 percent of units in 2000 to 28 percent in 2005 (S053), and to 58 percent of physical gross sales by 2018 (S066). Lesson: hold the direction when the customer logic is clear and the downside cannot be proven. (S014, S053, S064, S066)

11. **Fire phone to Echo.** Four years of engineering went into a phone no one bought; its people and lessons sped up Echo and Alexa. "No customer was asking for Echo. This was definitely us wandering." (S066) Lesson: salvage failures into the next bet, and do not expect market research to find the biggest products. (S018, S025, S066, S088)

12. **"How much do we have?"** Mid-way through Kindle, his CFO asked how much he would spend on it; that was his answer. Amazon had never built hardware, so it built the skill instead of shrinking the vision. Lesson: once committed to a conviction bet, fund it properly and learn what you lack. (S018, S028, S055, S093)

13. **Pets.com and living.com.** Land-rush stakes in single-category startups failed when financing dried up. Amazon knew fixed costs were high and still bet on placeholders. Lesson: notice when a mental model has expired; the land rush was right until it was not. (S008, S018, S048)

14. **Profitable too soon.** Amazon was profitable in December 1995, six months after opening, which he calls a mistake for a business growing that fast; the plan said $70 million of sales in 2000 with a small profit, and the result was $2.7 billion and losses by choice. Lesson: when customers love it and growth is explosive, reinvest. (S009, S027)

15. **Calling customer service in the meeting.** Metrics said waits were under 60 seconds, customers said otherwise. He dialed the number in the weekly business review and the room waited over ten minutes. Lesson: "when the data and the anecdotes disagree, the anecdotes are usually right." (S045) (S003, S045)

16. **The table that always comes back, and the broom.** In customer service training an agent knew before the call that a customer would return a table, because that table always came back, which led to the andon cord. A kaizen expert watching him sweep asked why he did not "eliminate the source of dirt" (S029). Lesson: fix defects at the root, once. (S022, S029, S033)

17. **Disagree and commit on an Amazon Studios show.** He doubted an original, said so, and backed the team anyway, promising never to say I told you so. Lesson: when the team has more ground truth, gamble with them fast instead of fighting to consensus. (S036, S045, S064, S073)

18. **Customer reviews and the Enya CD.** A publisher asked him to post only positive reviews; he refused because "we don't make money when you sell things we make money when we help people make purchase decisions." (S091) He also kept a warning that you already own an item, though it cut sales. Lesson: trade a sale for a customer. (S003, S021, S051, S091)

19. **Kind versus clever.** At ten he told his grandmother how many years smoking had cost her; she cried, and his grandfather said "Jeff one day you'll understand that it's harder to be kind than clever" (S020). Lesson: gifts are easy, choices define you. (S020, S023, S047)

20. **Too many ideas.** Jeff Wilke told him he had enough ideas to destroy Amazon. He began holding a private list and releasing ideas at the rate the company could absorb, and built more leadership bandwidth to take more. Lesson: meter founder ideas. (S023)

Also retold, with thinner coverage: the grandfather's ranch and the broken bulldozer (any problem is solvable; S003, S007, S032, S071, S086); the tenants who nailed a Christmas tree to the floor (owners think long term; S018, S051); the $15 minimum wage reframed from whether Amazon was competitive to whether it wanted to lead (S066, S091); publishing the National Enquirer's extortion letters (S069); the Kindle "1984" deletion, where the critics were right (S047); ordering nine copies of an out-of-print lichen book to meet a wholesaler minimum (S004, S082).
