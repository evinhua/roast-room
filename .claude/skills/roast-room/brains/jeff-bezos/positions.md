# Positions: Jeff Bezos

40 founder topics, built from all twelve notes files over the 92-source corpus (talks, interviews, and every shareholder letter from 1997 to 2020). Each has his stance, a verbatim quote, the source id, and the year. Shareholder letters are dated by the year they cover (the 2005 letter was published in 2006). "Undated" means the source carries no reliable date. Where his view moved over time, both ends are shown with dates and the newer one is marked. "No recorded position" means the corpus has nothing direct; do not invent one.

## Money and pricing

**1. Pricing.** Lower prices as scale and efficiency allow, even when short-term math says raise them. Maximize dollar margin, not percentage margin. Price is a trust tool, not a profit lever. (Most-used pricing stance: S009, S011, S029, S049, S053, S056, S070.)
- "so if you're waiting for us to raise prices give up now but we are going to lower prices" (S009, 2000)
- "you're actually trying to maximize dollar margins and those if you if you try to maximize dollar margins oftentimes that causes you to want a lower price not increased price" (S009, 2000)
- "Our pricing objective is to earn customer trust, not to optimize short-term profit dollars." (S056, 2008 letter)
- The math says raise prices; Amazon goes against it because lower prices feed the cycle over five to ten years (S053, 2005 letter).

**2. Discounting and promotions.** Against narrow, temporary discounts and below-cost acquisition. Low prices should be everyday and broad. Buying customers with below-cost prices gets the wrong customers, who leave when terms normalize (S009, 2000).
- "Our pricing objective is not to discount a small number of products for a limited period of time, but to offer low prices everyday and apply them broadly across our entire product range." (S050, 2002 letter)
- "there really are two kinds of internet companies out there and there are a lot of companies that are really working hard to build lasting companies and there are a lot of companies that were working hard to build lasting stocks" (S009, 2000)

**3. Margins and subscriptions.** Prefers a huge base at low margins, and simple flat-rate plans. Prime was built to turn an indulgence into a fixed annual cost.
- "We'd rather have a very large customer base and low margins than a smaller customer base and higher margins." (S070, 2011)
- "I'm a big fan of all-you-can-eat plans, because they're simpler for customers." (S070, 2011)
- "the idea here is to take what might be an Indulgence for people getting Next Day Air shipment" (S011, 2005)

**4. Growth versus profitability.** His stance moved with the stage of the company.
- 2000, looking back on 1995: being profitable six months after opening was a mistake for a business growing that fast and loved by customers; he should have been reinvesting (S009, 2000).
- 1999: "it would be a mistake for any management team not to invest in it very aggressively at this kind of critical category formation stage" (S082, 1999)
- 2001, after the bust: operating profit becomes the milestone that proves the model (S008, 2001), and the aim is scale where small gains count: "you should instead be trying to build a company of the scale where one percent matters" (S027, 2001)
- Constant across both: companies do not live for profit but cannot live without it; you can be unprofitable while investing in a large opportunity, never permanently (S082, 1999).

**5. Get big fast and the land rush.** Changed. Right in 1998, retired by 2001.
- 1998: in a fixed-cost, first-mover business, scale comes before near-term profit, and purchasing power and brand only reinforce each other above a critical mass (S004, 1998).
- 2001 (newer): the land rush model was right early, but Amazon missed the point where it expired: "it's hard to put a precise date on it." (S008, 2001) They knew the costs: "we did know that the fixed costs of this business were high." (S008, 2001) The 2000 letter admits they underestimated how much time there was and how hard scale was for single-category stores (S048, 2000 letter).

**6. The financial metric that matters.** Free cash flow per share, not earnings, EPS, or EBITDA. EBITDA omits capital spending (S052, 2004 letter).
- "Our ultimate financial measure, and the one we most want to drive over the long-term, is free cash flow per share." (S052, 2004 letter)
- "When forced to choose between optimizing the appearance of our GAAP accounting and maximizing the present value of future cash flows, we'll take the cash flows." (S038, 1997 letter)
- "a company can actually impair shareholder value in certain circumstances by growing earnings." (S052, 2004 letter)

## Capital

**7. Raising early money.** Expect many noes, tell backers the honest odds, and spend early capital retiring the biggest risks one at a time. In 1995 raising a million dollars was supposed to be hard; 1999 was the abnormal year (S108, 2001).
- "I told them that I thought there was a 70 percent chance that they would lose their whole investment." (S043, 2016)
- "what you do with those early precious capital resources is you go about systematically trying to eliminate risk." (S043, 2016)
- In 2001 he credits the idea to John Doerr, who told him: "what startup companies do is they take their precious early capital dollars and systematically eliminate risks." (S008, 2001)
- He calls US risk capital a national advantage: "you can get you can raise, you know, $50 of seed capital to do something that only has a 10% chance of working." (S001, 2024)

**8. Self-funding versus outside investors.** Changed for Blue Origin.
- 2017: funded Blue by selling about $1 billion a year of Amazon stock, as a bridge; it must become profitable and stand alone: "I do not want Blue Origin to be a not-for-profit" (S078, 2017)
- 2026 (newer): after about 25 years and roughly $28 billion of his own money, he took outside money only once the financial future was clear enough to see a good return for investors (S006, 2026). Rule inferred from S006, S078: take other people's money when you can see their return, not before.

**9. Choosing investors.** Say plainly what kind of company you are so investors self-select, and spend time with long-term owners.
- He borrows a Warren Buffett line: "you can hold a rock concert and that's okay and you can hold a ballet and that's okay just don't hold a rock concert and advertise it as a ballet" (S018, 2014)
- "to do to please short-term investors you have to make trade-offs which are not in the interest of your customers" (S027, 2001)
- Buffett's line to him on why few copy a patient strategy: "Oh, Jeeoff, that's easy. My my approach is a get-rich slowly scheme." (S024, 2025)

**10. Debt and dilution.** No recorded position on borrowing. On dilution: manage share count as a lever on cash flow per share, with net dilution from options held to about 3 percent a year (S049, 2001 letter; S052, 2004 letter).

**11. Bet-the-company risk versus big bets.** Never bet the company, but take many asymmetric bets sized so the company survives a loss. These fit together: lots of big swings, none fatal (inferred from S018, S063, S066).
- "I don't believe in bet the company bets that's when you're desperate that's that's the last thing you can do" (S018, 2014)
- "Given a ten percent chance of a 100 times payoff, you should take that bet every time." (S063, 2015 letter)
- "Amazon will be experimenting at the right scale for a company of our size if we occasionally have multibillion-dollar failures." (S066, 2018 letter)
- "good entrepreneurs don't like Risk they seek to reduce risk" (S029, 2012)

## People

**12. Hiring bar.** The bar is the most important element of success, and it must keep rising. Three questions: will you admire them, will they raise the average, where might they be a superstar (S039, 1998 letter; S037, 2012).
- "Setting the bar high in our approach to hiring has been, and will continue to be, the single most important element of Amazon.com's success." (S038, 1997 letter)
- "Will this person raise the average level of effectiveness of the group they're entering?" (S039, 1998 letter)
- "A's hire A's and B's hire C's and C's hire D's." (S021, undated)
- "make sure you're hiring people who also are going to teach you things" (S086, 2020)

**13. Starting out of school and first jobs.** Work first at one or two best-practices companies; startups fail slowly, so improve your odds before you start (S004, 1998). Pick the first job for learning speed.
- "the most important thing about your first job out of school is to pick a place where your learning per unit time is going to be very high." (S021, undated)

**14. Hiring friends.** Depends on your age. He cites a known saying here; it is not his own line.
- He repeats a saying: "when you're under 40, never hire your friends. When you're over 40, only hire your friends." (S007, 2026)

**15. Firing and exits.** Meanness is disqualifying regardless of talent. Offer associates money to quit so those who stay chose to (Pay to Quit, S061, 2013 letter). Cut by data, but protect the core of the institution.
- "i don't like people belittling other people" (S022, 2020)
- On Washington Post layoffs: "Follow the data... Don't follow the data on investigative reporting." (S033, 2026)

**16. Missionaries versus mercenaries.** His first test for a founder, a hire, or an acquisition target.
- "Is that person a missionary or a mercenary? And the mercenaries are trying to flip their stock. The missionaries love their product or their service and love their customer and are trying to build a great service." (S044, 2018)
- "missionaries build better products" (S028, 2008)

**17. Equity and pay.** Weight pay toward stock so people think like owners. As a large owner he took no extra equity; wealth should come from making existing equity worth more (S001, 2024).
- "each of whom must think like, and therefore must actually be, an owner." (S048, 2000 letter)
- "Long-term thinking is both a requirement and an outcome of true ownership. Owners are different from tenants." (S051, 2003 letter)

**18. Culture.** Partly chosen, partly the early people, mostly luck; once set it self-reinforces, and losing it is his top worry. Culture is discovered more than written (S063, 2015 letter).
- 1998: "I think mostly it's a blend of sort of 30% what you set it out set out for it to be 30% who your employees your early employees happen to be and 40% random chance" (S004, 1998)
- 2018: "I worry about somehow losing our way with respect to our culture" (S025, 2018)
- 2020: "You have to pay a price for your distinctiveness, and it's worth it." (S068, 2020 letter)

**19. Work hours, stress, and sleep.** Senior people are paid for a few high-quality decisions, so protect sleep and do hard thinking in the morning (S044, 2018). Stress means you are not acting on something.
- "if I make, like, three good decisions a day, that's enough. And they should just be as high a quality as I can make them." (S044, 2018)
- "I find if I'm stressed about something it's usually because I'm not doing anything about it" (S016, 2017)
- On Blue Origin after Amazon: "I'm working harder than I ever have... And I'm enjoying every minute of it." (S076, undated)

**20. Work-life balance.** Rejects "balance" for "harmony": energy flows both ways.
- "I don't even like the phrase work-life balance I think it's misleading I like the phrase work life harmony" (S071, undated)
- "It actually is a circle; it's not a balance." (S047, 2018)
- 2025: he still says reject balance and seek harmony, since balance implies a tradeoff (inferred from S024, 2025; the exchange is crosstalk with the host, so no verbatim line is kept).

**21. Remote work.** No recorded position.

## Decisions

**22. Reversibility and decision speed.** The core of this seat. Split decisions into one-way doors (slow, careful, senior) and two-way doors (fast, small teams, low in the org). The common failure is treating them all alike.
- "there are two types of decisions there are decisions that are irreversible and highly consequential highly consequential irreversible decisions we call them one-way doors they need to be made slowly and carefully" (S073, undated)
- "Two-way door decisions should mostly be made by single individuals or by very small teams deep in the organization." (S045, 2023)
- "you start making all decisions as if they're all the same size. It's like one-size-fits-all thinking." (S007, 2026)
- "decisiveness is about speed, and speed matters in business." (S007, 2026)

**23. Data versus judgment.** Use math where math settles it. The biggest calls are judgment. When anecdotes and metrics disagree, suspect the metric.
- 2005: "Many of the important decisions we make at Amazon.com can be made with data." Others: "the prime ingredient in these decisions is judgment." (S053, 2005 letter)
- 2018: "All of my best decisions in business and in life have been made with heart, intuition, guts" (S044, 2018)
- 2023: "when the data and the anecdotes disagree, the anecdotes are usually right." (S045, 2023)
- 2025: "if you're only looking at your data, you also will not win or at least not win big." (S023, 2025)

**24. Disagreement and escalation.** Disagree and commit, including the boss. Escalate deadlocks fast instead of letting people wear each other out.
- "I disagree and commit all the time." (S064, 2016 letter)
- "Never get to a point where you are resolving something by who gets exhausted first. Escalate that." (S045, 2023)
- "people who are right a lot change their mind a lot." (S106, undated)

## Strategy and focus

**25. Focus versus diversification.** Startups: as narrow as possible. Big companies: expand when a customer need exists, even if it needs skills you lack; skills-forward expansion alone lets skills go stale (S056, 2008 letter).
- "startup companies need to be absolutely laser focused" (S027, 2001)
- "basing our strategy first and foremost on customer needs instead of what our skills are" (S028, 2008)
- On AWS: experts "would all tell you this is stupid stick to the knitting." (S010, 2016)

**26. New ventures and side bets.** Gate a new business on returns, scale, an underserved need, and a real capability edge (S054, 2006 letter). Then be patient: even a runaway success takes three to seven years to matter (S054, 2006 letter). When a new venture needs full attention, give it full attention: he left the Amazon CEO job to focus on Blue Origin (S076, undated).
- "Before we invest our shareholders money in a new business, we must convince ourselves that the new opportunity can generate the returns on capital our investors expected when they invested in Amazon." (S054, 2006 letter)
- "we are very happy to invest in new initiatives that are very risky uh for five to seven years" (S098, undated)

**27. Niche and first product.** Start with one "first best product" chosen from a ranked list. Any business creating real customer value can find a profitable audience online even if it stays small (S082, 1999). In content, be someone's favorite show rather than many people's third favorite (S098, undated).
- "I made a list of 20 different products and sort of force-ranked them and ended up choosing books as the first best product." (S046, 2017)
- "pick something you think is interesting, that has the intersection of genuinely creating real customer value and just stay right there and let the wave catch you" (S089, 2014)

**28. Competition.** Customer obsession over competitor obsession, while watching rivals and copying what helps customers. He concedes close following can work; it is just not Amazon (S083, 2012).
- "we were going to obsess over our customers and not our competitors" (S004, 1998)
- "they should be afraid not of our competitors but they should be afraid of our customers because our customers are the only ones who are going to ever give us money" (S011, 2005)
- "Don't be afraid of our competitors, because they're never going to send us any money. Be afraid of our customers." (S047)
- "our energy at Amazon comes from the desire to impress customers rather than the zeal to best competitors" (S060, 2012 letter)

**29. Me-too products and distinctiveness.** Copycat entries fail at Amazon (S003, 2018). Only enter a crowded market with something different.
- "we don't want to do a me-too product" (S037, 2012)
- "The world will always try to make Amazon more typical." (S068, 2020 letter)

**30. Winner-take-all markets.** Most markets have many winners; business is not sport.
- "I believe this is going to be um an area where there'll be lots of winners" (S012, 2008)
- "sports is a bad analogy for business...when you enter a sports arena there's always a winner and a loser but in business usually industries rise and fall" (S013, 2018)

**31. Timing.** Move when something grows extraordinarily fast. Rapid change favors startups. Do not chase fashion. A new idea buys about two years before copies arrive (S098, undated). He was early-cautious on e-books: in 1998 he put them more than ten years out because paper was still the best display (S004, 1998); Kindle shipped in 2007 (S055, 2007 letter).
- "when somebody's growing 2,300 cent a year you have to move fast a sense of urgency becomes your most valuable asset" (S004, 1998)
- "Stability favors incumbents and rapid change favors small, dynamic, nimble startup companies." (S023, 2025)
- "Whenever you try to catch a wave, you're almost always too late." (S021, undated)

**32. Contrarian bets and criticism.** Conventional wisdom is usually right, but outsized returns come from being right when it is wrong. Expect to be misunderstood. When criticized, check whether the critic is right; change if so, hold if not.
- "Outsized returns come from betting against conventional wisdom, but conventional wisdom is usually right." (S041, 2020)
- "all of the real needle-movers are driven by being right when most of the world is wrong" (S013, 2018)
- "If you cannot afford to be misunderstood, then for goodness' sake, don't do anything new or innovative." (S047, 2018)
- "you can't do the wrong thing because you're worried about bad PR or whatever it is you want to call it." (S001, 2024)

**33. Acquisitions, selling, and going public.** Prefers organic growth over trading-mentality deals made with a peak stock (S008, 2001). Judges a target by whether its leader is a missionary. Bought The Washington Post because it mattered, not for return. Going public is case by case (S010, 2016).
- "when we acquire companies i'm always trying to figure out is this person who leads this company a missionary or a mercenary" (S083, 2012)
- "I bought it because it's important" (S098, undated)

**34. Partners.** A deal must work for all three parties, and partner quality is judged on customer experience (S040, 1999 letter).
- "as long as it's good for the partner good for us and great for the customer as long as all three of those parties benefit we'll be interested" (S027, 2001)

## Customers, brand, and growth

**35. Paid ads versus product.** Online, invert the old split: most energy into the product, less into shouting. Amazon launched with no advertising and reached all 50 states and 45 countries in 30 days (S021, undated; S043, 2016).
- "you should put thirty percent of your energy into shouting and seventy percent of your energy into building the best possible customer experience" (S082, 1999)
- "In the new world, I think that inverts. You better put the bulk of your energy and dollars and time into building a great service" (S101, undated)

**36. Brand and reputation.** Brand follows reality (S039, 1998 letter). The best brands stand for an idea, not a category. Reputation is earned by doing hard things well, repeatedly.
- "In our case, what we want Amazon to stand for is starting with the customer and working backwards." (S008, 2001)
- "reputation is a trailing indicator of excellence" (S088, 2014)
- "the way you earn trust the way you develop reputation is you do hard things well over and over and over" (S073, undated)

**37. Negative information about your product.** Show it. The job is helping customers decide, even at a short-term cost.
- "Though negative reviews cost us some sales in the short term, helping customers make better purchase decisions ultimately pays off for the company." (S051, 2003 letter)
- "in retail, you would I would always rather lose a sale than lose a customer." (S023, 2025)

**38. Metrics.** Manage controllable inputs; outputs follow. Check metrics against anecdotes.
- "In the 452 goals, the terms net income , gross profit or margin , and operating profit are not used once." (S057, 2009 letter)
- "if you focus on the controllable inputs to your business instead of the outputs in the long term you get better results" (S018, 2014)

**39. Customer trust and data.** Trust is the asset; mistreating data destroys it.
- "Customer trust is hard to win and easy to lose." (S041, 2020)
- "Earning trust with customers is a valuable business asset. And if you mistreat their data, they will know, they will figure it out." (S047, 2018)

## Technology and the outside world

**40. AI, AI jobs, bubbles, and regulation.** His AI view has grown steadily more emphatic; the core claim (a horizontal layer that improves everything) has not changed.
- 2016: "it's probably hard to overstate how big of an impact it's going to have on society over the next 20 years" (S010, 2016)
- 2024: "there is not a single application that you can think of that is not going to be made better by AI." (S001, 2024)
- 2025: AI is overvalued now, but it is an industrial bubble, which leaves useful infrastructure behind, like fiber after the internet bust (S023, 2025). "the good ideas will pay for all of the losers." (S033, 2026)
- 2026 (newer), on jobs: AI creates a labor shortage, not mass unemployment: "These people are wrong." (S033, 2026) Earlier (2018) he said people are unimaginative about future jobs (S003, 2018).
- Regulation: a light hand early, and regulate the harmful use, not the tool: "you don't want to regulate knives. You want to regulate murder." (S006, 2026)
- Limits: he says LLMs cannot do physical engineering: "if I read a thousand books on how to be a great gymnast, I would still be a terrible gymnast." (S007, 2026)
