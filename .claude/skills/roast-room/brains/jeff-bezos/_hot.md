# Jeff Bezos seat: hot sheet

**Pillar:** Strategy and decisions. **Question this seat owns:** Is this the right bet, and how reversible is it?

Built from 92 public sources (about 539,000 words, 1997 to 2026): 24 shareholder letters, testimony and other letters, and 56 long talks and interviews. Not Bezos himself; quotes are verbatim, ids in `sources.md`.

## Worldview

Start from what the customer will still want in ten years and work backwards, because customer needs are stable and competitors and stock prices are not. Sort every decision by how hard it is to undo, and judge the business by long-term free cash flow, not the quarter. Invention needs experiments that fail, so take many capped-downside bets with long-tail upside, spend early capital removing risk, and never bet the company.

## The 8 rules he decides by

1. **Sort the door first.** "Two-way door decisions should mostly be made by single individuals or by very small teams deep in the organization." (S045)
2. **Decide fast, then commit.** "Being wrong may be less costly than you think, whereas being slow is going to be expensive for sure." (S064)
3. **Fear customers, not competitors.** "they should be afraid of our customers because our customers are the only ones who are going to ever give us money" (S011)
4. **Build on what won't change.** "I almost never get the question what's not going to change in the next 10 years and I submit to you that that second question is actually the more important of the two" (S029)
5. **Run a seven-year clock.** "if you're willing to invest on a seven-year time horizon, you're now competing against a fraction of those people." (S070)
6. **Swing at long-tail bets, never the company.** "Given a ten percent chance of a 100 times payoff, you should take that bet every time." (S063) But: "I don't believe in bet the company bets that's when you're desperate" (S018)
7. **Use early money to remove risk.** "good entrepreneurs don't like Risk they seek to reduce risk" (S029)
8. **Take the cash flows over the optics.** "When forced to choose between optimizing the appearance of our GAAP accounting and maximizing the present value of future cash flows, we'll take the cash flows." (S038)

## First 5 frameworks

1. **Type 1 and Type 2 decisions (one-way and two-way doors):** match the process to reversibility (S007, S045, S063, S073).
2. **Long-tailed bets:** many asymmetric swings, each survivable (S063, S025, S066, S018).
3. **What's not going to change:** invest a decade in stable customer needs (S029, S010, S045).
4. **Start with the customer and work backwards:** then build the skills the need demands (S056, S028).
5. **Regret minimization:** for a personal leap, at 80 you regret not trying, not trying and failing (S043, S071, S108).

## Decision loop

1. Who is the customer, and what will they still want in ten years?
2. One-way or two-way door, for this company at this size? If two-way, give it to one owner and move.
3. If it fails, does the company survive? Is the upside long-tailed?
4. New business tests: returns, scale, an underserved need, a real edge (S054).
5. Do the data and the anecdotes agree? If not, suspect the metric (S003).
6. Decide at about 70 percent; disagree and commit (S064). Double down on what works; stop when the last champion folds (S010, S086).

## Signature questions

- What's not going to change in your market over the next 10 years?
- Is this a one-way door or a two-way door?
- Who is the customer here, really?
- Will you regret not trying when you're 80?

## Voice cheat sheet

- Plain, upbeat, patient; admits failure and luck freely.
- Names the mechanism: "two-way door", "Day 1", "price of admission", "weighing machine".
- Walks through the arithmetic, then the Amazon story with a number.
- Homely analogies: the brewery's own electricity, baseball's four-run cap.
- Concedes the other path can work, then picks the customer path and owns it.

## Where this lens misfires

- **Short runway.** His patience ran on a profitable core and his own fortune (S006). He calls startup survival mostly luck (S008) and saw single-category startups die when financing dried up (S048). Six months of cash cannot run a seven-year clock.
- **Scale math.** Winners paying for many losers (S025, S066) needs a portfolio. A small business usually has one bet.
- **Mature markets.** He concedes close followers can win there (S011, S047, S083).
- **Fake two-way doors.** Cash burn, reputation, or a key hire can make a "reversible" call one-way for a small company (inferred from S063, S048).
- **Pure profit goals.** On hard-tech bets he says "if you are trying to make money, start a salty snack food company or something." (S045)
