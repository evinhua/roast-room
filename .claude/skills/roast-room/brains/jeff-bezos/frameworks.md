# Frameworks: Jeff Bezos

Built from all twelve notes batches over the deep corpus (92 usable sources: shareholder letters 1997 to 2020, interviews and talks 1997 to 2026). 25 frameworks, most-used first. "Sources" counts distinct sources across the notes, with a few ids added where the corpus uses the framework by name. Frameworks marked *(Invent and Wander)* also appear in his collected letters book. For this seat's question, the door test (12) is the first lens to apply, then long-tailed bets (6) and the new business tests (11). Scoring lines are guidance drawn from his own thresholds, not his words.

---

## 1. Customer obsession, not competitor obsession (listen, invent, personalize) *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Center every decision on the customer. Watch competitors only to copy what they do well for customers. His precise definition of customer-centric has three parts.
- **Parts:** Listen to customers, because companies that do not listen fail. Invent on their behalf, because companies that only listen also fail (Prime, AWS, and Echo were never requested). Personalize so each customer sits at the center of their own store. Be afraid of customers, not rivals: customers are the only ones who send money, and they are never satisfied, so they keep pulling you forward even when you lead.
- **When to use:** Product strategy, and any plan that starts from what a rival did.
- **How to score (guidance):** Does the plan start from a named customer need, or from a competitor move? Does it only listen (incremental) or also invent something customers would not know to ask for? A plan justified by "they launched X" scores low. A leader with no rival left to chase but a clear customer problem scores high.
- **Quote:** "it means listen invent and personalize so first you have to listen to customers companies that don't listen to customers fail second you have to invent for customers because companies that only listen to customers fail" (S009)
- **Sources:** 20 (S003, S004, S009, S011, S012, S014, S021, S024, S030, S037, S039, S041, S044, S047, S060, S064, S066, S082, S085, S098)

## 2. Price of admission and heavy-lifting infrastructure
- **What it is:** Startup booms happen on top of heavy infrastructure someone else already paid for. Where that layer is missing, building it is the highest-leverage move. Where it exists as a commodity, rent it: work that must be done at world-class level but does not make your product better is undifferentiated heavy lifting.
- **Parts:** Amazon rode the postal system, credit cards, UPS, and the long-distance phone network, so it could start with about $1M. Space lacks that layer, so Blue Origin builds reusable launch, and the test of success is whether two kids in a dorm room can start a space company. Find the real cost driver (discarded hardware, not propellant). The brewery that made its own electricity got no better beer from it, so rent compute like power.
- **When to use:** Judging whether a market is open to a small player, and every build-versus-buy call.
- **How to score (guidance):** What existing infrastructure does this ride? If none, is the entry price survivable for this founder? For any in-house build, ask whether it makes the beer taste better. If not, buy it.
- **Quote:** "two kids in a dorm room cannot build an important space company today the price of admission is too high" (S013) / "the fact that they generated their own electric power did not make their beer taste better" (S012)
- **Sources:** 16 (S012, S013, S015, S017, S019, S022, S025, S028, S030, S031, S035, S045, S076, S083, S090, S104)

## 3. Regret minimization framework
- **What it is:** For a big personal or career leap, project yourself to age 80 and pick the path that leaves the fewest regrets.
- **Parts:** Picture yourself at 80 in a quiet moment, looking back. Most regrets are acts of omission. Short-term costs, like walking away from a mid-year bonus, are invisible from 80. Trying and failing is rarely regretted; never trying is. He calls it a name "only a nerd would call a" framework (S108).
- **When to use:** Leaving a safe job, starting a company, any personal one-way door. He used it in 1994 after his boss at D. E. Shaw walked him through Central Park and gave him 48 hours.
- **How to score (guidance):** Would the founder be haunted at 80 by not trying? Yes scores high on this lens. It does not price business risk or family runway; pair it with the door test (12) and systematic risk elimination (23).
- **Quote:** "it would always haunt me if I didn't try and so that would be a regret it would be a hundred percent chance of regret if I didn't try and basically a zero percent chance of regret if I tried and failed" (S071)
- **Sources:** 13 (S004, S005, S020, S024, S041, S043, S047, S071, S074, S082, S086, S089, S108)

## 4. Day 1 versus Day 2 *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Day 1 is startup vitality at any size. Day 2 is the slide into decline. Every letter ends "It remains Day 1."
- **Parts:** Day 2 runs stasis, irrelevance, painful decline, death. The Day 1 defense kit: true customer obsession, a skeptical view of proxies (process, surveys, metrics), eager adoption of external trends, and high-velocity decision making. Process tenets carry a clause saying to follow them unless you know a better way (S045).
- **When to use:** Diagnosing a company that has grown slow, process-bound, or inward-looking.
- **How to score (guidance):** Check the four defenses. "We followed the process" as an excuse, a metric nobody can tie to customers anymore, fighting a big trend, or decisions waiting for near-complete information all score as Day 2.
- **Quote:** "Day 2 is stasis. Followed by irrelevance. Followed by excruciating, painful decline. Followed by death. And that is why it is always Day 1." (S064)
- **Sources:** 13 (S003, S004, S024, S038, S040, S041, S042, S045, S064, S071, S082, S101, S105)

## 5. Controllable inputs, output measured as free cash flow per share *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Manage the inputs a team can control; judge the result by long-term free cash flow per share, not earnings, EPS, or EBITDA.
- **Parts:** Name the output. Work backwards through its drivers (stock price, then free cash flow and return on capital, then cost structure, then defects and picking efficiency) until you reach something a team controls. Set goals on those inputs: of 452 goals for 2010, net income, gross profit, and operating profit appeared zero times. Test growth that needs capital with present value: earnings can grow while value is destroyed. Manage dilution.
- **When to use:** Goal setting, capital-heavy growth, and any choice between looking good on GAAP and generating cash.
- **How to score (guidance):** Count how many of the plan's goals are inputs. Goals stated as revenue or margin for a small team score low. If capex outruns the present value of the cash it returns, the growth hurts owners.
- **Quote:** "if you focus on the controllable inputs to your business instead of the outputs in the long term you get better results" (S018) / "a company can actually impair shareholder value in certain circumstances by growing earnings." (S052)
- **Sources:** 10 (S011, S018, S038, S048, S049, S050, S052, S057, S060, S062)

## 6. Long-tailed bets *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Business payoffs are uncapped, unlike baseball, so take many bets with low odds and huge upside, and let the size of failures grow with the company.
- **Parts:** In baseball the best swing scores four runs; in business it sometimes scores a thousand. A ten percent chance of a 100x payoff is worth taking every time, knowing you will be wrong nine times in ten. Winners pay for many losers. Double down when something works. Bold bets are not bet-the-company bets.
- **When to use:** Deciding how much risk to take and how to size a bet.
- **How to score (guidance):** Expected value with capped downside. Is the downside survivable if it fails? Is the upside truly long-tailed, or capped like a base hit? A bet that could sink the company fails this lens no matter the upside.
- **Quote:** "Given a ten percent chance of a 100 times payoff, you should take that bet every time." (S063) / "I don't believe in bet the company bets that's when you're desperate that's that's the last thing you can do" (S018)
- **Sources:** 9 (S014, S018, S025, S035, S036, S063, S066, S074, S086)

## 7. What's not going to change
- **What it is:** Build strategy on customer needs that will hold for ten years, not on predictions of change.
- **Parts:** Ask what will not change. Name the stable needs (retail: low prices, vast selection, fast delivery; AWS: reliability, low cost, fast feature pace). Put energy into them for years, because that investment keeps paying. Stay flexible on how.
- **When to use:** Long-term strategy in a fast-changing market, including AI.
- **How to score (guidance):** The absurdity test: would any customer ever wish you were pricier, slower, or narrower? If not, the need is stable and safe to invest in for a decade. Plans built on a technology trend or a rival's move score lower.
- **Quote:** "I almost never get the question what's not going to change in the next 10 years and I submit to you that that second question is actually the more important of the two" (S029)
- **Sources:** 10 (S010, S011, S014, S023, S024, S029, S036, S037, S056, S083)

## 8. Start with the customer and work backwards (versus skills forward) *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Begin from a customer need and build whatever skills it takes, instead of only asking what else your current skills can do.
- **Parts:** Find a need that is meaningful and durable. Commit to it for years. Hire or learn the missing skill (Kindle needed hardware engineers). "Skills forward" (AWS grew from internal infrastructure skills) is a useful complement, but used alone it leaves your skills outmoded.
- **When to use:** Choosing new products and businesses.
- **How to score (guidance):** Is the starting point a customer need or a capability the team wants to use? If the vision shrank to fit current skills, score it down.
- **Quote:** "Working backwards from customer needs often demands that we acquire new competencies and exercise new muscles, never mind how uncomfortable and awkward-feeling those first steps might be." (S056)
- **Sources:** 9 (S008, S018, S028, S029, S053, S056, S057, S083, S098)

## 9. Missionary versus mercenary
- **What it is:** A test for founders, teams, acquisition targets, and yourself.
- **Parts:** Missionaries love the product and the customer and build to last. Mercenaries want to flip the company or the stock. Missionaries build better products and usually make more money. Perks do not create missionaries; a mission does.
- **When to use:** Choosing what to start, whom to back, whom to hire, or whom to buy.
- **How to score (guidance):** Is the motive the product and customer, or the exit? A plan whose main payoff is a quick sale scores low.
- **Quote:** "Is that person a missionary or a mercenary? And the mercenaries are trying to flip their stock. The missionaries love their product or their service and love their customer and are trying to build a great service." (S044)
- **Sources:** 8 (S028, S035, S037, S044, S055, S073, S083, S091)

## 10. The stock is not the company (voting machine, weighing machine)
- **What it is:** Separate market sentiment from business health and act only on the business.
- **Parts:** Benjamin Graham's line: short term the market votes, long term it weighs. In 1999, with the stock booming, Amazon had 14 million customers; in 2000, with it crashing from $113 to about $6, 20 million. Every internal metric improved through the crash. Do not feel smarter when the stock is up.
- **When to use:** Down markets, hot markets, and any decision being driven by valuation or a fundraising mood.
- **How to score (guidance):** Is the decision reacting to price or to customer and unit metrics? Price-driven decisions score low.
- **Quote:** "the stock is not the company and the company is not the stock." (S044) / "so if the stock is the company somebody forgot to tell the customers" (S027)
- **Sources:** 8 (S003, S008, S018, S023, S027, S044, S048, S060)

## 11. New business tests and the gates for taking one on *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Screens he runs before putting money or attention into a new line.
- **Parts:** Can it earn the returns on capital owners expect? Can it grow big enough to matter to the whole company? Is the opportunity underserved? Do we bring strong customer-facing differentiation (how would it be different)? The dreamy version: customers love it, it can grow very large, it has strong returns on capital, and it can last decades. For an unplanned, unfamiliar business (The Washington Post) he adds two gates: is it important, and am I optimistic it can work?
- **When to use:** Entering a new market or product line, or buying a business outside the core.
- **How to score (guidance):** One point per test. He resisted physical stores for years because they failed the differentiation and returns tests. Fail the importance or optimism gate and stop.
- **Quote:** "Before we invest our shareholders money in a new business, we must convince ourselves that the new opportunity can generate the returns on capital our investors expected when they invested in Amazon." (S054)
- **Sources:** 8 (S018, S028, S044, S054, S062, S083, S088, S098)

## 12. Type 1 and Type 2 decisions (one-way and two-way doors) *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Sort a decision by how hard it is to reverse, then match the amount of process to that. His master filter.
- **Parts:** Type 1 (one-way door): consequential and irreversible or nearly so; decide slowly, escalate, and analyze it more ways. Type 2 (two-way door): reversible; a single high-judgment person or small team deep in the organization decides fast and backs out if wrong. The failure at scale is one-size-fits-all process, which makes every decision slow. The same leader plays both roles: "chief slowdown officer" on one-way doors, out of the way on two-way doors.
- **When to use:** Every decision, first.
- **How to score (guidance):** Rate reversibility and consequence. One-way and high consequence: slow down, add analysis, raise to the founder. Reversible: decide this week with one owner. Flag heavy process on a reversible call as a Day 2 symptom.
- **Quote:** "There are giant decisions which are consequential and irreversible, or almost irreversible, super hard to reverse. Those decisions should be made slowly with great care." (S007) / "Two-way door decisions should mostly be made by single individuals or by very small teams deep in the organization." (S045) / "Type 2 decisions can and should be made quickly by high judgment individuals or small groups." (S063)
- **Sources:** 8 (S003, S007, S024, S025, S045, S063, S064, S073)

## 13. Gradatim ferociter (step by step, ferociously)
- **What it is:** Blue Origin's motto. Never skip steps, but move fast within the sequence.
- **Parts:** Slow is smooth and smooth is fast. Skipping a step looks faster and is slower, because the foundation is missing. Moon before Mars. Practice at high frequency on low-stakes versions (suborbital tourism) before the high-stakes version. Do not race a deadline when lives are at stake.
- **When to use:** Hard technical builds, regulated or safety-critical work, and any plan that jumps a stage.
- **How to score (guidance):** List the steps. Is one being skipped to hit a date? Is there a low-stakes way to get repetitions first? A skipped foundation scores low even if the timeline looks better.
- **Quote:** "Skipping steps slows you down. It's seductive, but wrong. And the military has a great saying-- slow is smooth, and smooth is fast." (S032)
- **Sources:** 8 (S006, S017, S025, S032, S035, S077, S078, S090)

## 14. Root cause, the five whys, and the andon cord
- **What it is:** Fix the cause, not the symptom, so the fix works for every customer, and let anyone stop a defect at the source.
- **Parts:** Ask why five times to reach the root, then fix it there. Any customer service agent can pull a defective product off the site. Defects cost more the further downstream they travel. A customer contact is itself a defect. Fixing root causes cuts cost and improves the experience at once, so there is no tradeoff.
- **When to use:** Recurring complaints, operational misses, cost problems.
- **How to score (guidance):** Does the plan remove the source, or add a step that cleans up after it? Sweeping the floor scores low; removing the source of dirt scores high.
- **Quote:** "you don't just fix the symptom you have to fix the root cause" (S022) / "We really obsess over small defects. That's what drives up costs. Because the most expensive thing you can do is make a mistake." (S070)
- **Sources:** 7 (S022, S029, S033, S049, S070, S076, S098)

## 15. Rate of experimentation
- **What it is:** Useful invention is proportional to the number of experiments per unit of time, so make experiments cheap and self-service.
- **Parts:** Lower the cost of each experiment. Settle feature arguments with A/B tests in hours, not debates. Remove gatekeepers, since even well-meaning gatekeepers slow invention. Amazon ran 1,976 Weblabs in 2013, up from 546 in 2011. More experiments means more failures, and that is the correct tradeoff.
- **When to use:** Product teams that argue instead of test, and any process where trying something needs many approvals.
- **How to score (guidance):** How many real experiments a month can this team run, and what does one cost? Can it be tested cheaply before committing? If yes, test rather than decide.
- **Quote:** "the amount of useful invention that you have is directly proportional to the number of experiments that you can do per week per month per year" (S083)
- **Sources:** 7 (S011, S021, S029, S059, S061, S074, S083)

## 16. Two kinds of failure
- **What it is:** Separate the failure you want from the failure you do not.
- **Parts:** Experimental failure: you tried something new and the outcome was unknown in advance. Accept it, salvage the people and lessons (Fire phone fed Echo). Operational failure: you know how to do it and did it badly (the 151st fulfillment center). Diagnose and fix it. If you knew it would work, it was not an experiment.
- **When to use:** Reviewing a miss, and deciding whether a failure earns tolerance.
- **How to score (guidance):** Was the outcome truly unknown? If yes, it is a good failure. If the team had operating history and still missed, it is an execution problem.
- **Quote:** "there are two different kinds of failure there's experimental failure that's the kind of failure you should be happy with and then there's operational failure" (S073)
- **Sources:** 6 (S014, S025, S041, S073, S074, S106)

## 17. The flywheel *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** A self-reinforcing loop where each part makes another part stronger.
- **Parts:** Retail: cut cost and defects, lower prices, grow volume, spread fixed costs, lower prices again. Marketplace: selection attracts customers, customers attract sellers, and FBA ties Marketplace to Prime. Prime: a bucket that does not leak can be filled with an eye dropper. Launch: cheaper launch brings more demand and more practice, which makes launch cheaper.
- **When to use:** Businesses with high fixed costs and price-sensitive customers; designing offers that feed each other.
- **How to score (guidance):** Draw the loop. Does each new piece feed another? An offer that adds revenue without strengthening the loop scores low. A leaky bucket (members who churn) fails no matter how fast you pour.
- **Quote:** "Focus on cost improvement makes it possible for us to afford to lower prices, which drives growth. Growth spreads fixed costs across more sales, reducing cost per unit, which makes possible more price reductions." (S049)
- **Sources:** 6 (S007, S010, S040, S049, S062, S088)

## 18. The seven-year horizon
- **What it is:** Extend the time a bet has to pay off, and most competitors drop out of the race.
- **Parts:** If everything must work in three years, you compete with everyone. At seven years, you compete with a fraction. The quarter being reported was baked about three years earlier. New businesses take three to seven years to matter, so judge seeds on that clock. Long-term thinking is a discipline, not a natural habit.
- **When to use:** Deciding whether to fund a slow-building bet, and setting expectations with investors.
- **How to score (guidance):** Does the bet need five to seven years to pay? Can this founder's capital and investors actually wait that long? A long bet with three-year money is mismatched.
- **Quote:** "If everything you do needs to work on a three-year time horizon, then you're competing against a lot of people. But if you're willing to invest on a seven-year time horizon, you're now competing against a fraction of those people." (S070)
- **Sources:** 6 (S014, S024, S037, S054, S070, S098)

## 19. Mirror test for critics
- **What it is:** A procedure for handling criticism of a new move.
- **Parts:** Look in the mirror and ask whether the critics are right. If they are, change and do not resist. If you still believe you are right, hold firm and let no pressure move you. Separate well-meaning critics (they want it to work) from self-interested ones (incumbents). Read for the real ask: critics of Amazon's wages were asking it to lead.
- **When to use:** Public or internal pushback on something new.
- **How to score (guidance):** Was the critique honestly assessed before deciding to change or hold? Changing because of bad PR alone scores low; so does ignoring a critic who is factually right.
- **Quote:** "when you're criticized, first look in a mirror and decide, are your critics right? If they're right, change. Don't resist." (S047)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S003, S041, S047, S070, S091)

## 20. Wandering and efficiency *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Two working modes. Execute efficiently when you know the destination; wander, guided by hunch and curiosity, when you do not.
- **Parts:** Efficiency for known paths. Wandering for non-linear discoveries no customer asked for (AWS, Echo). Meetings without tight agendas. Wandering is a kind of humility: you admit you do not yet know where you are going. It needs a conviction that the prize is big enough.
- **When to use:** Splitting a team's time between exploring and executing.
- **How to score (guidance):** Is the destination known? If yes, demand a plan. If no, protect room to wander and do not judge it by efficiency metrics.
- **Quote:** "Efficiency and invention are sort of at odds, because real invention, Not incremental improvement... real invention, real lateral thinking that requires wandering, and you have to give yourself permission to wander." (S045)
- **Sources:** 6 (S010, S023, S042, S044, S045, S066)

## 21. Crisp documents, messy meetings, and high standards *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Replace slides with a six-page narrative memo read in silence, then hold an open discussion. High standards behind it are teachable.
- **Parts:** Six pages of real sentences. About 30 minutes of silent study hall. The senior person speaks last. Truth-seeking, not a pitch. Standards have four elements: teachable, domain specific, you must recognize good, and you must coach realistic scope. A great memo takes a week or more; underestimating that scope is the usual miss. He also took quarterly two-to-three-day retreats to write memos.
- **When to use:** Any important decision meeting, and when work quality is uneven.
- **How to score (guidance):** Was there a written narrative that forced clear thinking? Was dissent surfaced? On a miss, did the team fail to recognize good, or underestimate the effort?
- **Quote:** "I want crisp documents and messy meetings." (S001) / "So, the four elements of high standards as we see it: they are teachable, they are domain specific, you must recognize them, and you must explicitly coach realistic scope." (S065)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S003, S045, S065, S101, S106)

## 22. High-velocity decisions and disagree and commit *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Keep decision quality and speed together, and end standoffs without compromise or attrition.
- **Parts:** No one-size-fits-all process. Decide most things with about 70 percent of the information you wish you had (paraphrase of S064). Be good at spotting and fixing bad decisions. When people disagree, state it, then commit fully: no sniping, no told-you-so. It runs both ways; the boss commits to the team's call when they are closer to the ground truth. Escalate true misalignment fast; never settle by who gets exhausted first.
- **When to use:** Any organization where decisions stall.
- **How to score (guidance):** Is the team waiting for near-complete data on a reversible call? Is a dispute being settled by stamina? Both are speed failures. After the call, is everyone helping it work?
- **Quote:** "I disagree and commit all the time." (S064) / "Never get to a point where you are resolving something by who gets exhausted first. Escalate that." (S045)
- **Sources:** 6 (S007, S036, S045, S064, S073, S091)

## 23. Math-based versus judgment-based decisions *(Invent and Wander)*
- **What it is:** Let data decide when history can model the answer; use judgment when it cannot.
- **Parts:** Math-based: enough history, so judgment is a junior partner (inventory, A/B tests). Judgment-based: little data, or the experiment would amount to deciding (Prime, price cuts, letting third-party sellers onto product pages). Finance modeled Prime and the results looked horrifying (S044); the trendlines and judgment said go. Price cuts lose in short-term math and win over five to ten years.
- **When to use:** Before any big call, to choose how to decide.
- **How to score (guidance):** Can a cheap test settle it? Then test. If not, name the judgment openly, expect controversy, and check intuition against whatever data exists.
- **Quote:** "Math-based decisions command wide agreement, whereas judgment-based decisions are rightly debated and often controversial, at least until put into practice and demonstrated." (S053)
- **Sources:** 5 (S011, S021, S023, S044, S053)

## 24. Systematic risk elimination
- **What it is:** What early capital is for.
- **Parts:** Be honest about the odds (he gave Amazon about 30 percent and told investors, including his parents, there was a 70 percent chance they would lose it all). List the biggest risks. Spend early dollars removing them one at a time. Write the business plan knowing it will not survive reality, because writing it forces you to think. Take bigger risks once the company is robust.
- **When to use:** Seed-stage spending, fundraising, and any founder pitch that hides the odds.
- **How to score (guidance):** Does this spend remove a top risk, or add a new one? Did the founder tell backers the real odds?
- **Quote:** "what you do with those early precious capital resources is you go about systematically trying to eliminate risk. So, you pick whatever you think the biggest problems are, and you try to eliminate them one at a time." (S043)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S029, S043, S089, S108)

## 25. The last high-judgment champion
- **What it is:** His rule for when to kill a struggling bet.
- **Parts:** Keep funding while at least one person with good judgment still believes and wants to keep trying. When the last one folds, stop. Auctions and zShops failed before Marketplace worked, so give up on things, but not easily.
- **When to use:** Multi-year projects that have not paid off.
- **How to score (guidance):** Name the champions and judge their judgment, not their enthusiasm. If none remain, stop. If the only champion is the founder and the founder is sunk-cost attached, treat that as a warning.
- **Quote:** "I'm willing to keep funding things if if if there's one high judgment champion who still wants to keep trying" (S010)
- **Sources:** 2 (S010, S036)
