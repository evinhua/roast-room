# Principles: Gary Marcus

Built from sixteen batches of extraction notes over 109 sources (2017 to 2026; S079 to S115 are undated video transcripts). 30 decision rules, roughly most-used first. "Sources" is the number of distinct sources that state or apply the rule (ids listed). Confidence: high = 3 or more sources, medium = 2, low = 1. Where his view moved over time, the newer view is marked.

1. **If there is no principled fix, more data and more scale will not fix it.** "you need an actual solution to that problem. More data is just hand-waving." (S008) "these neural network models that we have right now they're not reliable and they're not truthful ... just because you make them bigger doesn't mean you solve that problem." (S047) He mocks the industry's standing reply as "Oh, Gary, give us a little more data and we'll solve all these problems." (S081) Sources: 13 (S001, S006, S008, S013, S017, S026, S047, S081, S084, S101, S110, S112, S113). Confidence: high.

2. **Treat hallucination as part of the product, not a bug that will be patched.** "hallucinations are not a bug in that sense right hallucinations are inherent to the way that the system works" (S013) "this is not going away as long as we're using this architecture" (S113) He says he predicted it in The Algebraic Mind (2001), and that some newer reasoning models hallucinate more, not less (S010, S032). Sources: 17 (S010, S011, S013, S025, S028, S032, S035, S036, S076, S080, S081, S097, S100, S101, S106, S111, S113). Confidence: high.

3. **Match the reliability you need to the stakes before you hand over authority.** "if you want to delegate authority to a machine the machine needs to work right" (S013) "what if the LLM is 80% correct at such like making up examples for brainstorming, that's probably great if it's 80%. But if you're doing some medical thing, 80% might not be that great." (S098) Sources: 14 (S008, S009, S013, S019, S045, S047, S087, S092, S097, S098, S100, S102, S108, S113). Confidence: high.

4. **A system that cannot check its own work needs something outside it that can.** "large language models cannot fact check their work they cannot sanity check their work and that's as far as I'm concerned fatals to the whole operation" (S113) "LLMs are no substitute for good well-specified conventional algorithms." (S067) Put the checking in code, a database, a verifier, or a skilled person (inferred from S054, S081, S103, S113). Sources: 8 (S010, S032, S054, S067, S068, S081, S103, S113). Confidence: high.

5. **Power should track proven reliability: do not give a system rope until it shows it understands what you asked.** "we shouldn't be giving it much rope we can't really be giving it that much opportunity to do things until we can be confident that it does understand the things that we're asking." (S047) "putting a nots smart system in charge of things is not itself a smart thing to do." (S093) Sources: 7 (S009, S014, S047, S056, S093, S107, S111). Confidence: high.

6. **Give agents as little power as the job needs.** "Giving the keys to the castle to an LLM, at our current level of technological readiness, seems to me to be batshit insane." (S039) "Unless we radically up our game, deploying LLMs as agents is an accident waiting to happen." (S070) Newer (2026): agents with credentials and no monitoring are already causing real breaches, so he calls it foreseeable, not hypothetical (S074, S077, S082, S089). Sources: 11 (S027, S030, S039, S047, S070, S074, S077, S082, S089, S109, S111). Confidence: high.

7. **Fluency is not truth; assume reviewers will be fooled by polish.** "The information that they make is so fluid and so grammatical that even professional editors sometimes get sucked in and get fooled by this stuff." (S002) "generative AI promises to answer literally any question about the world it almost never says I don't know" (S112) Sources: 11 (S002, S007, S032, S040, S042, S047, S087, S103, S107, S110, S112). Confidence: high.

8. **Humans in the loop are not magic; check that the check works.** "Having humans in the loop is not magic" (S073) The CNET case (roughly half of AI stories with errors editors passed) is his standard example (S087, S110). When review is fast and output always looks plausible, the human rubber-stamps (S073). Sources: 4 (S073, S087, S108, S110). Confidence: high.

9. **Ask where it fails, not only where it works.** "understanding exactly why an error happens any kind of cognitive error um is very informative in order to understand how the system as a whole works" (S013) "you can't improve the engineering if you can't identify the problems and weak points, such that you can address them. Any engineer knows that." (S052) Sources: 13 (S006, S010, S013, S018, S040, S042, S052, S057, S058, S086, S091, S107, S111). Confidence: high.

10. **Strong claims need strong evidence: the denominator, the data, replication, and the boring explanation ruled out.** "that's just advertising that's not science" (S006) "Strong claims need strong evidence." (S061) "any given piece of data, on its own, can probably be explained in more than one way; and often the correct explanation is the less colorful one." (S065) Sources: 10 (S001, S006, S042, S044, S050, S061, S063, S065, S094, S114). Confidence: high.

11. **A better score is not a different kind of system.** "Real progress on some dimensions, but stuck in place on others." 12. **Patching the viral example or adding "don't hallucinate" to the prompt is not a fix; test nearby variants.** "The real question is not about the replicability of the specific strings that Ernie and I tested, but about the replicability of the general phenomena." 13. **Benchmarks are not your job; measure on your own fresh, messy tasks and check for contamination.** "Benchmark success is a limited indicator of general intelligence." 14. **Judge what has shipped, not what is promised.** "The cards Sam is holding are not as great as he lets on." 15. **Discount forecasts from people who profit from belief, and watch what they do with real money.** "industry has a vested interest in making people believe that AGI is imminent" (S033) On AI CEOs who lobby against the rules they call for in public: "this is you know very much at least the company speaking from two sides of its mouth" (S110) "we can infer that the banks don't really think that OpenAI is worth its current valuation. 16. **Early exponential curves do not continue forever.** "Anyone assuming that everything is simultaneously rising exponentially, and that it will continue to do so indefinitely, is a fool." 17. **Remember the record of broken AI promises when you forecast.** "The failure of the AI 2027 team to reckon with the immense history of broken promises and delays in the AI field is, in a team that styles itself as forecasters, inexcusable." 18. **Multiply the odds of every step, and ask how it would actually happen.** "the AI 2027 scenario almost certainly underestimates how much time we have to prepare for general intelligence, by years if not decades." 19. **Use LLMs where errors are cheap and a skilled person checks the work.** "next-token prediction is great for a kind of fancy autocomplete for coding, for example." ## Where these rules bend
- He is strongest on what fails and weakest on how fast adoption spreads anyway. He admits he underestimated ChatGPT's uptake: "I just didn't think it was gonna become that popular ... I should have seen it." (S009) He also says "I didn't anticipate that computer programmers would find enormous value in large language models" (S101), and that he speculated less about what LLMs would be good at than about how they fail (S104).
- His rules are built around reliability in open-world, high-stakes use. For low-stakes products where a person checks every output (drafts, brainstorms, code with tests), several rules above loosen sharply, by his own cost-of-error logic (inferred from S008, S032, S007, S098, S100).
- He mostly argues about frontier labs, AGI claims, and policy, not about running a small company; applying his market-level bubble calls to one startup's timing is a stretch. He has been early on collapse timing more than once and calls the timing "entirely psychological now" (S015); the pattern is inferred from S015, S023, S035, S054, S107.
- His misses run in one direction: things moved faster or got more money than he expected (Winograd schemas (S052), machine reading (S111), OpenAI's funding and valuation (S035, S111), voiceover jobs (S108)). Read his "not soon" as strong on reliability and weaker on adoption, funding, and narrow capability gains (inferred from S035, S052, S101, S108, S111).
- He says he is not a finance expert and does not know how exposed banks are (S087), and that he does not follow token pricing in detail (S081). His economic numbers are borrowed and rough.
