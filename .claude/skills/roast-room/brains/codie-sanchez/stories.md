# Stories: Codie Sanchez

Built from the batch notes over 117 usable sources (S001 to S120, minus S045, S091, S092). 20 stories and cases she reuses, with the lesson she draws, most retold first. Source ids in parentheses. Where her numbers change between retellings, every version is listed. Member case studies written in the newsletter's "we" voice are grouped at the end and marked as company teaching.

1. **The first laundromat.** While still in finance she bought a laundromat with an operator who knew the business, told almost nobody, and was embarrassed by it. "that laundromat that I bought that cash flowed $67,000. We bought it for 100k" (S058) Figures vary: about $100K for $67K a year (S013, S018, S043, S056, S058, S066, S067); $60K to $80K, raised to $150K to $180K after new machines and a lease renegotiation (S010); $65K a year (S033); split 50/50 with the operator for about 25% a year (S090); $0 of her own cash through $100K of seller financing over three years plus equipment loans, a 268% cash-on-cash return (S072); sold later at about 3x (S061). It grew to 25 to 30 laundromats (S033) and to stakes in dozens of companies. Lesson: size a first deal so a loss is survivable and a win is real income, and borrow the operating skill you lack (S018, S042, S053, S061). She later called the laundromat a terrible first business to recommend and says she uses it because it is easy to understand (S042).

2. **Her uncle's plumbing company.** At retirement (in the 2023 telling, during cancer) her uncle shut down a profitable plumbing company and paid to close it, because nobody told him it could be sold. "he shut the business down. He had no idea he had a sellable asset." (S010) Numbers vary: $2 to 3M profit on about $5M revenue (S006, 2021); $5 to 6M revenue and $1 to 3M profit (S010, 2023); millions in revenue and hundreds of thousands to close (S013); millions in revenue (S070). It was the origin of her micro private equity thesis. Lesson: many owners do not know they hold a sellable asset, which is why off-market outreach works (S006, S010, S013, S070). The same pattern shows up in the Sean Rad bookbinding shop (S044) and the crochet website that sold for $90K (S070).

3. **Strike Fire Productions: expertise for equity.** Her company paid a podcast production firm each month; the owner hated sales, so she offered growth for equity. Terms and income vary by telling: $10,000 for 49%, $5,852 in month one and her money back in three months (S020); 49% for $10K and $5K to $10K a month for about a year, then sold back as too small (S014); "I made like 8 or 9K a month off of that business that I invested only 10K for." (S021); $10K to $30K a month for years (S022); 40% of a $300K revenue, $150K profit company for $10K (S058); about $10K down for a company that grew to $300K to $400K a year (S042). Lesson: read your own expense list for businesses you can buy into with skill instead of cash (S010, S020, S047, S058).

4. **Her own starts failed first.** Selling South, a US and Latin America consulting site, and Threads Refined, a fashion styling marketplace that lost six figures, went nowhere (S004, S010, S011). Retellings differ on Selling South: bought as a small website and built up until her State Street boss made her sell it (S065), or built because she wanted to without testing demand (S004). She lists more misses: a podcast, a real estate deal, a CPG company, and a Mexico deal. "It was like failure, failure, failure, failure, failure until eventually I learned so much from those failures." (S022) Her first deal lost $50,000 and another $75,000 (S027). Lesson: validate with credit cards before you build; for her the thing that worked was buying (S004, S011, S022, S027).

5. **The $12 million losses: trust is not diligence.** She tells several large losses that may overlap. A cannabis pre-rolls owner in Oakland stole and she lost $12 million in 90 days (S023); a counterparty fudged the books, took the money to Dubai, and nothing was recovered (S049); "I've lost like one deal we lost $12 million on lots of deals I've lost like sub a million bucks." (S021), with about $25M lost inside a PE group (S021); a company she funded called six weeks later three weeks from out of cash and fraud surfaced, put at $25M (S062); $12M exposure with manipulated statements and someone leaving the country (S033). After enough losses she pattern-matches and walks (S047). Lesson: she could model the business but trusted the wrong person; the risk is who, not how (S033, S049, S062).

6. **Friends and partners who stole.** A close friend her husband wanted to invest with stole from them after she talked herself out of a gut flag (S009); "I did a deal with a guy that I thought was really buddies with me and it turned out he just really wanted to steal from us and use us" (S053); "we lost several hundred thousand dollars but more important a friendship" (S044); two friendships ended, "One incompetence and one fraud." (S010); she wrote a big check to remove a partner with a record of mediocrity (S027), put at $1 million in one telling (S087). Her fix: "now I have a new rule and the rule is No New Friends" (S008), also told as a one-year rule (S043, S044, S053). Lesson: friends go through the same diligence as strangers, and the partner choice is the mistake (S008, S009, S023, S027, S043, S044, S053, S054, S055).

7. **"We get rich quietly."** Her finance firm refused to let her build a public voice, and she left to build Contrarian Thinking. Tellings differ: her CEO walked her on a beach and said they get rich quietly (S005, S028); in one retelling she quotes the older billionaire's words to her: "Here, we get rich quietly. We don't do it that way. And we're on a boat going right. And if you want to go left, you got to get your own bow." (S052); two PE partners each made her pick (S013); a managing director emailed that she had "dumbed down our company" and she later said her bank account is bigger than his (S063, S075). She needed to be pushed out twice before she went alone (S013, S048). Lesson: audience is leverage and a deal-flow engine, and an exit from a bad fit costs a toll worth paying (S005, S013, S028, S052, S063, S075).

8. **Spencer's trash route.** A software engineer angry at his neighborhood trash service posted on Facebook, collected prepayments, and only then bought a truck; he says he is happier as a garbage man. Numbers differ: about $15,000 in prepayments overnight (S011, S090); 150 customers in one day and a $15K used truck (S025); $13,000 in monthly recurring revenue before the truck (S085); about 200 households prepaying three months, with t-shirts and a free year as rewards (S102, newsletter). Lesson: if you start, get money before you build (S011, S025, S085, S090, S102).

9. **Bill Perkins: has small infected your thinking?** Her billionaire mentor heard her deals and asked whether years of small deals had shrunk her ambitions. In her retelling, his words: "do you think you have been investing in small business for so long small has affected your thinking" (S008) She tells it as liberation, not insult, and as a reason to stay the smallest person in the room (S003, S008, S015, S042). Perkins also lost $10M on a Salvadoran power plant and called it silly, sized to his wealth (S024). Lesson: the small-deal lens has a ceiling, and risk should be sized to real wealth, not fear (S003, S008, S015, S024, S042).

10. **The number two who quit, and the flights to Chile.** Her number two resigned by phone, the third key person to leave in 30 days. "I wasn't the indispensable founder holding it all together, I was the bottleneck holding it all back." (S029) She told it again as realizing she was the business and asking what system needs to exist so it does not matter who leaves (S076). Earlier she flew to Santiago more than 100 times believing she was essential, broke down crying in an airport on her birthday, and the business then ran eight years without her (S095, S111). She also turned down Richard Branson's island because she thought the company would fail without her, and called it "a lie I told myself" (S002, paraphrase). Lesson: a business that needs the owner is a job (S002, S029, S076, S095, S111).

11. **The empty bank accounts.** She walked into a bank expecting about a million dollars and found the account near zero because her head of operations hid the slide (S004). Contrarian Thinking itself came within four weeks of zero because one person ran finances wrong, caught only during a loan analysis (S050), told also as "a few weeks away from being completely out of money" (S087, paraphrase). A portfolio CEO called eight weeks from zero cash with a 12-week sales cycle, and she wrote the list of every cash lever: raise prices, collect faster, stretch payables, renegotiate rent (S044, S050). Lesson: track cash weekly yourself and never let one person be the only eyes on the money (S004, S044, S050, S087).

12. **The 19-year-old who bought in with sweat equity.** A teenager with no money bought into or out of a business he worked for. The 2024 telling names him Adam, buying his employer's graphic design vendor with seller financing (S001); the 2026 telling names him Jared, buying the owner out over 18 months for $60K total, with the company worth $1M+ two years later (S021); another tells a 19-year-old who negotiated 20 to 30% of a real estate marketing agency and sold it (S090). These may be one story told several ways. Lesson: you can buy with time and skill instead of cash (S001, S021, S090).

13. **Jay's zero-down buyout.** A former coworker wanted Jay to run his $1.5M revenue business; she said she does not run other people's businesses unless she owns them, and he sold instead, zero down and paid from profits over ten years. He had no kids to inherit, was tired, cared about his staff, and did not want private equity (S020, S022, S023). Lesson: the trusted insider is the best seller-financing buyer, and the seller's motive is rarely just price (S020, S022, S023).

14. **The reluctant partner bought out over three years.** As a minority partner she wanted to buy out the majority holder, who refused for three years until she proved herself; she paid about $1 million from the business's profits and it now does $15M to $16M a year (S022). Lesson: patience, and the business can fund its own purchase (S022).

15. **Thirty-five deals for one.** Trying to buy a marketing agency, she got a string of nos and silence. "So, 35 deals in one vertical to get one seller financing deal done." (S022) Members repeat it: one signed 200+ NDAs and lost a competitive deal but kept the broker's trust, who then called him first on a seven-figure deal (S036, newsletter); another followed up for over a year before closing a laundromat (S109). Lesson: buying is a volume game and follow-up wins (S022, S036, S109).

16. **The deals she walked from.** A Chicago dental manufacturer looked profitable until three months and about $20K of legal diligence showed profit was off by roughly $2M (S058). In another telling of a Chicago dental deal she flew in during a blizzard, drove 45 minutes from the airport, and walked on lifestyle and geography (S062). A cannabis CEO's hallway of celebrity photos told her the company was going to zero, and it did (S062, paraphrase). She lost money on autonomous ships and protein synthesis: "I have lost money on things like autonomous ships and protein synthesis because they were way outside of my dealbox there go but I did them anyways" (S074; also S022). Lesson: verify the numbers, bet on the person, and stay inside your deal box (S022, S058, S062, S074).

17. **The gross profit clause.** Her attorney missed a clause that paid a partner on gross profit instead of net, and she had to pay out on the top line, nearly sinking the deal (S007, paraphrase). Lesson: definitions and structure decide the real price (S007).

18. **The ad-dependent client.** A $10M business with 70% margins spent heavily on Facebook and Google until costs ate the margin. She told the owner to cut all ads; revenue fell about 30%, profit rose, and pop-ups and influencer deals later pushed both past the start (S039). Her own media company did $3M+ on under $10K of content spend, then $5M+ on $200K, then $7M on about $1M, with zero ads (S039). Lesson: paid growth had outrun the real economics; earned attention lasts (S039).

19. **Members who bought instead of built.** She reuses ordinary buyers as proof: Hannah, 22, from factory shifts to a $0 down car wash (S025); a $675K purchase of two car washes and storage, 95% seller financed (S025); Meredith's $900K landscaping company with 85% of customers over ten years and no website, where "I start to hear the sound of money" (S025, paraphrase); Diana's SBA-funded window cleaning company with an operator (S021); a $125K appliance repair company, 100% seller financed at 0% (S021); Anna's $1.5M SBA deal with no business background (S026, S036); Dave, a pharma engineer, closing a $10M+ industrial services company 14 months after joining (S117, newsletter); a former medical device salesman with an eight-figure portfolio in a few years (S075). Lesson: the process is teachable and ordinary people run it (S021, S025, S026, S075).

20. **Building from scratch where a buy existed.** A laundromat couple built new instead of buying, spent $2 million, ran nine months late, nearly went bankrupt, and, as she retells it, the wife told her "we should have listened to you" (S090; the couple's words in her retelling). She says every dollar spent building a new laundromat is worse than buying one (S090). Lesson: the cleanest build-versus-buy case she tells; buy first, then improve (S090).

**Company teaching cases (newsletter, we voice).** Erika and Kevin, a Navy couple, bought a 1968 scratch-and-dent appliance store with $1M+ in sales and no advertising because they understood the seller wanted care, not the top bid (S032; also told in S097, which is signed Codie). Katie and John lifted their price ceiling and bought a $2M brand they would have missed (S032). Two businesses with the same $500K profit at 8% and 25% margins carry very different risk (S030). Dave, a 71-year-old electrical contractor with a 2009 website who wants to go fishing, is the seller behind the AI data center boom (S101). Read these as company teaching, not her own deals.
