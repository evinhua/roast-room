# Frameworks: Codie Sanchez

25 frameworks built from 117 usable sources (2017 to 2026): long-form interviews, her own YouTube and BigDeal channels, the two books as she teaches them, and Contrarian Thinking newsletters. Roughly most-used first; the build-or-buy entry leads because it is the question this seat owns. "Sources" counts distinct sources across all notes where the idea is used (ids listed). Names are hers where she named them; otherwise a short descriptive name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from her stated thresholds, not quotes. Newsletters signed "Team Contrarian" or unsigned (S030 to S032, S034 to S037, S040, S041, S093, S094, S096, S098, S100 to S105, S108, S110, S112 to S117, S119, S120) are company voice: cited as support only, never quoted as her words. ## 1. Buy the odds: start for a calling, buy for cash flow *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Her core answer to build or buy. First split by motive: if you cannot sleep for wanting this specific thing, build it. If you want income and freedom, it is a numbers problem, and the numbers favor buying a business that has already survived the startup years and found product-market fit. Buying lets you take the baton partway through the race instead of running every leg.
- **Parts:** Name the motive (mission or cash flow) (S007, S015). If cash flow, compare odds: startups fail about 90% of the time; bought businesses fail far less (S001, S011, S090). Ask whether the business already has paying demand, because product-market fit is what kills most new businesses (S054, S055). Compare the founder's first years of pay: early building years pay little (S019, S002). Then match the path to the person: a first-timer may need a franchise's system, a manager wants to buy, a risk lover can start (S043).
- **When to use:** Any time a founder proposes building from zero to make money, or weighs a new product line, location, or capability against acquiring one.
- **How to score:** Build scores high only if the founder is obsessed with this exact product, can fund years of low pay, and has paying demand. Otherwise score toward buying or buying in. Her numbers move between tellings (90% of startups fail; bought businesses 25% at worst and 5% at best in S011; SBA default rates of 7% to 25% in S090; a 5-year-old business has a 60% better chance of lasting 5 more in S021; 30% to 40% of businesses die for lack of product-market fit in S055), so treat them as rhetorical anchors, not one data set.
- **Quote:** "startups have a 90% failure rate over any 10e rolling period small businesses you buy have a 25% failure rate at the height and a 5% failure rate at the low" (S011)
- **Also:** "I could go grab the Baton from the fifth sixth seventh or eighth person and just run the last couple legs of the race" (S065)
- **Sources:** 20 (S001, S002, S006, S007, S011, S015, S016, S019, S021, S043, S049, S054, S055, S062, S065, S068, S069, S082, S085, S090)

## 2. Seller financing: remove the bank *(unnamed; pitched as more money, faster close, lower taxes)*
- **What it is:** The seller lets the buyer pay over time out of the business's own future profits. She pitches it to the owner as a better deal than a bank sale: a higher total price, interest paid to the owner instead of the bank, a close in weeks instead of about 120 days, and taxes spread over years.
- **Parts:** Earn trust first; show the owner what a bank would value the business at; list the costs of bank financing (rate, slow close, tax hit); offer a higher price on seller terms (S014, S018, S090). Use her terms (about 5.5% versus 8% to 12% at a bank) as a chip to offer the seller more money (S090). A seller with no buyer will take creative terms over no deal: she cites 95% seller financing on a $675K car wash (S025). Put down little cash if you want the owner to stay and help; pay cash if you want them gone (S069). Other no-cash routes she pairs with it: revenue share, profit share, asset-based lending, outside investors (S057), SBA loans (S021, S058).
- **When to use:** The buyer is short on cash, the seller is older and tired with no successor, the business is simple, and the books are messy enough that a bank would undervalue it.
- **How to score:** High if the seller fits the avatar (see 17) and cash flow covers the note with room to spare; low if the seller is young with several offers, or the deal only works with debt the business strains to carry. She usually says 60% of small business sales include some seller financing (S011, S018, S058, S061, S074), but once put it at 16% (S064), and says it is less normal for online businesses (S020). Treat 60% as deals with some seller financing, not deals that are all seller financed.
- **Quote:** "I pay you 1.5 instead of one uh so you get another $500,000 plus I will pay you the interest on the loan instead of the bank except I'll pay you 4% instead of 8%" (S014)
- **Also:** "A seller with no buyer lined up will take a creative deal over no deal at all and that will be your gain." (S025)
- **Sources:** 24 (S001, S008, S011, S013, S014, S018, S020, S021, S025, S043, S044, S049, S053, S056, S057, S058, S061, S064, S068, S069, S070, S072, S074, S090)

## 3. Personal P&L review, the Venmo challenge, and core satellite deals
- **What it is:** The businesses worth owning are already in your spending. Turn what you pay into what you own, and buy the businesses that orbit what you already do, because they are worth more to you than to anyone else on the street.
- **Parts:** Pull your Venmo, PayPal, credit card, and your company's P&L; drop the big public companies; for each small vendor ask whether you can reach the owner, whether it is worth owning, and whether you could find an operator (S001, S020, S022, S072). Run the list through AI to sort vendors (S047). For a company, read your own P&L and ask which vendors to buy, a move she says she copied from Amazon (S010). Core satellite: keep a core business and buy the satellites around it, such as a property owner buying the cleaning and landscaping firms they already pay (S062, S072, S074). Her first buy should sit next to what she knows (S022, S058). Your employer and its vendors are candidates too (S020). She bought out half of a former partner's company this way, with a small down payment backed by the company's assets (S022).
- **When to use:** A buyer who does not know where to start, or a founder about to build a tool, service, or capability they already pay someone else for.
- **How to score:** High if the founder already pays a small, reachable, under-run vendor that does what they were about to build, and their own business would make it worth more; low if the build has no existing provider to buy or the target is unrelated to anything they know.
- **Quote:** "The way you get deals for less than they're worth is that they are worth more to you than they are to anybody else on the street." (S072)
- **Also:** "It's you turning your costs into profits." (S020)
- **Sources:** 17 (S001, S008, S010, S020, S022, S033, S042, S047, S058, S061, S062, S072, S074, S076, S086, S090, S093)

## 4. Three ways to buy: the business buying tripod (money, expertise, time)
- **What it is:** You can buy into a business with cash, with expertise, or with time and sweat. Having no money is not a reason to skip ownership.
- **Parts:** Money is the highest-leverage leg, so use it if you have it (S008). Expertise beats sweat because it carries leverage (S022). No money but time: be the operator for someone with capital. No time but money: hire an operator. Neither: consult for equity or revenue share (S059). Decide what kind of risk you want (S047). Her count of how many legs you need changes: one in most tellings (S001, S008), two of the three in S090.
- **When to use:** A founder with skills but little capital, or one who could buy part of a business instead of starting a rival.
- **How to score:** High if the buyer can name which leg they bring and the deal is built around it; low if they bring none. She does not oversell the zero-dollar path: broke with no knowledge does not qualify (S021), and in debt, with dependents, and unable to read a P&L means learn first and do not act (S063).
- **Quote:** "there's three ways to buy a business you can either use cash you can use expertise or you can use time or what I call Sweat Equity" (S001)
- **Sources:** 15 (S001, S008, S018, S021, S022, S044, S047, S049, S053, S057, S059, S063, S068, S073, S090)

## 5. Downside first: protect the house *(unnamed; "go to zero risk")*
- **What it is:** Before upside, model how the deal dies, and size every deal so the worst case cannot take you out. Her first and only rule of buying is never to do a bad first deal.
- **Parts:** Stress test revenue drops of 20%, 50%, and 75% and pass if you are no longer profitable (S027). Ask how the business could go to zero and write protection for it, such as escrow against customer concentration or a third-party valuation of trucks and equipment (S027). Cap a deal at 20% of net worth and keep 12 months of cash after closing (S027). Model a grid from worst case to best case and set price and terms with enough buffer that normal wear does not ruin you (S060). Score every investment and keep the worst and best case so you can check yourself later (S072). Size the first deal so a loss is annoying, not ruinous (S007, S013, S061).
- **When to use:** Every deal and every big bet, especially a first one.
- **How to score:** 5 if the business stays profitable after a 50% revenue drop, the deal is a small slice of net worth, and the one thing that could kill it is covered in the contract; 3 if it survives only a 20% drop; 1 if a single customer, license, machine, or person can sink it and nothing protects against that.
- **Quote:** "What if the worst thing happens to your business this year are you still profitable if not we pass" (S027)
- **Also:** "I've never done a deal that could bankrupt me." (S058)
- **Sources:** 15 (S001, S007, S013, S026, S027, S043, S044, S056, S058, S060, S061, S072, S077, S087, S090)

## 6. Business or job test (the Owner Bottleneck)
- **What it is:** If the business needs you to run, you bought or built a job, not a business. She uses it to judge targets and to judge the founder's own company, and a buyer prices the business the same way.
- **Parts:** The three functions test: if fulfillment, sales, or distribution rests entirely on you, you are self-employed (S002). The vacation test: two weeks away without the business losing money, not expected in years one to three (S016); two to eight weeks in S004. As a buy signal, ask every seller what they do when they are not working (S013). The Owner Bottleneck Test: five questions on how much revenue, deals, pricing calls, problems, and profit depend on you, averaged into a percentage (S095). Three owner types: owner, operator, self-employed (S111). The trap is the hero habit, which she calls heroin (S002, S004, S016, S067).
- **When to use:** Valuing a target, deciding whether a founder can add a second business or product, or diagnosing why growth stalled.
- **How to score:** Use her bands from S095: under 30% owner dependence is a business; 30% to 60% is a well-paid job with equity; over 60% the business owns you.
- **Quote:** "Under 30%. You own a business. 30 to 60%. You own a well-paid job with equity attached. Over 60%. The business owns you, and a buyer is going to price it that way." (S095)
- **Also:** "If any one of those three falls entirely in your hands, you're still self-employed." (S002)
- **Sources:** 16 (S002, S004, S013, S014, S016, S029, S053, S061, S067, S071, S072, S076, S095, S098, S105, S111)

## 7. I buy realities, not dreams (the four buy rules and the target profile)
- **What it is:** Her pass filter. A target must be real, profitable, not a turnaround, and simple enough to explain to her grandmother. Buy what it makes today, not what it might make.
- **Parts:** The four tests (S012). First-deal profile: under $10M revenue to stay out of private equity's way, at least a 15% margin, nothing complex or patent-pending (S020). Margin bands: 15% to 50% is a healthy boring business, 1% to 3% is awful (S056). For a hands-off portfolio: 25%+ margins and about 10 or fewer employees (S059). Her own box: under $3M revenue, $100K to $1M profit, service-based, 3 to 5 years old, books that match the tax returns (S064). Three or more years of consistent profit and clean books (S097). The deal should work on a napkin (S057). Her image: a nice house on a normal block, not the fixer-upper and not the best house (S011, S027).
- **When to use:** Screening any acquisition, and judging any plan that rests on a future that has not happened yet.
- **How to score:** Pass if any of the four tests fails. For a first deal, a margin under 15%, a turnaround, or a story that needs a spreadsheet to explain is a pass.
- **Quote:** "I buy realities not dreams they have to be profitable businesses I don't do turnarounds and I want to be able to explain the business to my grandmother" (S012)
- **Also:** "Potential is what sellers call the work they didn't do" (S097)
- **Sources:** 16 (S010, S011, S012, S014, S020, S027, S043, S044, S049, S053, S056, S057, S059, S064, S074, S097)

## 8. Price versus terms
- **What it is:** A deal has two levers. Amateurs fight over price; pros control terms, which change the real price. Let the seller have the price if you get the terms.
- **Parts:** Small businesses are valued on profit, not revenue, usually 2x to 3x profit or seller's discretionary earnings, no calculator needed (S020, S070, S074). She calls 3x to 5x fair and aims for 2x to 3x by changing terms (S013). Stretch to milestone payments if the seller wants more (S090). Protect your return with terms: capital back before operator draws, a bigger stake for early risk, a minimum multiple on an early exit (S056). Her teaching stunt: a billion-dollar price paid at a dollar a day (S007). Use silence (S056). Laundromat multiples have risen from 2x to 3x to 4x to 7x, so the old arbitrage has mostly closed (S060).
- **When to use:** Negotiating any purchase, sale, partnership, or comp plan.
- **How to score:** High if the structure lets the business pay for itself and shifts unverified risk back to the seller; low if the founder is anchored on the headline price and ignoring timing, interest, gross versus net, and guarantees.
- **Quote:** "you can have your price and my terms or my terms and your price but you can't have both" (S018)
- **Also:** "Anybody who focuses on price is an amateur. Pros focus on cash flow and terms." (S021)
- **Sources:** 13 (S007, S013, S018, S020, S021, S056, S060, S070, S072, S074, S090, S010, S014)

## 9. Deal box and Deal Clarity (the perfect fit business)
- **What it is:** A written filter for what you will buy before you look at anything, like a Zillow search for a house. She refuses to name a "best" business because the right one depends on the buyer.
- **Parts:** Deal Clarity is a three-circle Venn: profit, obsession, and probability (S012); elsewhere skills, network, and obsession (S001, S062, S068). The worksheet asks how much you want to make, how much risk and time you will put in, where you must be, and what you will not do (S007, S061). The deal box sets industry, size, location, investor or operator role, financing, must-haves, and can't-haves (S020, S053), with green, yellow, and red scoring (S074). She adds two things private equity ignores: fun and dignity (S018). After several deals, only buy where you have an unfair advantage (S061). Revisit it often, because what fit six months ago may not fit now (S062).
- **When to use:** Before any search, and whenever a founder falls for a shiny deal that does not match what they need.
- **How to score:** Pass a target that fails the box. Score the founder high if they can state their box in a few lines; low if they are chasing whatever is in front of them. Her own losses in autonomous ships and protein synthesis came from deals outside her box (S074).
- **Quote:** "the biggest misn in buying businesses is that there's a good or a bad business there's really not it's like what's good or bad for you" (S049)
- **Sources:** 14 (S001, S007, S012, S018, S020, S049, S053, S061, S062, S063, S068, S069, S072, S074)

## 10. The deal funnel and reps (100 to 1, never fall in love)
- **What it is:** Dealmaking is a numbers game. You look at many businesses to buy one, fluency comes from reps, and the deals you walk away from matter as much as the ones you do.
- **Parts:** About 100 looked at to 1 bought (S001); 100, 50, 10, 1 (S023); about 50 listings to 1 business (S020); the average buyer in her community looks at about 30 businesses before one offer (S022); talk to 10 owners to find 1 prospect (S051). Track each deal through outreach, NDA, analysis, offer, and close, with the reason it was killed (S020, S022). Timelines: 3 months to learn, 6 to close, 12 to integrate in 2023 (S027); 3, 9, and 12 in late 2024 (S023); 8 to 18 months to a first deal and a three-year "MBA" of buy, grow, then do the next deal (S022, S062). Pick one to three sourcing methods, not all (S022). Never fall in love with a deal (S027, S043). No FOMO (S070).
- **When to use:** A buyer stuck on one deal, falling in love with it, or quitting after a few nos.
- **How to score:** High if the buyer has reviewed dozens of deals and walked away from many; low if this is the first or second business they looked at.
- **Quote:** "the most important deals that you ever do will be the ones you don't do" (S043)
- **Also:** "Most deals die at the altar of no follow-up." (S022)
- **Sources:** 12 (S001, S020, S022, S023, S027, S036, S043, S051, S062, S070, S072, S119)

## 11. Gateway drug businesses and the levels of the game
- **What it is:** Start with a simple, small business to learn the P&L, the deal, and the people without betting the house. Then sell it or move up. The point of the first deal is the lesson, not the money.
- **Parts:** Gateway examples: laundromats, car washes, vending (S001, S048, S069). Must be cheap, simple, few employees, and able to run without you full-time (S068). Four levels from Main Street Millionaire: Solo Venture, Hands-On CEO, On-Deck Operator, Market Leader (S014). Wealth levels need different skills: what gets you the first $100K will not get you the first million (S024, S043). Stair-step: do the unscalable thing first, then sell it to buy the scalable one (S069).
- **A tension, not a dated change:** She made laundromats the famous example, and she also warns about them: laundromats are a gateway, not a path to millions (S005, S059), do not start one (S053), their multiples have risen (S060), and she calls the laundromat a terrible first business to recommend, used only because it is easy to understand (S042). S005 (2023-10) holds both sides and the rest are undated, so there is no order to mark. Read: use the laundromat to explain, not as the default buy.
- **When to use:** Sizing a first deal, or telling a capable buyer they are aiming too small.
- **How to score:** For a first-timer, a gateway business scores high; for someone with real capital or skill it scores low because the upside is capped.
- **Quote:** "I call them gateway drug businesses because it's your like it's your little ease in to this game of business." (S048)
- **Sources:** 13 (S001, S005, S014, S024, S042, S043, S048, S053, S059, S060, S068, S069, S072)

## 12. BRRT and the value-add levers
- **What it is:** Her buy box in four letters: Boring, Recession resistant, Raise prices, add Technology. Then add more of the same. Most small businesses are underpriced, so price is the first lever after closing.
- **Parts:** The four letters (S008, S011, S074); she sends back anything that fails (S008). Raise prices: most small businesses are 30% to 300% underpriced (S054, S055, S065) or have not raised in one to three years (S074). Her first 3M is pricing, operational bloat, and automation (S065); a second 3M is markets, marketing, and measurements (S057). Layer income streams: one laundromat into eleven (S043). Tech by growth rate: bond-like businesses get hardware only, minor-growth get field software, high-speed get the full stack (S012). Underwrite AI both ways: how much drag it removes and how safe the core is from it (S093, team voice).
- **When to use:** A fast first screen on any target, and the first plan after closing.
- **How to score:** One point per letter. A business that cannot raise prices or add tech has no value-add story and scores low even if it is boring.
- **Quote:** "we buy boring businesses that are recession resistant we raise the prices we add technology" (S054)
- **Sources:** 12 (S008, S011, S012, S043, S047, S054, S055, S057, S065, S074, S093, S102)

## 13. Boring beats sexy (the Songhurst matrix, the fit dad bod)
- **What it is:** The sexier the industry, the less the average person in it makes; the more boring, the more. Pick unglamorous, needs-based businesses with demand that holds when the market gets weird.
- **Parts:** The matrix (she calls it Songhurst or Song Hearst) plots sexiness against income (S014, S046, S063, S085). Examples: marketing graduates versus electricians, Joe Rogan versus the average podcaster (S059). Her own record: the sexy companies she passed on made little; the boring ones paid (S051). Durable businesses have code requirements, inspection cycles, and service contracts (S038). The picks-and-shovels of booms: electricians, HVAC, concrete (S101, team voice). Industry survival math: information businesses survive ten years at 29%, manufacturing and agriculture at 49% (S085). Divorce passion from profit (S059).
- **When to use:** A founder drawn to a glamorous idea, or choosing between a hot category and a dull one.
- **How to score:** Favor the dull, needs-based option unless the founder has a real edge in the glamorous one. A business whose appeal is mostly its logo scores low.
- **Quote:** "typically the sexier something is the less money you make in it on average the more boring something is the more money you can make in it on average" (S059)
- **Sources:** 12 (S005, S014, S023, S024, S038, S046, S051, S059, S063, S078, S085, S101)

## 14. SOWS: Stale, Old, Weak, Simple (with the fax machine test and Lindy)
- **What it is:** Her first-buy screen. Stale means un-innovated; Old means it has survived; Weak means weak competitors and a weak current owner; Simple means easy to understand.
- **Parts:** Fax machine test: a fax machine, a landline in use, or paper invoices means room to add software and marketing (S013); like shag carpet over hardwood floors, and no marketing yet is a signal, not a red flag (S057). Lindy effect: the longer a business has existed, the likelier it continues (S014, S021, S023, S056, S074, S090). Her "old" ranges from 3 to 6 years (S018) to 5 to 7 (S014) to 5 to 10 (S011). Weak means the worst competitor you can imagine, so being 10% better is a moat (S074). She orders the letters differently across tellings (S014, S018, S074). Add-backs: owners run personal spending through the business, which hides real profit (S013).
- **When to use:** Picking a first acquisition, or judging whether a target has upside you can add.
- **How to score:** One point each for stale, old (5 years or more), weak competition, and simple. 4 is her ideal first deal; 2 or less is a pass for a beginner. She says Lindy is a probability, not a promise (S018).
- **Quote:** "the Lindy effect says the longer a business has been in existence the higher likelihood it will have to continue to be in business" (S014)
- **Sources:** 11 (S011, S013, S014, S018, S021, S023, S056, S057, S074, S090, S097)

## 15. Courting the seller: curiosity, empathy, pain, solution (and the Love Formula)
- **What it is:** A seller conversation run like dating. Build trust, find the pain the owner already has, then offer a low-pressure way out. You cannot create the urge to sell, only find the owner who already has it.
- **Parts:** Curiosity, empathy, pain pushing, solution giving (S008). The Love Formula: likeability plus understanding plus belief (S073). Never open with price or terms (S072) and never with a list of what is wrong (S008). Start with humility (S012). Sell yourself with wins, grit, relevant experience, curiosity, and compassion (S018). Make every conversation a win for the owner, even when you pass, because owners talk (S008). Never sell anyone: find the people already predisposed (S042, S057).
- **When to use:** Off-market outreach, seller financing, and any negotiation with an owner who built the thing.
- **How to score:** High if the buyer can state the seller's real motive and offers to solve it; low if the pitch is a lowball and a list of fixes.
- **Quote:** "you just start with curiosity empathy pain pushing solution solving" (S008)
- **Also:** "you never sell anyone at anything. You only find people who are already predisposed to want what you're selling." (S057)
- **Sources:** 10 (S008, S012, S014, S018, S042, S057, S062, S072, S073, S090)

## 16. Off-market origination: tell everyone you buy businesses
- **What it is:** Most good deals are never listed. Find owners before they list, through the people and places that touch them, and make deals find you.
- **Parts:** Say publicly that you buy businesses, with your geography and size (S022, S062). Work the professionals who touch the owner's money: accountants, attorneys, insurance agents, advisors (S023). Strategic targeting, direct outreach, third-party sourcers, and scraped leads (S020). Content as deal flow: talk publicly about buying and sellers come to you (S005, S010, S064). Raise your outreach effort a step above normal: DM, then note, then door knock (S063).
- **Where her view moved:** She has said never use public marketplaces and buy off-market only (S058, S062), but her 2024 and 2025 playbooks teach both: off-market if you have time and little money, on-market if you have money and little time (S022, S020). She advises against buy-side brokers (S020). Newer view: both, off-market for price.
- **When to use:** Building a pipeline, or when a buyer complains there are no good deals.
- **How to score:** High if the founder has a repeatable source of owners who are not yet listed; low if they rely only on listings.
- **Quote:** "the best way to find deals is off market deals period and the best way to find off market deals is for deals to find you" (S064)
- **Also:** "I want you to start telling people that you buy businesses. That is step one." (S022)
- **Sources:** 11 (S005, S010, S020, S022, S023, S049, S058, S062, S063, S064, S072)

## 17. The seven Ds and the seller avatar
- **What it is:** Why owners sell, and which owners will sell to you on good terms.
- **Parts:** The seven Ds: departure, divorce, disease, disagreement, distress, death, dullness (S020, S023, S090). Earlier lists were shorter: five Ds (S061) and three Ds (S072). The seller financing avatar checklist: long-running, under $1M profit, brick-and-mortar, long owner tenure, no successor, older owner, messy books, no leadership team, a prior relationship; more checks means likelier terms (S020). The average owner is in their late 60s and boomers own most small businesses (S020, S062). Pair the Ds with a business 5+ years old in an industry growing 3% to 5% a year (S090).
- **When to use:** Prioritizing outreach and predicting whether creative terms are possible.
- **How to score:** Count avatar checks. A young, fast-growing seller with several bids scores low.
- **Quote:** "do they have some version of the the 7ds which are you know disease dullness departure disagreement distress divorce death." (S090)
- **Also:** "The higher the number of checks, the more likely the seller will sell to you." (S020)
- **Sources:** 9 (S020, S023, S041, S061, S062, S072, S090, S101, S051)

## 18. Expertise to equity (and the six levels of dealmaking)
- **What it is:** Trade a result, not your hours, for a share of a business. Offer to raise revenue, cut costs, or remove the owner's pain, and get paid in a slice of what you create.
- **Parts:** The three legs: make the company more money, cut its costs, or take pain off the owner (S018, S053). Three flavors: revenue share, profit share, straight equity (S073). The value matrix: current profit, profit you add, times the industry multiple, then the share to ask for (S073). Leverage teeter-totter: the shakier the business and the more proven you are, the more equity you can ask for (S073). For creators, six levels of deals from flat fee up to pure equity plus distributions (S050). Partial acquisitions as a stair-step to full ownership (S044).
- **When to use:** A founder or employee with a skill an owner lacks, or a creator choosing between a fee and a stake.
- **How to score:** High if the offer names a result (take profit from X to Y) and pairs equity with some cash flow; low if it is straight equity for effort, which she calls equity you can't eat.
- **Quote:** "if I take your business to make this year $300,000 I charge you nothing for that could I own 20% of your business" (S073)
- **Also:** "you can't eat Equity" (S073)
- **Sources:** 9 (S014, S018, S022, S044, S048, S050, S053, S073, S083)

## 19. Operator first (the Sauron's eye problem)
- **What it is:** The owner's attention keeps a business running; when it moves, problems show up. So the deal needs a proven operator before it closes, and the cash flow has to pay that person.
- **Parts:** Sauron's eye: performance holds only where attention sits unless a killer operator holds it (S012). Find the operator first, then look for a business together (S064). Stack rank operators: experienced talent, known talent, unknown talent (S064). Ask candidates who they would bring from prior companies; the answer shows a leader or a manager (S062). Look in your own network for under-used, high-hustle people (S070). She has said she will no longer do a deal without an operator (S062), yet tells first-timers to get their hands dirty on the first acquisition (S061).
- **When to use:** Any acquisition, and any plan where the founder will own several things at once.
- **How to score:** High if a named, proven operator is in place and paid from cash flow; low if the plan is that the buyer will run it at night.
- **Quote:** "in my perfect world it's get your operator first and then together go and look for a business" (S064)
- **Sources:** 9 (S010, S012, S014, S056, S061, S062, S064, S070, S072)

## 20. Income stream test and debt sizing
- **What it is:** The business's income has to carry the deal, not your hopes. If it cannot pay for the debt, you, and an operator, you bought a job.
- **Parts:** The income must cover loan interest, your income, an operator's salary, and working capital (S014). Keep leverage at or under about 30% on a first deal (S021). Aim for a 3 to 5 year payback on money down (S010). Never fund a business at a loss for long (S010). Debt is good when the asset grows faster than the interest; keep a gap between loan and margin call, which she calls sleep (S026).
- **Where her view moved:** In 2023 she said she would never take a personally guaranteed loan on first deals (S027). She later teaches SBA loans, which are personally recourse, with eyes open (S058, S021 in 2026). Newer view: guarantees are acceptable with conservative sizing.
- **When to use:** Any purchase with debt.
- **How to score:** 5 if cash flow covers all four buckets with margin after a stress case; 3 if it covers debt and a modest owner income only; 1 if the buyer needs refinancing or growth to make the payments.
- **Quote:** "you don't buy a job you buy a business and it has to cover working capital" (S014)
- **Also:** "Debt should make you more free. If it makes you more fragile, you're using it wrong." (S026)
- **Sources:** 8 (S010, S014, S021, S026, S027, S048, S058, S072)

## 21. Diligence as detective work (tax returns, the 80/20 check, the 13-week cash flow)
- **What it is:** Assume the seller knows more than you and the numbers are wrong until proven. Verify against what the owner told the government, and watch cash weekly.
- **Parts:** Three things get you 80% of the way: the P&L by week and month for seasonality, the tax return, and the few factors that drive this type of business, like lease and utilities for a car wash (S057). Three to five years of tax returns beat P&Ls; verify cash in and expenses yourself (S066). Price toward the tax return when the books disagree (S014). Separate the business from the real estate (S053, S060). Hidden liabilities: liens and debt (S006), environmental studies for dry cleaners (S010), key-man and client concentration in service firms (S060). A graded due diligence question list, updated after every deal that goes wrong (S054). The 13-week cash flow, watched weekly, to catch fraud (S053, S090). The sniff tests: a great guy with a business versus a business with great guys, trust me bro, and will it be around in 15 years (S097).
- **When to use:** After a letter of intent and before close, and when a friend or partner wants to skip a step.
- **How to score:** High if the books match the tax returns, the key risks have a named check, and cash is tracked weekly; low if the buyer is trusting the seller's P&L or a relationship.
- **Quote:** "Tax returns are obviously more reliable than their financial statements or their P&Ls" (S066)
- **Sources:** 11 (S006, S008, S010, S014, S053, S054, S057, S060, S066, S090, S097)

## 22. The Own system: 12 profit levers and the Owner Score
- **What it is:** From Own or Be Owned. Run your business the way private equity runs a portfolio company: diagnose, score, set a strategy, review every quarter. Start with the P&L and fix what exists before adding anything.
- **Parts:** Diagnose, score, strategy, quarterly review (S004). Start with the P&L (S004). Twelve P levers, with pricing, product, persona, profit, process, and people as the core (S004); Promote is the ad lever (S039). Score the two levers dragging hardest, fix them, rescore every 90 days (S111). Three moves to more profit: sell to richer customers, raise prices, add recurring revenue (S004). The Owner Score sorts owners into 12 archetypes (closer, ball hog, visionary, artist, martyr, workhorse) that predict where they win and fail (S002, S016, S067). Pick lifestyle, scale, or exit for at least a year (S016). For a service business's first million she leads with persona, pricing, and product (S084).
- **When to use:** Before a founder builds or buys anything new. Fix the existing business first.
- **How to score:** Low if the founder wants a new product while prices have not risen, costs are unreviewed, and there is no recurring option; high if those are done and the next lever is clearly growth. Her claim: of about 1,600 businesses reviewed, all were underpriced and only about 30% had recurring revenue (S004).
- **Quote:** "the ones that matter are pricing, product, persona, profit, process, people." (S004)
- **Also:** "You don't have to choose for life, but you got to choose for a year." (S016)
- **Sources:** 9 (S002, S004, S016, S039, S067, S084, S095, S111, S098)

## 23. The first 90 days: transfer and transition (the valley of death)
- **What it is:** After closing, change almost nothing at first. Document, shadow the seller, then stabilize, then grow. The first year is when hidden problems surface, and the old owner probably ran it better than you will at first.
- **Parts:** Days 0 to 60 transfer and transition, 60 to 90 stabilize and systematize, 90 to 120 power up, 120 plus harness (S023). Announce yourself, say you are not there to cut jobs, then remove the one or two bad actors quickly (S090). Contract key people and the seller to stay through the transition (S010). The seller's mind has already left; plan the handover from day one (S070). Valley of death: lawsuits, inflated margins, and bad hires show up in year one (S062).
- **When to use:** Any acquisition plan that promises big changes right after close.
- **How to score:** High if the plan holds changes until the business is documented and the seller's knowledge has transferred; low if the buyer plans to rebuild in month one.
- **Quote:** "the first 60 days are called transfer and transition so this is where you're documenting everything you're following the person around and you're really not changing much" (S023)
- **Also:** "you should assume that the guy who's running it for 10 to 20 years probably did better than you and is going to." (S062)
- **Sources:** 7 (S007, S010, S014, S023, S062, S070, S090)

## 24. Sell first, build second *(unnamed; pre-sell before you build)*
- **What it is:** Her test for anyone who still wants to start. Get money, not compliments: pre-orders, deposits, or backers, before you spend on the build.
- **Parts:** Ask three people to pay and take their cards (S004). Take capital along with opinions (S004). Validate with pre-orders before equipment or inventory (S085). Write the press release before building; if it is not compelling, do not build (S089). Rent equipment first and buy only when revenue covers it (S086). Start at home or as a pop-up before a lease (S078). Move from idea to launch fast (S085). Her own failures, Threads Refined and Selling South, were never market tested (S004).
- **When to use:** Any build decision, new product, or new location.
- **How to score:** 5 if paying customers or deposits exist before the build; 3 if there are signed letters of intent only; 1 if the evidence is praise, likes, or a waitlist.
- **Quote:** "go and ask three people if they'd pay for it. Then take their credit cards and see if they'll pay for it." (S004)
- **Also:** "Sell first and build second." (S085)
- **Sources:** 7 (S004, S078, S085, S086, S089, S102, S011)

## 25. Rent check and the Ad Trap (own your distribution)
- **What it is:** Paid growth can outrun real economics: it rewards you at first, then acquisition cost climbs while margins fall. A business that rents its customers on someone else's platform does not fully own itself. Build owned distribution before you need it.
- **Parts:** Profit = (lifetime value minus acquisition cost) times customers. Ad Dependency Ratio: paid revenue over total revenue. Return on content spend prices the relationship; return on ad spend prices a transaction. Media ladder: owned first, earned second, paid last (S039). Platform businesses on Amazon, Meta, or TikTok are rented land (S025). Renting attention is a tax; build an audience before a product (S085). Do not run ads until persona, recurring revenue, referrals, pricing, and product are nailed (S084). A product without distribution is a secret (S033).
- **When to use:** A founder whose growth depends on paid ads or one platform, or one deciding whether to build a channel or buy reach.
- **How to score:** Her bands: Ad Dependency under 30% healthy, 30% to 60% danger, over 60% trapped (S039). She is against dependency, not against all ads, and leaves the timing of diversifying to the owner (S039).
- **Quote:** "If the business can't sing without ads, don't turn up the volume. Fix the song." (S039)
- **Sources:** 7 (S025, S033, S039, S081, S084, S085, S115)
