# Stories: Steve Jobs

20 stories and cases he reuses, built from the verified notes of all 17 batches, most-used first, with the lesson he draws. Source ids in parentheses.

1. **Cutting Apple from many products to four (1997).** He could not understand the product line after weeks, so he cut it to a grid of four: consumer and pro, desktop and portable. Engineers on cancelled projects came out relieved because they knew where they were going, the loss turned to profit within a year, and the grid was full and turned in about 148 days. Lesson: focus is a financial strategy and it energizes the right people (S001, S011, S021, S023, S044, S071).
2. **Xerox PARC (1979).** He saw a rough graphical interface and within ten minutes knew every computer would work that way, so blinded by it he missed the networking and objects in the room, and it took him years to find them again. Xerox never shipped it; Apple had to finish the work and make it cheap. Lesson: seeing it is not enough, you have to make it real, and look at everything in the room (S005, S010, S038, S067, S070).
3. **Pixar stops for the story.** Toy Story halted for months while the team was paid to fix a story that was not good enough, and every later film hit a story crisis and stopped too. Lesson: discipline on the core beats discipline on the schedule; no technology saves a bad story, a Pixar mantra he credits to John Lasseter (S012, S028, S066).
4. **Fired from Apple at 30.** The board sided with Sculley, and he was moved to an empty office where no reports came. He calls it the best thing that happened to him, since it led to NeXT and Pixar, but also says he lacked the experience to run a $2 billion company. Lesson: adversity can reset you, and own your part (S001, S005, S027, S036, S082).
5. **The iTunes deal with the labels.** Apple told the labels their copy protection would not work, got thrown out, kept coming back, called which rival services would fail, and won the 99 cent store, beating Napster and Kazaa on ease rather than in court. Later he used the labels' own DRM-free CDs to argue for DRM-free downloads. Lesson: be right, be patient, bring evidence, and compete with free by being better (S002, S009, S016, S030, S079, S080).
6. **NeXT: hardware failed, software survived.** The cube sold about 10,000 units and NeXT stopped making computers; the door for a new hardware company had closed before it started. Its software ran the first web server and became the base of Mac OS X. Lesson: check the world has not moved, and know which part of the bet is the real asset (S006, S015, S027, S067, S070).
7. **Saying no to the PDA to fund the iPod.** Apple passed on the PDA because phones would cover what most users did on the road. The resources went to the iPod, which took 70 percent share after years of single digit Mac share. Lesson: a no is what pays for the big yes (S012, S015, S029).
8. **Apple Retail.** Retail staff turned over too fast to explain a new product, so Apple built stores with a Genius Bar. Opening lines ran into the parking lot, 40 percent of buyers did not own a Mac, and the stores reached a billion dollars in sales faster than any retailer before. Lesson: if the channel fails your product, build your own (S029, S030, S049, S075).
9. **Making peace with Microsoft (1997).** With Apple near death he called Gates, settled terms on an hour's barefoot walk with Microsoft's CFO, and dropped the idea that Apple needed Microsoft to lose, since together they were the whole desktop market. Lesson: false competitive frames can kill a company (S008, S011, S042).
10. **The Lisa bet and the Mac.** Apple put its best people on Lisa for three and a half years with no backup plan, and it was a mismatch for the company's culture and channels. The Mac was the course correction. Lesson: check the product against who actually buys from you (S005, S027, S041).
11. **Ten years and $50 million on Pixar.** He funded Pixar for about a decade before Toy Story and the IPO, and later said they should have funded only animation from the start; he pulled 25 animators off commercials so the studio could own its work. Lesson: the biggest creative bets need patience, and side businesses dilute them (S001, S032, S066).
12. **The iPhone enclosure reset.** Weeks before launch he could not love the design, told the team to throw out a year of work, and they agreed. Lesson: a last-minute reset is what separates great from good (S012).
13. **Killing the iPod mini with the nano (2005).** Apple replaced its best-selling iPod with a smaller one that kept the same promise, and pulled it from the watch pocket of his jeans on stage. Lesson: replace your own hit before a rival does (S054).
14. **Removing the fan from the iMac.** He insisted on no fan, which meant re-engineering power and heat through the whole machine. Lesson: real design is core engineering, not decoration (S071, S090).
15. **The hacksaw iMac rejected (2002).** Apple could have glued a flat panel to the old iMac; that would have ruined the screen and the drive, so it spent two years on a new design instead. Lesson: the obvious shortcut usually ruins both parts (S049).
16. **Desktop publishing and desktop video surprises.** Nobody planned desktop publishing for the Mac; within a year Apple moved its whole strategy there. Later no user ever asked to edit movies, and iMovie became the thing they loved. Lesson: watch for the use you did not plan, and build what customers cannot yet ask for (S027, S090).
17. **MobileMe to iCloud.** A cloud service launched badly; Apple scrapped it and rebuilt it from scratch. Lesson: when the concept fails, rebuild, do not patch (S065).
18. **The Intel switch, prepared for five years.** Every Mac OS X release had been compiled for Intel in secret, so the switch was clean and the line moved inside a year. Lesson: buy optionality before you need it (S053, S055, S056).
19. **Attrition from 33 to 15 percent.** On his return a third of staff left each year and many in upper management were burnt out. He removed them, repriced options, and ten months later attrition was below the Valley average. Lesson: people stay when they can see the company matter again (S011, S023, S040, S045).
20. **Paul Rand's one logo.** Asked for options for the NeXT logo, Rand said he would solve the problem once and Jobs could take it or leave it. Jobs found that clarity refreshing. Lesson: a real professional sets the terms (S033).

Also reused, single source: the LaserWriter's first page that sold itself at a glance (S004); OpenDoc cut despite real effort because it did not fit (S004); Avi Tevanian winning a year-long fight over the OS X password prompt (S028); the Apple II floppy that brought VisiCalc (S010); lunches with Noyce and Grove in his twenties and a cold call to Bill Hewlett at 13 (S001, S092); engineers sent to customers' homes during the iPhone 4 antenna problem (S083).
