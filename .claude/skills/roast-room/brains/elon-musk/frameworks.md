# Frameworks: Elon Musk

Built from the 94 usable sources in `sources.md` (long interviews and podcasts, Starbase tours, Starship updates, TED talks, university lectures, Tesla master plans, Battery Day, Investor Day, shareholder meetings and earnings calls, and SpaceX presentations, 2003 to 2026). S041 and S068 are listed there but not used. 25 frameworks: engineering and execution first (1 to 17), then management and company building (18 to 25). Within each group, roughly most-used first, except the five-step algorithm, which leads because most of his other execution tools are pieces of it. ## Engineering and execution

## 1. The algorithm (the five-step process)
- **What it is:** His ordered method for any part, process, or plan. He calls it something he wants every team to "implement rigorously" (S014), and elsewhere calls it his first principles algorithm (S052). The order is the point: most waste comes from doing the steps backwards.
- **Steps:** (1) Make the requirements less dumb, and put a named person on each one. (2) Delete the part or process step. (3) Simplify or optimize, and only now. (4) Accelerate cycle time. (5) Automate, last.
- **When to use:** Any plan that is slow, costly, or complicated. Before a founder hires for a process, buys software for it, or automates it.
- **How to score:** 5 if requirements have owners, steps were deleted, and about 10% of deletions had to come back; 3 if the plan simplifies but deletes nothing; 1 if it speeds up or automates a process nobody questioned (his own Model 3 line mistake, S014, S052).
- **Quote:** "your requirements are definitely dumb. It does not matter who gave them to you." (S014) The deletion threshold: "If you're not adding back at least 10% of the things you're deleting, you're not deleting enough." (S093)
- **His own misuse:** he says he has gone backwards through all five steps more than once, automating first and then undoing it (S014, S052, S093).
- **Sources:** taught in full in 3 (S014, S052, S093); described by a Tesla executive as his method in S069; its steps (question, delete, simplify before speeding up, automate last) are applied in 9 more (S010, S011, S012, S053, S066, S067, S078, S084, S095). Total 13.

## 2. Tackle the limiting factor *(his phrase)*
- **What it is:** Find the one thing capping speed right now, solve it, then ask again. Money, people, and his own time follow the bottleneck; a project that is going well gets almost none of his attention.
- **Parts:** Pick a point in time; name the single binding input at that time (in 2026, power for AI in about a year and chips in three to four years, S002, S038, S061); if nobody else will fix it, fix it yourself (the lithium refinery, S031); predict the next bottleneck before you hit it (silicon, then transformers, then electricity, S001); repeat. In a crisis the whole company swarms the one thing that will kill it first (S056).
- **When to use:** Any plan to go faster, a growth stall, or a question about where founder time and capital should go.
- **How to score:** High if the plan names one binding constraint for a specific date and acts on it directly. Low if it works on five things at once, or on what is interesting instead of what caps speed.
- **Quote:** "Speed is important. I'm generally going to do the thing that... I just repeatedly tackle the limiting factor." (S002) And at Tesla: "we're we're going to address whatever we think the limiting factor is at any point in time" (S069)
- **Sources:** 16 (S001, S002, S009, S010, S027, S029, S030, S031, S034, S038, S046, S056, S061, S069, S079, S083)

## 3. The best part is no part (delete the part, delete the process)
- **What it is:** The cheapest and most reliable part, step, or rule is the one that is gone. Anything not needed to solve the problem in front of you now is deleted or deferred.
- **Parts:** Ask whether the item is needed to solve the current problem (orbit first, doors later, S009); use the ground or an existing system instead of adding one (the tower arms catch the booster so the legs go, S010, S094; ullage gas replaces separate thrusters, S095); merge many pieces into one (171 metal pieces into 2 castings, S072; the Model Y rear casting cut the body shop by 30%, S053); remove flanges, seals, and shrouds (S011, S067, S093); score code by what you remove (S053); delete whole structures, not pieces, or they grow back (S050); fix problems at the factory so service is not needed (S084).
- **When to use:** Feature lists, product scope, process steps, approval chains, headcount plans, and recurring costs.
- **How to score:** High if the plan lists what was removed and something had to come back. Low if every item survives on an in-case argument.
- **Quote:** "the best part is no part the best process is no process it weighs nothing costs nothing can't go wrong." (S066) On cost work: "obviously, the best is you delete some parts." (S084)
- **Sources:** 16 (S009, S010, S011, S012, S014, S050, S053, S059, S066, S067, S072, S084, S093, S094, S095, S052)

## 4. Prototypes are easy, production is hard (the machine that builds the machine)
- **What it is:** Designing the product is the small job. Designing the system that makes it at volume is 10 to 100 times harder, so the factory is the real product. He calls this the biggest epiphany of 2016 (S022) and has repeated it in nearly every Tesla and SpaceX setting since.
- **Parts:** Design the product and the factory together from the first sheet of paper (who makes each part, where, how it ships, how it goes in, S084); design for manufacturing from day one (S045, S083); version the factory like a product (S040); then the third stage is harder still: production at scale with positive cash flow (S031), and taking 20% of cost out after that (S071).
- **When to use:** Any physical product, and any plan that counts a prototype, demo, pilot, or launch event as the win. For services, the same test applies to the delivery system (inferred from S014, S024, S071).
- **How to score:** High if the plan has a delivery system with a cycle time and a cost per unit at volume. Low if success is defined as a working prototype, a press release, or a first customer.
- **Quote:** "basically the prototype is somewhere is at best 10% of the difficulty and probably closer to 1%." (S070) And: "prototypes are easy manufacturing is hard um and then and then manufacturing at scale with positive cash flow is excruciating" (S031)
- **Sources:** 19 (S006, S008, S014, S022, S024, S031, S040, S045, S053, S054, S056, S063, S070, S071, S080, S083, S084, S052, S082)

## 5. First principles and the raw-material floor (the magic wand number, the million-units test)
- **What it is:** Boil a problem down to what you are most sure is true, then reason up instead of copying what others did. For cost, add up the raw materials at market price: that is the floor, and everything above it is how you put the atoms into shape. If something is still expensive at a million units a year, the design is the problem, not the volume.
- **Steps:** Check the idea does not break physics; list the materials and their spot price (batteries at about $80/kWh of metals against $600/kWh packs, S003; rocket materials at 1 to 2% of rocket cost, S021, S064); ask why the gap is so large; imagine the ideal arrangement of atoms and find the tools to get there, not the reverse (S006); scale to the limit (a million units, the most efficient possible architecture, S006, S094) to find what really constrains it.
- **When to use:** Pricing, unit economics, build-versus-buy, and any claim that "this is just what it costs" or "that's how the industry does it."
- **How to score:** High if the plan knows its cost floor and has a path toward it at volume. Low if the cost is taken from the industry average or from what the last company paid.
- **Quote:** "let's boil something down to the most fundamental principles, the things that we are most confident are true at a foundational level, and that sets your axiomatic base, and then you reason up from there." (S006) The floor: "you could wave a magic wand and rearrange the atoms into the final shape, that would be the lowest possible cost that you could make this thing for" (S006) The test: "If it's too expensive at a million units a year, then volume is not the reason why your thing is expensive." (S006)
- **Sources:** 16 (S003, S006, S016, S021, S028, S040, S042, S048, S049, S051, S056, S064, S071, S079, S083, S094)

## 6. The S-curve and the least lucky part *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Output of anything new follows an S-curve: agonizingly slow, then exponential, then linear, then flat. The early slope is set by the worst of about 10,000 parts and process steps, so a product with an all-new supply chain has a long, flat start. People misread both ends: they extrapolate the slow start in a straight line and lose patience.
- **Parts:** Count the new items in the supply chain; expect the line to move at the pace of the least lucky one (S035, S038, S042, S054, S086, S087); measure the ramp as the rate at which you clear the pile of small problems (S072); predict the endpoint with more confidence than the next quarter (S033, S086); never extrapolate the early ramp linearly (S064, S080).
- **When to use:** Ramp plans, hiring plans tied to volume, revenue forecasts for a new product, and any board asking why month three is behind.
- **How to score:** High if the forecast has a flat start sized to the number of new parts and names the slowest item. Low if it assumes a straight line from day one or treats a slow start as proof the idea failed.
- **Quote:** "It'll move as fast as the least lucky, slowest, dumbest part in the entire 10,000." (S087) And: "The output per unit time always follows an S-curve." (S002)
- **Sources:** 14 (S002, S033, S035, S038, S042, S054, S064, S072, S075, S080, S086, S087, S088, S083)

## 7. Iteration rate is the moat (a high production rate solves many ills)
- **What it is:** Advantage equals how many iterations you get times how much each one improves. A high production rate makes each test unit cheap, so you can try more things and lose a unit without losing the program. Any new technology needs about three major versions before it is excellent.
- **Parts:** Count iterations per year and progress per iteration (S076, S093); raise the build rate so a lost unit is a data point (S067); keep the whole loop close together so iterations are fast (masks, logic, memory, and packaging under one roof, S076, S087); render the product in successive passes, blurry first (S011); plan on three major versions (S015, S037); ship on a fixed cadence instead of holding for perfect (the annual chip cycle, S032; weekly design reviews, S003).
- **When to use:** Product roadmaps, R&D budgets, competitive analysis, and any plan that bets on getting one big launch right.
- **How to score:** High if the plan raises iterations per year and treats early versions as learning. Low if it has one big release, slow feedback, or expects version one to be the product.
- **Quote:** "How many iterations do you have? And what's your time and progress between iterations?" (S093) And: "what matters is not Innovation but the rate of innovation" (S071)
- **Sources:** 13 (S003, S011, S015, S032, S037, S045, S051, S067, S071, S076, S087, S093, S040)

## 8. Same asset, more hours (full and rapid reuse, utilization) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** An expensive asset used once, or used a few hours a week, is the cost problem. Make it fully and rapidly reusable, or keep it busy, and the cost per use collapses. Partial or slow reuse does not change the economics.
- **Parts:** Reuse must be rapid and complete: only propellant and scheduled maintenance change between flights (S025, S079); full reuse cuts the cost of access to space by about 100 times (S012, S061); a car used 10 to 12 hours a week becomes about five times more useful if it drives itself 50 to 60 hours (S029, S040, S052); then design for the new duty cycle, built like a commercial truck (S038); judge by fully considered cost per mile, not sticker price (S084).
- **When to use:** Capital equipment, fleets, software infrastructure, staff utilization, and any business model where the main cost is an asset that sits idle.
- **How to score:** High if the plan raises use per asset and prices on cost per use. Low if it adds assets to grow, or counts partial reuse as solved.
- **Quote:** "reusability is only relevant if it is rapid and complete" (S025) And: "the cost of access to space will drop by a factor of a hundred when you achieve full reusability." (S061)
- **Sources:** 18 (S010, S012, S018, S025, S029, S036, S038, S040, S049, S052, S056, S061, S063, S067, S079, S084, S089, S090)

## 9. One metric that shows real capability *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Pick the one number that measures what the company is actually for, and make the whole organization chase it. Reject counts that flatter (launches, press releases, raw engagement).
- **Examples:** cost per ton to orbit (S014, S095); payload mass to orbit per year, not launch count, which is comparing a dinghy to a supertanker (S010); tonnage of batteries (S024); unregretted user time at X (S046, S058); years by which Tesla sped up sustainable energy (S057, S073); terawatt scale or you are not moving the needle (S084); intelligence per gigabyte (S038, S051, S086); progress measured by the timeline to a self-sustaining Mars city (S037).
- **When to use:** Setting company goals, board dashboards, comp plans, and any debate about whether the company is winning.
- **How to score:** High if the plan has one physical or customer-value metric tied to the mission. Low if it tracks vanity counts, or so many metrics that none decides anything.
- **Quote:** "it's not about press releases it's about tonnage" (S024) And: "what is your fully considered cost per ton to orbit" (S095)
- **Sources:** 13 (S010, S014, S024, S037, S038, S046, S051, S057, S058, S073, S084, S086, S095)

## 10. Rapid iteration with a risk dial *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Set how much failure is allowed by what a failure costs. Uncrewed test programs should expect to blow up; anything with people or customers on it sits at the opposite end. Also look for the asymmetry that freezes an organization: big punishment for a failed change, small reward for a good one.
- **Parts:** Place the program on the dial (Dragon at the conservative end, Starship at the iterative end, S009); in test, explore the corners of the box and worst-case every parameter (S012); treat test flights as data (S011); protect what is hardest to replace, such as the launch pad (S009); when people or the public are exposed, go slow and widen in steps (toe, then foot, then leg, S083), and never harm anyone (S018, S088); do not scale while a better, safer version is already in the pipeline (S087).
- **When to use:** Launch plans, beta programs, pricing tests, regulated products, and any team that is afraid to change anything.
- **How to score:** High if risk tolerance matches who gets hurt, and failures in test are cheap and fast. Low if a safe experiment is treated like a crewed flight, or a customer-facing system is treated like a test article.
- **Quote:** "we expect it to explode um it's weird if it doesn't explode frankly" (S008) The freeze: "big punishment for, if you make a change and something goes wrong, big punishment. If you make a change and it goes right, small reward." (S009)
- **Sources:** 11 (S008, S009, S011, S012, S018, S083, S086, S087, S088, S094, S033)

## 11. Make it work, then make it good (crawl, walk, run) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Get one way that works before you look for the best way. Robustness before performance; flexible setups before fixed automation; a rough version flying before a polished one on paper.
- **Parts:** Find one working path (S011); crawl, walk, run: make anything at all, then something useful, then scale (the Terafab, S018); tune for robustness first (Raptor 2, S093); defer known flaws that do not block the current goal (the forward flaps, S095); use tents and temporary lines until the design is stable (S011, S066); accept high unit cost on the first version (S039).
- **When to use:** New products, MVPs, new markets, and any team stuck choosing the optimal design before anything works.
- **How to score:** High if the plan ships the simplest version that works and lists what it will optimize later. Low if it optimizes, automates, or builds permanent infrastructure before one version works.
- **Quote:** "The first order of business is to get one way at all That works... Thereafter to optimize." (S011)
- **Sources:** 7 (S011, S018, S039, S066, S093, S095, S009)

## 12. Aggressive timelines, and a long schedule means a wrong design
- **What it is:** Work expands to fill the time it is given, so set the most aggressive internal date you can, about a 50th percentile date. Then use the schedule as a test of the design: if the build takes a long time, the design is too complicated.
- **Parts:** Set an internal date with about even odds (S002, S074); keep internal targets tighter than public ones (S025); when the schedule is long, redesign rather than add time (S066, S078); feed schedule results back into the design loop (S066); know the bias (below).
- **When to use:** Project plans, launch dates, and hiring plans tied to dates.
- **How to score:** High if the plan has a hard internal date with a stated chance of hitting it and a design simple enough to build fast. Low if the date was padded, or if a slow build is accepted as normal.
- **Quote:** "we want to set the most aggressive timeline that we can. Because there's sort of like a law of gaseous expansion where, for schedules, where whatever time you set, it's not going to be less than that." (S027) The test: "if the schedule is long the design is wrong" (S078)
- **His known bias:** he admits it often: "I do have a habit of being optimistic with schedules." (S009) "I guess I have somewhat of a pathological optimism" (S029). Changed over time: in 2016 to 2019 he gave public dates for full self-driving that did not happen (S045, S042); from 2024 on he calls his dates internal estimates, says he has been "overly optimistic in the past." (S085), and in 2026 calls the Optimus ramp "literally impossible to predict" (S087). The newer view is the hedged one.
- **Sources:** 15 (S002, S009, S015, S025, S027, S029, S042, S045, S066, S074, S077, S078, S084, S085, S087)

## 13. Is success in the set of possible outcomes? (think in probabilities) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Before starting, check that success is possible at all; it does not need to be likely. Give every plan a probability, not a certainty. Accept the odds up front, and take drastic action only when success is no longer possible.
- **Parts:** Ask whether success is in the set of outcomes (S064, S067); put an honest number on it (he gave SpaceX and Tesla under 10% at the start, S047, S077, S079); decide whether the goal is important enough to do anyway (S091); review: as long as success is possible, keep going; when it is not, change the plan drastically (S002); treat the future as a branching set of probabilities your actions shift (S025, S045).
- **When to use:** Bets that could fail, moonshots, and keep-or-kill decisions on a struggling project.
- **How to score:** High if the plan states its odds and why the bet is still worth it. Low if it claims certainty, or keeps going after success has left the set.
- **Quote:** "when embarking on an endeavor success should be at least one of the possible outcomes" (S067) And: "I'll take drastic action only when I conclude that success is not in a set of possible outcomes." (S002)
- **Sources:** 15 (S002, S006, S010, S011, S021, S025, S028, S045, S047, S064, S067, S077, S079, S082, S091)

## 14. Decompose the impossible (and run the pieces in parallel) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** When told something will take years or is impossible, break it into its physical requirements, attack each one at once, and run every dependency in parallel. SpaceX's stated specialty is turning impossible into late.
- **Parts:** List the irreducible needs (for the Memphis cluster: building, power, cooling, networking, then 24/7 work in shifts, which built 100,000 H100s in about six months instead of 18 to 24, S021); do not serialize anything with a gestation period (S059); if the box has you in checkmate, invent a new piece (the Model 3 tent line, S076; building Starship outdoors because buildings would take too long, S066); change all factories over at once, not one by one (S033).
- **When to use:** Deadlines that others call impossible, build-outs, launches with many dependencies.
- **How to score:** High if the plan lists the physical needs, owners for each, and runs them in parallel. Low if it accepts the industry timeline, or runs steps one after another that could overlap.
- **Quote:** "At SpaceX we specialize in converting things from impossible too late." (S010) And: "try not to serialize uh dependencies so if you can put as many elements in parallel as possible" (S059)
- **Sources:** 6 (S010, S021, S033, S059, S066, S076)

## 15. Build it yourself when the supply chain is too slow *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** He integrates from need, not belief. When suppliers cannot move as fast as you need, give you their weakest team, or do not exist, bring the work in house. If you inherit their supply chain you inherit their speed and cost.
- **Parts:** Ask whether the supplier can meet your pace even in the best case (S032); if not, build it (motors, gearboxes, and electronics for Optimus from physics first principles, S016, S083; the lithium refinery, S031; the Terafab, S038, S087); where there is no supply chain at all, build all of it (S088); otherwise buy everything suppliers can make (S073); keep production close to engineering so defects show up fast (S056); de-risk critical parts at home even if imports are cheaper (S076).
- **When to use:** Build versus buy, outsourcing, key vendors, and any plan whose speed is capped by a partner.
- **How to score:** High if the plan names the supplier limit and either fixes it or brings it in house with a clear payoff. Low if it integrates for pride, or waits on a vendor that is the limiting factor.
- **Quote:** "not necessarily because we think that there's some religious reason to be a product integrator but because uh the pace that we needed to move was just much faster than the supply chain could move" (S017) In one podcast reply he sums it up as integrate or die; the captions do not mark the speaker, so this is inferred from S056, not a quote.
- **Sources:** 14 (S016, S017, S031, S032, S038, S052, S056, S070, S073, S076, S083, S087, S088, S085)

## 16. The factory is like a chip, and cost is a game of pennies *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Treat the factory like a chip: put stations closer, make them smaller, and raise the clock speed. A factory that runs twice as fast is worth two factories. Then take cost out across all of roughly 10,000 parts, a few cents each, starting with deleting parts. There is no single silver bullet.
- **Parts:** Measure what fraction of the factory volume is doing useful work, not conveyance (2 to 3% today, aiming for 30%, S070); cut cycle time toward consumer electronics speeds (S032); follow the journey of the atom and fix steps that make no sense (S051); give each station about the same work in linear flow (S011); to take $5,000 out of a $40,000 car, find 50 cents in each of 10,000 items (S084); expect hundreds of small improvements, not one breakthrough (S059).
- **When to use:** Operations, gross margin plans, cost-down programs, and capacity decisions.
- **How to score:** High if the plan targets throughput per unit of space and time and a cost per unit across every line item. Low if it adds a second factory to fix a slow first one, or looks for one big saving.
- **Quote:** "you think of a factory is like a chip" (S051) And: "there's really no one Silver Bullet uh that has has um been responsible for a substantial portion of of the cost savings it's been really hundreds of small Innovations and improvements" (S059)
- **Sources:** 10 (S011, S017, S032, S051, S052, S059, S070, S071, S072, S084)

## 17. Enter high, drive down, and know how you will pay for it *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** The 2006 Tesla plan: start at the high end where buyers pay a premium, use the money to fund the next, cheaper model, and repeat. Every stage must pay for the next one; a big project with no funding path does not happen.
- **Parts:** Sell an expensive first version (Roadster) to fund a cheaper second (S039); plow free cash flow into R&D to go down market (S039); size the big project so it also serves a business that pays now (the 2017 BFR, sized to replace Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy, and Dragon so all resources go to one system, S036); stack stepping stones where each layer earns revenue and funds the next (launch, then Starlink, then Moon and Mars, S017, S081); demand at the bottom is set by affordability, not desire (S069, S071).
- **When to use:** Go-to-market sequencing, pricing a first product, funding a long-range plan.
- **How to score:** High if each stage earns money and funds the next, and the price falls with volume. Low if the plan needs one huge raise to reach the first revenue.
- **Quote:** "The strategy of Tesla is to enter at the high end of the market, where customers are prepared to pay a premium, and then drive down market as fast as possible to higher unit volume and lower prices with each successive model." (S039)
- **Sources:** 7 (S017, S036, S039, S040, S069, S071, S081)

## Management and company building

## 18. Time goes to the limiting factor (go where the problem is) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** His own calendar is the limiting-factor rule applied to one person. He schedules himself, spends most of his time on engineering and the factory floor, and goes to whatever is breaking.
- **Parts:** Schedule your own time, because nobody else knows the priorities (S030); spend about one company per day to cut context switching (S030); 80 to 90% on engineering and design, not business operations (S079, S096); watch the end of the line, and if output stalls walk upstream to the blockage (S029); in a crisis, live on the floor (S024, S052); budget brain cycles across ventures and refuse new ones that would overload them (no planes, S007, S008).
- **When to use:** A founder's calendar, a CEO who manages by reports, and any plan that adds a new venture to a busy founder.
- **How to score:** High if the founder's time goes to the bottleneck and to where the work is done. Low if it goes to what is going well, to meetings far from the product, or to a new side project.
- **Quote:** "I go where the problem is essentially" (S083) And: "I actually allocate time according to where the limiting factor." (S002)
- **Sources:** 12 (S002, S007, S008, S024, S029, S030, S038, S052, S079, S083, S096, S067)

## 19. Everyone is chief engineer (the leader must know the product)
- **What it is:** Every engineer should understand the whole system well enough to see when their own optimization is pointless. The person in charge must know the technology; engineering must sit next to production, not in an ivory tower.
- **Parts:** Every requirement and design choice traced to a named person (S014, S052); engineers understand the system, not only their part (S014); the CEO of a technical company can do the technical work (S015, S054, S063); engineering offices beside the line (S055); if no one good will join, the founder is chief engineer by default (S036); the CEO gets a distillation of the company's worst problems, so must be able to solve them (S064).
- **When to use:** Hiring a CEO or head of product, org design, and any founder planning to step away from the product.
- **How to score:** High if the decision makers understand the product and sit near where it is built. Low if decisions run through managers who cannot explain how the product works.
- **Quote:** "you really want everyone to be chief engineer" (S014) And: "if you if you're the Cavalry Captain you should know how to ride a horse" (S015)
- **Sources:** 9 (S014, S015, S036, S052, S054, S055, S063, S064, S083)

## 20. The org chart shows up in the product *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Structural errors in the organization appear physically in the product: extra brackets, redundant enclosures, mismatched materials, and tolerance stack-ups at team boundaries. To remove the pointless part, find the boundary that created it and dissolve it.
- **Parts:** Look for duplicated parts and seams in the product (S053, S056); trace each to the team boundary (S056); merge teams or parts (castings, S053, S072); fewer parts means fewer tolerances to stack (S053).
- **When to use:** Product bloat, integration bugs, handoff failures, and reorganizations.
- **How to score:** High if the plan fixes the org boundary behind a product defect. Low if it patches the product and leaves the boundary.
- **Quote:** "you can generally see the the errors the organizational structure errors they manifest themselves in the product" (S053)
- **Sources:** 3 (S053, S056, S072)

## 21. Hire for evidence of exceptional ability *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Hire on talent, drive, trustworthiness, and goodness of heart, judged in conversation, not on credentials. Ask about the hardest problem they solved: people who really solved it know the details.
- **Parts:** Ignore the resume and trust the interaction (S002); ask for the story of their life and their hardest problem, and probe one level down (S049); weight character as much as intellect, a mistake he says he made (S002, S063); promote on skills, talent, and integrity only (S062, S092); the best idea wins regardless of who proposed it (S059, S063); watch where the smartest engineers want to work, because that company wins (S031, S072); degrees are not evidence (S078).
- **When to use:** Hiring, promotions, choosing cofounders, and judging a competitor's strength.
- **How to score:** High if the plan tests for proof of exceptional work and character. Low if it hires on pedigree, title, or a polished pitch.
- **Quote:** "don't look at the resume. Just believe your interaction." (S002) And: "the people that really solved the problem, they know exactly how they solved it. They know the little details." (S049)
- **Sources:** 10 (S002, S031, S049, S059, S062, S063, S072, S078, S092, S036)

## 22. Incentives explain behavior *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** To predict or explain what a person, company, or government does, map its incentives. Whatever you reward will happen, so design the incentive before blaming the people.
- **Parts:** Map who is rewarded for what (portfolio managers judged yearly push for short-term earnings, S013; Twitter's old bonuses tied to user growth left bots alone, S046); look for big punishment and small reward for change (S009); for contracts, use fixed milestones and at least two competing vendors, not sole-source cost-plus (S080); no conspiracy is needed when the incentive is plain (S044, S062).
- **When to use:** Comp plans, sales commissions, partner and vendor deals, and diagnosing why a team behaves badly.
- **How to score:** High if the plan's incentives point at the one metric that matters. Low if it pays for activity, growth at any cost, or cost-plus effort.
- **Quote:** "it's economics 101 whatever you whatever you incent will happen" (S080) And: "when you when you understand the incentives, then then you understand the behavior" (S012)
- **Sources:** 7 (S009, S012, S013, S044, S046, S062, S080)

## 23. Garbage collection for rules *(his phrase)*
- **What it is:** Rules are like lines of code for running an organization or a country. They get added every year and almost never removed, until everything is slow or illegal. Rules need an active removal process, and it should be easier to remove one than to add one.
- **Parts:** Find rules no named person will defend (inferred from S014, S015); delete whole structures, not parts, or they regrow like weeds (S050); give rules a sunset and a lower bar to repeal than to pass (S027, S045, S056); keep a few referees, not more referees than players (S026, S044, S057).
- **When to use:** Policies, approval chains, compliance processes, and internal rules at a growing company. Most of his sources apply it to government; the business use is inferred.
- **How to score:** High if the plan removes rules as it adds them and each rule has an owner. Low if process only grows.
- **Quote:** "It's easy to add rules but we don't actually have a process for getting rid of them that's the issue there's no garbage collection" (S015)
- **Sources:** 13 (S006, S015, S026, S027, S044, S045, S048, S050, S056, S057, S080, S091, S014)

## 24. Close the loop on reality (be less wrong) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Assume you are wrong to some degree and try to be less wrong over time. Seek negative feedback on purpose, because people who like you will not offer it, and keep ego small so the loop to reality does not break.
- **Parts:** Ask what is wrong, not what is good, and overweight it (S003); ask friends for criticism explicitly (S049); invite critique of your own analysis (S031); let outsiders or competitors grade the work (S018); go to the source material, not opinion (S044); do not jump to conclusions before the data is analyzed (S094); keep the ego-to-ability ratio low (S021).
- **When to use:** Product reviews, post-mortems, strategy reviews, and any founder surrounded by agreement.
- **How to score:** High if the plan has a real channel for bad news and has changed course because of it. Low if feedback is collected only from fans or the team grades its own work.
- **Quote:** "always take the position that you are to some degree wrong and your goal is to be less wrong over time" (S049) And: "if you if your ego to ability ratio is gets too high then you're you're you're going to basically break the feedback loop to reality" (S021)
- **Sources:** 10 (S003, S007, S018, S021, S031, S044, S049, S065, S094, S048)

## 25. Control, cash, and a long horizon (and utility times people) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Long bets need protection from short feedback loops: enough cash to survive irrational markets, and enough voting control not to be removed before the work is done. Pick the work by how much better it makes things times how many people it reaches, and let money follow.
- **Parts:** Get to positive cash flow as soon as you can and keep reserves (S017); when runway is months, cut immediately (Twitter at $3 billion negative cash flow with $1 billion in the bank, S046); stay private when the horizon is longer than public markets tolerate (S008); hold enough votes not to be ousted by activists, about 25% (S019, S026); do not take money off the table while investors are at risk, and keep skin in the game (S057, S091); choose work by utility delta times people affected (S021, S022); a company exists to make useful products and services (S017, S057).
- **When to use:** Fundraising, dilution, going public, selling secondary shares, and choosing what to build next.
- **How to score:** High if the plan protects cash and control in line with its horizon and aims at a large useful change. Low if it trades control for a valuation, pulls money out early, or chases money directly.
- **Quote:** "sufficient voting control such that um I cannot be ousted by activist investors is what matters to me." (S026) And: "just uh make what's your cash flow and get the cop positive cash flow soon as you can" (S017)
