# Stories: Andrew Ng

20 stories and cases he reuses, with the lesson he draws, built from thirteen batches of verified notes on 125 usable sources (about 577,000 words, 2013 to 2026). Most-told first. Source ids in parentheses.

1. **Google Brain's first customer.** Friends, Yoshua Bengio among them, told him scaling up neural networks was a bad career move. He pitched Larry Page over dinner without his slides, then picked speech recognition, not ads, as the first project because it could win and its team had a clear benchmark. Maps came next, then advertising. Lesson: make the controversial bet, but sequence early projects to earn credibility and allies (S054, S055, S061, S072, S074, S075, S079).
2. **Coursera.** He spent years on small online-learning experiments as a side project, unsure he was even allowed to, with many bad ideas along the way, before the format took off. He filmed courses late at night because 100,000 people had signed up before a video existed. One feature that let two learners share a browser got zero use. Lesson: what looks like overnight success is years of iteration, and clever features still need testing (S001, S019, S047, S074, S090, S097, S101, S126).
3. **Non-engineers who build.** At AI Fund the CFO scripted deck and document reviews that saved hours a week, the general counsel automated NDAs, a recruiter built a resume screener, a marketer built a feedback app in two days, and the receptionist went from web scrapers to CRM code. Lesson: people who build with AI move faster and get better data, whatever their title (S010, S011, S049, S076, S081, S105).
4. **Bearing AI and the ships.** Mitsui brought a ship-routing idea he says he "would never have come up with myself" (S008). AI Fund validated it for a month, recruited a CEO, and built the company. He has put the fuel saving at about $450,000 per ship per year (S008, 2023) and later at about $500,000 across 600 plus ships (S051, 2025). Lesson: domain experts plus AI people build what neither could alone (S008, S020, S051, S073).
5. **GPT-3.5 beats GPT-4.** On a coding benchmark the older model wrapped in an agentic loop scored far above the newer model used zero-shot (95.1% against 67.0% in his letter). Lesson: a better workflow can matter more than a better model, so do not wait for the next model (S002, S018, S076, S124).
6. **Steel, pills, and the loose screw.** On steel defects, teams spent weeks on new architectures and gained nothing over a 76.2% baseline, while fixing the data reached 93.1% in two weeks. A factory inspector labeled the same part a defect in the morning and fine in the afternoon. Pill defects improved sharply once labels used a consistent scratch-length cutoff. Elsewhere a senior engineer told a team to stop retraining and tighten the camera screw. Lesson: when the model is good enough, fix the data, the labeling rules, and the physical setup before collecting more (S023, S024, S095, S119).
7. **The thirty-second decision.** Renate Nyborg, former Tinder CEO, settled a major architecture question in thirty seconds on a call; they later built Amorai together, pairing her knowledge of relationships with AI Fund's AI depth. Lesson: most people have never seen real startup speed, and rare expertise pairings face little competition (S008, S020, S073).
8. **Months on the wrong part.** A team spent six months swapping web search engines when error analysis would have shown in minutes that page selection was the problem. Another company had a team of 30 stuck for three months until one colleague took the data home for a long weekend and beat them. Lesson: diagnose where errors come from before you spend, because skill in directing the work is worth more than headcount (S060, S066, S069, S070).
9. **The kids' game and the eighteen prototypes.** He built a game in about ten minutes, then spent an hour tracking down kids to test it. To find two ideas worth scaling you build about eighteen that go nowhere, and he builds dozens of throwaway prototypes on weekends. Lesson: feedback, not code, is now the slow step, and cheap failure is the price of finding winners (S004, S086).
10. **9:20 instead of after lunch.** A team member planned to wait until after lunch for feedback; he wanted it by 9:20 that morning, because the gain is a much faster loop, not three saved hours. An energy company CPO gave a team one week for dashboards they had estimated at three months, and they delivered. Lesson: demand AI-era cycle times, because teams will not set them on their own (S081, S084).
11. **The ten-minute loan and the five-minute grade.** A bank can automate one underwriting step and save an hour of staff time, which is small. Redesigning the flow to sell "approved in 10 minutes" drives growth. Likewise, AI grading that only replaces a human grader saves labor, but grades in five minutes instead of a week change how a course can teach. Lesson: value comes from redesigning the workflow, not speeding up one step (S007, S088).
12. **The hundred-million-dollar dataset.** A CEO spent over $100 million buying a company for its data, then asked him how to make money from it; the data was not what was needed. Lesson: you cannot know what data is worth until you train on it, so do not buy data on faith (S061, S065).
13. **Picking the use case.** A financial firm sent him 300 AI ideas to rank. An agriculture company expected to automate field work, but the real win was moving harvested goods. His team found 10% of contact center calls were refund requests and routed them automatically. Lesson: break jobs into tasks and select hard; the best target is rarely the obvious one (S007, S057).
14. **Lensa, the flashlight app.** A popular photo app whose revenue spiked and collapsed, which he called a thin layer on someone else's API, against Uber's lasting value. Lesson: thin wrappers are fads, not lasting businesses (S008, S073).
15. **Blue River's cabbages.** Stanford students took phone pictures of cabbages, built a small dataset, shipped a product farmers accepted, and grew the largest crop-image dataset through deployment before John Deere bought them. Lesson: start scrappy with data, then let deployment build the data asset (S098).
16. **Tariffs and solar farms.** After tariffs came up in a presidential debate, his team built an agentic workflow for tariff documents that became Gaia Dynamics. AES shared solar farm maintenance pains and AI Fund built around them. Lesson: unglamorous domain pain plus fast AI building turns into products few others chase (S049, S076).
17. **Two headcount requests.** In one week one person asked to hire a human and another asked for budget to use AI. He said no to the first and yes to the second. Lesson: knowing when AI can do the job is now a core management skill (S012).
18. **Feature three.** He preferred the first of four product ideas; a user survey picked the third. He spent hours working out why his judgment was off, to update his intuition, not just to ship feature three. Lesson: use data to train your gut, then decide fast from it (S086).
19. **Self-driving's broken promises.** A non-technical CEO once asked for a self-driving car from six engineers in one year. He says the self-driving industry, which he was part of, set poor expectations on timelines. Lesson: leaders need enough technical judgment to know what is feasible, and AI products need expectations set and reset (S021, S041, S074).
20. **Killing good businesses.** AI Fund has killed several projects with sound economics because the team did not want them to exist. Lesson: making money is not enough; the work has to leave people better off (S001, S008).
