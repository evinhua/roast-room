# Positions: Andrew Ng

40 topics, built from thirteen batches of verified notes on 125 usable sources (about 577,000 words: talks, lectures, interviews, The Batch letters, and his two books, 2013 to 2026). Each stance has a verbatim quote, source id, and date (upload date for talks, publish date for letters; "undated" where `sources.md` has no date, with the year added only when the title gives it). Where he changed his mind or shifted emphasis, the versions sit side by side with dates. Topics with no evidence say so.

## Where AI creates value in a business
- **Stance:** At the application layer, not in chips, clouds, or foundation models. The apps have to earn more than every layer below them, so that is where the money must be. Someone else has already paid for the models, which makes app work capital-efficient.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2023-12: "there has to be, mathematically, for this whole ecosystem to be successful at the applications" (S057)
  - 2025-07: "almost by definition, the biggest opportunities have to be at the application layer" (S001)
  - 2025-11: "I believe there is significant underinvestment in AI applications." (S035)
  - 2026-07: "it was the applications built on top of the internet that became even more valuable." (S013)
  - undated: "at the application layer the economics look very favorable to to people you know building and want to deploy stuff" (S094)
- **Read:** the most repeated view in the corpus (S001, S003, S008, S013, S018, S034, S035, S051, S057, S072, S073, S081, S089, S094). He compares models to power plants and apps to the far larger consumer electronics business (S072). He stays out of hardware: "I personally don't like to play there myself." (S008, 2023-08)

## Growth versus cost savings
- **Stance:** Use AI to grow, not just to cut cost. Treat it as a speed weapon. If a task gets far cheaper or faster, do much more of it, or sell the speed.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2025-03, on how companies respond when AI makes a task cheaper: "if you can make a task a thousand times cheaper let me do this task a thousand times more and this ends up being a recipe for Revenue growth" (S051)
  - 2025-05: "Growth is more interesting to most businesses than cost savings" (S029)
  - 2026: "I think of AI as very much not a cost-savings thing, not a productivity thing, but a massive speed advantage." (S084, AI Fund Frontier '26)
  - 2026-06: "growth has almost no practical ceiling" (S007)
  - 2026-10 (newest): "Far too many businesses are still thinking of AI as primarily a way to increase efficiency and save costs." (S127)
- **Read:** he says CEOs talk up cost savings because they can manage those expectations, while the bigger prize is new products (S092, S093). He also notes "higher productivity" lands badly with the public, who hear layoffs (S111).

## Point solutions versus workflow redesign
- **Stance:** Bolting AI onto one step gives small gains. The big wins come from redesigning the whole workflow so the product itself changes. A company plus AI is not an AI company.
- **Over time:**
  - 2019-09: "applications of AI can cannot be implemented as a SAS service where you swipe your credit card and then use it" (S059)
  - 2020 playbook and undated talk: "A taxi company plus a website was not an Internet company." (S075) The playbook makes the same point with a shopping mall plus a website (S061).
  - undated: "AI is automation on steroids." (S075) Companies underestimate the change management it needs (S075).
  - 2026-01: "running many experimental, bottom-up AI projects" ... "has failed to lead to significant payoffs." (S027)
  - 2026-07: "no company ever gain competitive advantage just by buying a CH GP or Microsoft co-pilot license. The problem is finding the right use cases." (S013)
  - 2026-10 (newest): keep funding bottom-up experiments, but add top-down backing to scale the best ones (S127).
- **Read:** consistent since 2019. In 2026 he names scattered bottom-up pilots as the common failure, then softens it to bottom-up plus top-down (inferred from S007, S013, S027, S127).

## Speed of execution
- **Stance:** Speed is the top predictor of startup success. Skilled teams are 10 times faster or more, and the gap compounds.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2023-10: "team that actually knows what they're doing will execute an AI project 10 times faster, you know, than a team that doesn't" (S020)
  - 2025-05: "the number one predictor of a startup success is speed" (S006)
  - Autumn 2025 lecture: "I've literally seen many teams in many well-known companies with good brands spend a year working on a project that I will see a more skilled team execute in a month." (S066)
  - 2026: "one of the best predictors of startup success is where you can execute at an insane velocity" (S084)
- **Read:** a team twice as fast is far ahead at every point in time, not just a few hours ahead (S068, Autumn 2018 lecture). He sees 10 to 100 times differences in execution speed among leaders (S072).

## Prototyping and testing ideas
- **Stance:** Build a crude version in days, look at how it fails, and expect most prototypes to die. Cut scope until you can build something in the time you have.
- **Evidence:**
  - undated book: "don't start off trying to design and build the perfect system. Instead build and train a basic system as quickly as possible" (S060)
  - 2021-09: when a shot is cheap, take many shots, Ready, Fire, Aim (S042, S126)
  - 2023-07: if building took six months, plan carefully; if it takes a day, just build it and see (S123)
  - 2025-06: "it's actually faster for me to just build a prototype in 3 or 4 hours in an afternoon rather than, you know, write a long memo" (S050)
  - 2025-07: "If you find yourself with only limited time to build, reduce the scope of your project until you can build something in whatever time you do have." (S104)
  - 2025-12: "the price of finding two great ideas to take to scale is you have to build 18 other things... that's okay." (S004)
- **Read:** he builds dozens of throwaway prototypes on weekends (S086). His exception: if you have built this kind of system many times, experience can justify more upfront investment (S065, S067).

## Concrete ideas versus vague markets
- **Stance:** Start from a concrete product idea an engineer could build today, even though it is more likely to be wrong. Vague ideas feel safe and kill speed.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2023-08: "I like to engage only when there's a concrete idea." (S008)
  - 2024-07: "Articulating a concrete idea" ... "which is more likely than a vague idea to be wrong" ... "takes more courage." (S030)
  - 2024-07: he disagrees with the design-thinking advice not to rush to a solution as the default start for AI projects (S030).
- **Read:** never brainstorm just one project, because the CEO's favorite is often the wrong one (S075).

## Focus versus diversification
- **Stance:** Bet on one hypothesis at a time, then switch fast when the data says no. Do not hedge across many half-bets.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2025-05: "Rather than hedging, it is often better to pursue one hypothesis (for example, build one concrete product) but also be willing to switch quickly to a different hypothesis." (S028)
  - 2025-07: "pick one go for it and if data tells you to lose faith in that idea that's actually totally fine. Just pivot on a dime" (S001)
- **Nuance:** at the idea stage he wants many options so no one idea gets forced to work because a senior person backed it (S031, 2024-07; S075).
- **Pivoting:** pivot on data, quickly and without drama. Concreteness is what makes the pivot fast. "rapid execution will let you discover the flaws sooner" (S030, 2024-07) If a prototype gets good feedback, scale it; if not, keep the lesson and move on (S105, 2025-06).

## Customer feedback and the bottleneck
- **Stance:** With AI coding, writing software is no longer the slow step. Getting user feedback and deciding what to build is. Get feedback in hours, not days, and use data to train your gut, not replace it.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2020-02: "ultimately we don't get to vote if we succeed or fail is only the customer" (S019)
  - 2025-07: gut, a few friends, strangers, 100 testers, then A/B tests, from fastest to slowest; a domain expert's gut "is actually surprisingly good" (S001)
  - 2025-07: "having this crude prototype to show friends helped me get user feedback that shaped my views on the product idea." (S104)
  - 2025-12: "the bottleneck is increasingly getting user feedback" (S004)
- **Read:** when a survey proved his feature pick wrong, he spent hours updating his intuition rather than just following the result (S086). For many small decisions, automate; for few critical ones, use a PM's mental model of the user (S103, 2025-07).

## Competition
- **Stance:** Watch customers, not rivals. The application layer, and boring B2B work in particular, is far less crowded than the model layer. Better frontier models will not wipe out deep applications.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2024-08: "I focus more on the application layer where I find that there's a very rich set of opportunities and is not nearly so hyper competitive" (S018)
  - 2025-08: "to be really decisive and move really fast you need to obsess usually about the customer" (S012)
  - 2025-11: "there also remains a huge set of valuable applications that the current trajectory of progress of frontier models won't displace for a long time." (S113)
  - undated: on logistics, tariffs, and wind farms, "we just work on it for a long time and and people don't seem interested in computing with us" (S076)

## Moats and defensibility
- **Over time:**
  - undated (older talks): "AI algorithms from a company point of view are for the most part not defensible there's so much open-source." (S099) Data is the asset; a search engine cannot compete without the query data (S099).
  - 2017-02: "at large companies, sometimes we'll launch products, not for the revenue, but for the data." (S021)
  - 2018: Blue River started with phone photos of cabbages and grew the largest crop-image dataset through deployment (S098).
  - 2023-08: thin wrappers will not last; Lensa "was a relatively thin software layer on top of someone else's really powerful APIs." (S008)
  - 2025-07 (newer): "I find that moes tend to be overhyped... more businesses tend to start off with a product and then evolve eventually into a moat." (S001)
- **Read:** he moved from data as the moat (2017 to 2018) to product first, moat later (2025). The thin-wrapper warning still stands (S008, S073, S113). In 2024 he repeated, with approval, an AI Fund Fellow's view that RAG builds a thicker layer than a bare wrapper (S033); the view is not originally his.

## Pricing and discounting
- **No recorded position** on how to price what you sell. Nearest: plan around falling input costs. "I'm confident that token prices will continue to fall rapidly." (S032, 2024-08) If an app is a bit too expensive to run today, ship it anyway and let prices catch up (S032).
- **Discounting: no recorded position** on offering discounts. As a buyer he refuses long vendor deals even when discounted: "I personally almost never signed longer than a one-year contract, regardless of the discounts offered, because I value that optionality" (S007, 2026-06)

## AI costs and margins
- **Stance:** Use the best model first and ignore cost until something works and the bill hurts. Then cut hard: smaller models, fine-tuning, or open weights.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2024-08: "I advise teams to focus on building a useful application rather than on optimizing LLM costs." (S032)
  - 2025-03: "use the latest best model just build something that is datable and only after you succeed at that and only if it turns out to be expensive then work on the cost optimization" (S052)
  - 2025-03: "the vast majority of applications my teams build do not use any fine-tuning at all." (S115)
  - 2025-11, on poor app-layer margins: "I think it still varies. Um I'm seeing a lot of green shoots of software applications that um were not that expensive to build" (S049)
- **Read:** at one startup he fine-tuned a smaller model to escape an inference bill that would have sunk the product at scale (S064). He compares today's AI apps to VC-subsidized food delivery: real value, but you must get through the subsidy era (S049).

## Model choice and vendor lock-in
- **Stance:** Keep switching costs low. Build evals so you can swap models when a better one ships. Prefer open, stable platforms, and keep your own data portable.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2025-07: "sometimes our engines will change it without even bothering to tell me because the eval show the new model works better." (S001)
  - 2025-11: some SaaS vendors charge heavily to extract your own data, to push you onto their agents; pick vendors that let you take it out (S112).
  - 2026-06: "I have no idea what would be the leading AI model a year from now" (S007)
  - 2026-06: "Platforms succeed when they are viewed as stable, reliable partners that one can build on." (S110)
- **Read:** forward deployed engineers from a vendor tie you to that vendor; he hires his own AI engineers instead to keep optionality (S106, 2026-05). He backs open-weight models so no gatekeeper controls AI the way iOS and Android control mobile (S076, S090).

## Build versus buy
- **Stance:** Buy what exists; build only what you want to own. Own your data and, long term, your AI team.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2020: outsourced partners can speed the start, but lasting advantage needs an in-house AI team (S061).
  - 2025-03: "if you can buy I often advise Partners you just buy it" (S051)
  - 2025-11: "if you buy SAS software um almost every SAS company will try to create a data silo in your company" (S054)
  - 2026-05: "while my organizations do hire FDEs, we hire far more AI Engineers!" (S106)

## Data readiness
- **Stance:** Do not wait to clean all your data before starting, and do not buy data on faith. Let projects pull the data work. Small, consistent, well-labeled data often beats big messy data.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2020: "I have tragically seen CEOs over-invest in collecting low-value data" (S061)
  - 2021-03: "the majority of machine learning problems actually have you know relatively smaller data sets" (S024)
  - undated: "the most important shift the ai world needs to go through in this decade will be a shift to data centric ai" (S095)
  - 2022-10: AI can work "just fine even on modest amounts of data, such as the data generated by a single pizza store." (S022)
  - 2025-11: "not to spend two years rebuilding data to be AI ready. That actually doesn't work that well" (S054)
  - 2025-11: "AI agents are getting better at looking at different types of data in businesses to spot patterns and create value. This is making data silos increasingly painful." (S112)
- **Read:** the value of a dataset is almost impossible to know until you train on it (S065). Collect a small set in a day or two, train, then decide what to collect next (S065, S066, S067, S068).

## Metrics, evals, and ROI
- **Stance:** Measure AI in business terms. Run evals and error analysis from day one; that discipline predicts progress better than tool choice. Start with rough evals and improve them like you improve models.
- **Evidence:**
  - undated book: "I've seen many engineers reluctant to carry out error analysis. It often feels more exciting to just jump in and implement some idea" (S060)
  - 2025-04: evals may measure only part of the task at first and shift from human judges to automated checks over time (S114).
  - 2025-10: "the single biggest predictor of how rapidly a team makes progress building an AI agent lay in their ability to drive a disciplined process for evals" (S036)
  - 2026-06: the best projects are glaringly obvious wins, not debates over 2% gains (S007)
  - 2026-08: "I find that the business outcome of AI is more function of the business than the function of the AI." (S011)
- **Read:** average accuracy is not enough; check rare cases and subgroups (S074). AI's consistency against noisy human judgment is an underused argument when pitching a project (S119, 2022-08).

## Where to start inside a company
- **Stance:** Start with a small, winnable project, get a success story, then go bigger. Not the most valuable project first, the one that can succeed.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2020-02: "the first step is actually to start small" (S019)
  - 2020 playbook: show concrete results within 6 to 12 months; a full transformation takes 2 to 3 years (S061)
  - 2023-08: his advice for generative AI matches his deep learning advice: "start small and build projects quickly to hone intuition about what's possible" (S120)
  - 2025-11: his first Google Brain project was speech, not ads, and "that let us have a success story, gain allies" (S054)
- **Read:** the playbook is written for companies worth $500M and up and may not fit small startups (S061).

## How large companies can move fast
- **Stance:** Size is not the constraint; process is. Give teams a sandbox: internal users or NDA testers, a separate brand, a fixed budget, and no sign-off chain.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2025-05: "in the age of AI, large corporations" ... "can move fast." (S102)
  - 2025-05: if launching an MVP needs sign-off from five VPs, the company cannot learn what customers want (S102).
  - undated: an energy company CPO cut a three-month dashboard estimate to one week, and the team delivered (S081).

## Proof of concept versus production
- **Stance:** Cut corners in a POC to learn fast; harden only for production. Never mix the two up.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2021-01: "if it buys you speed, it is okay to hard-code parameters" (S046)
  - 2021-03: "when you're deploying production you should celebrate that congratulations and you may be halfway done" (S024)
  - 2026-04: on coding agents, "I rarely trust them for critical infra decisions." (S108)
  - 2026-07: for 0-to-1 prototypes, move fast and throw the code away; for large systems, architect in advance (S109).
  - 2026-09: "It is not helpful to over-design at the early stages or under-design a mature product." (S039)
- **Read:** once a sandbox prototype shows promise, invest in reliability, security, and brand before scaling (S102). He cites an agent that wiped a test app's database to show why production needs more care (S064).

## Raising money
- **No recorded position** on how much or when to raise. Nearest: AI Fund validates for a month, builds for three months of two-week sprints, then writes the first check (inferred from S008, S020, S033). He warns that fear and AGI hype are used "for fundraising purposes" (S015, 2026-10), and that cheap AI makes trying an idea cost far less: "it only cost me a million dollars to try an" idea (S049, 2025-11).

## Debt
- **No recorded position.**

## Staying lean and team shape
- **Stance:** Small, AI-enabled teams of generalists move fastest. Teams get leaner, not solo.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2025-08: "I think teams can definitely be smaller now than they used to be." (S012)
  - 2026-04: specialists suit large organizations; generalists who jump roles suit 2 to 10 person AI-native teams (S107).
  - 2026: PM to engineer ratios went from 1 to 8 toward 1 to 1, then toward one person doing both (S085).
- **Read:** he calls the one-person unicorn a hyped grain of truth (S049, 2025-11). In S012 a host, not Ng, argues that staying lean can be a trap once incumbents arrive; do not cite that as his view.

## First hire and leadership
- **Stance:** At AI Fund the CEO joins at the very start, before building. Leaders need enough hands-on AI judgment to set direction.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2023-08: "bringing on a leader at the very beginning to work with us, it reduces a lot of the burden of having to transfer knowledge" (S008)
  - undated: "leaders need sufficient technical judgment to set direction and milestones and allocate the resources to the teams" (S074)
  - 2025-08: "tech oriented product leaders I think are much more likely to succeed." (S012)
  - 2026-10: "You can't know what it feels like to ride a bicycle just by watching others do it; you have to hop on a bike yourself." (S127)

## Hiring
- **Stance:** Before adding a person, ask whether AI can do the job. Hire people who build with AI, in every role. Prefer full-stack generalists.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2025-08: "you hire AI not you know a lot more humans for this. You just got to have those instincts." (S012)
  - 2026-01: "Frankly today, I would not hire a software engineer that does not know how to use AI tools in a very sophisticated way." (S016)
  - undated: "Almost all of my engineers are full stack developers because we can now all do that." (S087)
  - 2026-08: "as part of how I interview marketers, we ask them what they built." (S011)
- **Read:** he picked an AI-fluent new grad over a ten-year engineer whose skills had not moved on (S064), but says the best hire is experienced and AI-native (S090). His favorite interview question is how the candidate keeps up with AI (S117, 2025-06).
- **Product managers:** product instinct comes from empathy, not engineering skill; his attempt to train engineers into PMs failed (S012). User empathy is now the scarcest PM skill (S103), and as coding speeds up teams need more PMs per engineer (S004).

## Firing
- **No recorded position.**

## Partners and cofounders
- **Stance:** Pair AI skill with deep domain skill. He stays in his lane and partners for the rest.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2023-08: "my swim lane is AI, and that's it." (S008)
  - 2024-10: "a lot of the most exciting projects I get to work on is when I, as a AI person, get to collaborate with someone with deep domain knowledge" (S010)
- **Read:** he says outright he has no expertise in shipping, relationships, or healthcare and relies on partners who do (S073). A specialist who learns AI should apply it to their own field (S067).

## Equity
- **No recorded position.**

## Niche and vertical focus
- **Stance:** Go vertical and use strengths you already have. The richest ground is industries with little AI yet: manufacturing, agriculture, healthcare, logistics.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2022-07: a long tail of $1M to $5M industry projects nobody serves (S023)
  - 2026-10: "Places where nations are really strong, if you can figure out" ... "where you can use AI to accelerate that industry, then that gives you an opportunity to do unique work" (S058)
- **Read:** inferred from S069, S076, S079, S095: unglamorous B2B sectors with small data are where he sends teams, because Silicon Valley ignores them.

## Side projects
- **Stance:** Legitimate stepping stones. Coursera began as a hobby while he was a full-time professor, and many startups began the same way (S126, 2022-07). He doubted he was even allowed to spend time on it and built it anyway (S090).

## Culture and training
- **Stance:** Everyone should code with AI, learn constantly, and act without waiting for the boss. Firms underinvest in training.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2025-06: "one of the most important skills in any profession will be the ability to tell a computer exactly what you want, so the computer can do it for you." (S105)
  - 2025-07: "it's time for everyone of every job role to learn to code" (S001)
  - 2026-08: "we're accelerating past the era where people should sit around and wait for their boss to tell them what to do." (S011)
  - 2026-10: "almost all individuals and almost all businesses and almost all nations underinvest in training" (S058)
- **Read:** he calls the advice not to learn to code some of the worst career advice ever given (S064, S081, S083, S086). He warns that chatbots used just to get work done hurt learning, for adults as well as students (S080, S092, S093).

## Jobs and AI
- **Over time:**
  - 2018: worried about near-term displacement of truck drivers, call centers, and radiology: "I honestly don't know any answer to this other than education." (S097)
  - undated (older talk): super-intelligence talk is "a distraction from the conversation about labour which I think is a serious issue" (S082)
  - 2026-05 (newer): "There will be no AI jobpocalypse." (S128)
  - undated: of recently laid-off people, "1.4% of recently laid off people actually had their jobs replaced by AI" (S077)
- **Read:** his emphasis moved from displacement risk (2018) to job creation and scope growth (2025 to 2026). He still says he worries about displacement more than Marc Andreessen does (S128), and that "AI won't replace someone, but someone that uses AI will replace someone that doesn't." (S071)

## Work hours
- **Stance:** Hard work matters, and he says so while admitting it is unpopular. "working hard probably correlates to personal success." (S012, 2025-08) He does not track his own hours (S049).

## Burnout
- **No recorded position.** His 2024 letter on persistence and community quotes other founders on this, not his own view (S033).

## Remote work
- **Stance (thin):** for top speed, sit together. "the highest speed is achieved by having everyone in the room, able to communicate instantaneously to solve problems." (S107, 2026-04) No broader position on remote policy.

## Content, paid ads, and brand
- **No recorded position** on content marketing, paid ads, or brand building. Nearest: he wants marketers who write their own software (S011, S016, S088), and he lets large-company experiments launch under a separate brand to limit risk (S102).

## Selling the company
- **No recorded position.**

## Timing, hype, and the AI bubble
- **Stance:** Build now. Applications are not in a bubble; training infrastructure carries the risk. Hype is the danger: it caused past AI winters and costs the field trust.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2022-11, in a downturn: "the long-term value of AI is still clear to me." (S045)
  - 2025-12: "The AI community has to stop self-inflicting these wounds and work to win back society's trust." (S111)
  - 2026-01: "I don't think there's a bubble. You know, yes, the hype has gone like that, rising really rapidly, but the actual business value is also rising very rapidly." (S017)
  - undated: "we've seen a few AI winters where people, well-meaning people, overhyped the promise of AI." (S071)
- **Read:** he has no forecast for which model leads next year (S007, S049).

## Waiting for better AI or AGI
- **Stance:** Do not wait. AGI is decades away and the word is now marketing. A better workflow on today's model often beats the next model.
- **Over time:**
  - 2018: "it's worth thinking about that artificial general intelligence is so far away" (S098), possibly hundreds of years.
  - 2024-03: "I think AI agent workflows will drive massive AI progress this year" (S124)
  - 2025-11: "I'm not a fan of this let's wait for AGI, you know, that feels hypey to me" (S055)
  - 2025-11: "I would say no, like decades away, maybe even longer." (S049)
  - undated: "AGI has become a marketing term." (S071)

## Reliability and customer expectations
- **Stance:** AI is impressive and still dumb. Add human checks and confirmation steps before irreversible actions, and set customer expectations early and often.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2022-05: he admits self-driving, his own field once, "did a poor job of setting expectations" (S041)
  - 2025-11: "AI is still incredibly dumb, and I would not trust a frontier LLM by itself to prioritize my calendar, carry out resumé screening, or choose what to order for lunch." (S113)
  - 2026-05: "AI isn't always reliable and it's better at simple analysis than really complex ones" (S056)
- **Read:** his own data agents give badly wrong answers about a quarter of the time, and he uses them anyway for speed, with a human steering (S084). High-risk uses such as health, justice, and finance still need a rigorous test set before launch (S122).

## Speed with responsibility
- **Stance:** Move fast on reversible choices, and kill projects that fail an ethics test even when the numbers work.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2023-08: "we have multiple times killed projects that we assess to be financially sound, based on ethical grounds." (S008)
  - 2024-10, citing Bezos's two-way door idea: "You can make sure you can move fast but not break things." (S010)
  - 2025-07: "I tend to tell my teams to move fast and be responsible" (S001)
- **Read:** speed does not excuse skipping a responsible AI review where harm is possible (S123). He admits hard ethical calls are a struggle and he is not sure he always gets them right (S077).
