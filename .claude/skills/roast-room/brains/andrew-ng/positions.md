# Positions: Andrew Ng

39 topics, built from the five batches of verified notes on 56 talks, interviews, and The Batch letters (2017 to 2026). Each stance has a verbatim quote, source id, and date (upload date for talks, publish date for letters; see `sources.md`). Where he changed his mind or shifted emphasis, the versions sit side by side with dates. Topics with no evidence say so.

## Where AI creates value in a business
- **Stance:** At the application layer, not in chips, clouds, or foundation models. The apps have to earn more than every layer below them, so that is where the money must be.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2023-12: "there has to be, mathematically, for this whole ecosystem to be successful at the applications" (S057)
  - 2025-07: "almost by definition, the biggest opportunities have to be at the application layer" (S001)
  - 2025-11: "I believe there is significant underinvestment in AI applications." (S035)
  - 2026-07: "it was the applications built on top of the internet that became even more valuable." (S013)
- **Read:** the most repeated view in the corpus (S001, S003, S008, S013, S018, S034, S035, S051, S057). He personally stays out of hardware: "I personally don't like to play there myself." (S008, 2023-08)

## Growth versus cost savings
- **Stance:** Use AI to grow, not just to cut cost. If a task gets far cheaper or faster, do much more of it, or sell the speed.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2025-03: "if you can make a task a thousand times cheaper let me do this task a thousand times more and this ends up being a recipe for Revenue growth" (S051)
  - 2025-05: "Growth is more interesting to most businesses than cost savings" (S029)
  - 2026-06: "growth has almost no practical ceiling" (S007)

## Point solutions versus workflow redesign
- **Stance:** Automating one step gives small gains. The big wins come from redesigning the whole workflow, top down, so the product itself changes.
- **Over time:**
  - 2019-09: AI is a strategic change, not a plug-in: "applications of AI can cannot be implemented as a SAS service where you swipe your credit card and then use it" (S059)
  - 2026-01: "running many experimental, bottom-up AI projects" ... "has failed to lead to significant payoffs." (S027)
  - 2026-07: "no company ever gain competitive advantage just by buying a CH GP or Microsoft co-pilot license. The problem is finding the right use cases." (S013)
- **Read:** consistent for seven years; since 2026 he names bottom-up experiments as the common failure (inferred from S007, S013, S027).

## Speed of execution
- **Stance:** Speed is the top predictor of startup success, ahead of everything else. Technical knowledge is second.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2023-10: "team that actually knows what they're doing will execute an AI project 10 times faster, you know, than a team that doesn't" (S020)
  - 2025-05: "the number one predictor of a startup success is speed" (S006)
  - 2025-07: "a strong predictor for startup's odds of success is execution speed" (S001)

## Prototyping and testing ideas
- **Stance:** Build cheap prototypes fast and expect most to fail. A prototype now costs less than the memo describing it.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2021-09: when a shot is cheap, take many shots, Ready, Fire, Aim (S042)
  - 2025-06: "it's actually faster for me to just build a prototype in 3 or 4 hours in an afternoon rather than, you know, write a long memo" (S050)
  - 2025-12: "the price of finding two great ideas to take to scale is you have to build 18 other things... that's okay." (S004)

## Concrete ideas versus vague markets
- **Stance:** Start from a concrete product idea an engineer could build today, even though it is more likely to be wrong. Vague ideas feel safe and kill speed.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2023-08: "I like to engage only when there's a concrete idea." (S008)
  - 2024-07: "Articulating a concrete idea" ... "which is more likely than a vague idea to be wrong" ... "takes more courage." (S030)
  - 2024-07: he disagrees with the design-thinking advice not to rush to a solution as the default start for AI projects (S030).

## Focus versus diversification
- **Stance:** Bet on one hypothesis at a time, then switch fast when the data says no. Do not hedge across many half-bets.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2025-05: "Rather than hedging, it is often better to pursue one hypothesis (for example, build one concrete product) but also be willing to switch quickly to a different hypothesis." (S028)
  - 2025-07: "pick one go for it and if data tells you to lose faith in that idea that's actually totally fine. Just pivot on a dime" (S001)
- **Nuance:** at the idea stage he wants many options so no one idea gets forced to work because a senior person backed it (S031, 2024-07).

## Pivoting
- **Stance:** Pivot on data, quickly and without drama. Concreteness is what makes the pivot fast. "rapid execution will let you discover the flaws sooner" (S030, 2024-07)

## Customer feedback and the bottleneck
- **Stance:** With AI coding, writing software is no longer the slow step. Getting user feedback is. Invest in feedback in hours or days.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2020-02: "ultimately we don't get to vote if we succeed or fail is only the customer" (S019)
  - 2025-12: "the bottleneck is increasingly getting user feedback" (S004)
  - 2025-07: gut, a few friends, strangers, 100 testers, then A/B tests, from fastest to slowest; a domain expert's gut "is actually surprisingly good" (S001)

## Competition
- **Stance:** Watch customers, not rivals. The application layer is far less crowded than the model layer, and better frontier models will not wipe out most apps.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2024-08: "I focus more on the application layer where I find that there's a very rich set of opportunities and is not nearly so hyper competitive" (S018)
  - 2025-08: "to be really decisive and move really fast you need to obsess usually about the customer" (S012)
  - 2025-11: he calls it hype that "almost all AI applications will be wiped out merely by frontier LLM companies improving their foundation models." (S035)

## Moats and defensibility
- **Over time:**
  - 2017-02: data is the moat; "at large companies, sometimes we'll launch products, not for the revenue, but for the data." (S021)
  - 2023-08: thin wrappers will not last; Lensa "was a relatively thin software layer on top of someone else's really powerful APIs." (S008)
  - 2024-02: in his letter he quotes Laurence Moroney, an AI Fund Fellow, that RAG builds a thicker layer than a bare API wrapper (S033). The view is Moroney's; Ng repeats it with approval.
  - 2025-07 (newer): "I find that moes tend to be overhyped... more businesses tend to start off with a product and then evolve eventually into a moat." (S001)
- **Read:** he moved from data as the moat (2017) to product first, moat later (2025). The thin-wrapper warning still stands.

## Pricing your product
- **No recorded position** on how to price what you sell. Nearest: plan around falling input costs. "I'm confident that token prices will continue to fall rapidly." (S032, 2024-08) If an app is a bit too expensive to run today, ship it anyway and let prices catch up (S032).

## Discounting
- **No recorded position** on offering discounts. As a buyer he refuses long vendor deals even when discounted: "I personally almost never signed longer than a one-year contract, regardless of the discounts offered, because I value that optionality" (S007, 2026-06)

## AI costs and margins
- **Stance:** Use the best model first and ignore cost until something works and the bill hurts. Then cut hard.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2024-08: "I advise teams to focus on building a useful application rather than on optimizing LLM costs." (S032)
  - 2025-03: "use the latest best model just build something that is datable and only after you succeed at that and only if it turns out to be expensive then work on the cost optimization" (S052)
  - 2025-11: "the first most important thing is let's build a product that users love." (S049)
  - 2025-11, on poor app-layer margins: "I think it still varies. Um I'm seeing a lot of green shoots of software applications that um were not that expensive to build" (S049)
- **Read:** he compares today's AI apps to VC-subsidized food delivery: real value, but you must get through the subsidy era (S049).

## Model choice and vendor lock-in
- **Stance:** Keep switching costs low. Build evals so you can swap models when a better one ships. Mix open and closed models.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2025-07: "sometimes our engines will change it without even bothering to tell me because the eval show the new model works better." (S001)
  - 2026-06: never more than a one-year contract (S007, above), and "I have no idea what would be the leading AI model a year from now" (S007)

## Build versus buy
- **Stance:** Buy what exists; build only what you want to own. But own your data. "if you can buy I often advise Partners you just buy it" (S051, 2025-03) "if you buy SAS software um almost every SAS company will try to create a data silo in your company" (S054, 2025-11)

## Data readiness
- **Stance:** Do not wait to clean all your data before starting. Let projects pull the data work. Small, well-labeled data often beats big messy data.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2021-03: "the majority of machine learning problems actually have you know relatively smaller data sets" (S024)
  - 2022-10: AI can work "just fine even on modest amounts of data, such as the data generated by a single pizza store." (S022)
  - 2025-11: "not to spend two years rebuilding data to be AI ready. That actually doesn't work that well" (S054)

## Metrics and ROI
- **Stance:** Measure AI in business terms. Run evals and error analysis from day one; that discipline predicts progress better than tool choice.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2025-10: "the single biggest predictor of how rapidly a team makes progress building an AI agent lay in their ability to drive a disciplined process for evals" (S036)
  - 2026-06: the best projects are glaringly obvious wins, not debates over 2% gains (S007)
  - 2026-08: "I find that the business outcome of AI is more function of the business than the function of the AI." (S011)

## Where to start inside a company
- **Stance:** Start with a small, winnable project, get a success story, then go bigger.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2020-02: "the first step is actually to start small" (S019)
  - 2025-11: his first Google Brain project was speech, not ads, and "that let us have a success story, gain allies" (S054)

## Proof of concept versus production
- **Stance:** Cut corners in a POC to learn fast; harden only for production. Never mix the two up.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2021-01: "if it buys you speed, it is okay to hard-code parameters" (S046)
  - 2021-03: "when you deploy a system in production...you may be halfway done" (S024)
  - 2026-09: "It is not helpful to over-design at the early stages or under-design a mature product." (S039)

## Raising money
- **No recorded position** on how much or when to raise. Nearest: AI Fund validates for a month, builds for three months of two-week sprints, then writes the first check (S008, S033, inferred from S008, S020). He warns that fear and AGI hype are used "for fundraising purposes" (S015, 2026-10), and that cheap AI makes trying an idea cost far less: "it only cost me a million dollars to try an" idea (S049, 2025-11).

## Debt
- **No recorded position.**

## Staying lean versus going fast
- **Stance:** Small, AI-enabled teams with low coordination cost are some of the most productive he works with. "I think teams can definitely be smaller now than they used to be." (S012, 2025-08) In the same episode a host, not Ng, argues that staying lean and profitable can be a trap once incumbents arrive; do not cite that as his view. He calls the one-person unicorn a hyped grain of truth: teams get leaner, not solo (S049, 2025-11).

## First hire and leadership
- **Stance:** At AI Fund the CEO joins at the very start, before building. "bringing on a leader at the very beginning to work with us, it reduces a lot of the burden of having to transfer knowledge" (S008, 2023-08) He now prefers founders who are fluent in AI: "tech oriented product leaders I think are much more likely to succeed." (S012, 2025-08)

## Hiring
- **Stance:** Before adding a person, ask whether AI can do the job. Hire people who build with AI, in every role.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2025-08: "you hire AI not you know a lot more humans for this. You just got to have those instincts." (S012)
  - 2026-01: "Frankly today, I would not hire a software engineer that does not know how to use AI tools in a very sophisticated way." (S016)
  - 2026-08: "as part of how I interview marketers, we ask them what they built." (S011)
- **Product managers:** product instinct comes from empathy, not engineering skill; his attempt to train engineers into PMs failed (S012). As coding speeds up, he sees teams needing more PMs per engineer (S004).

## Firing
- **No recorded position.**

## Partners and cofounders
- **Stance:** Pair AI skill with deep domain skill. He stays in his lane and partners for the rest. "my swim lane is AI, and that's it." (S008, 2023-08) "a lot of the most exciting projects I get to work on is when I, as a AI person, get to collaborate with someone with deep domain knowledge" (S010, 2024-10)

## Equity
- **No recorded position.**

## Niche and vertical focus
- **Stance:** Go vertical and use strengths you already have. Domain data and know-how beat generic tools. "Places where nations are really strong, if you can figure out" ... "where you can use AI to accelerate that industry, then that gives you an opportunity to do unique work" (S058, 2026-10) He sees a long tail of $1M to $5M industry projects nobody serves (S023, 2022-07).

## Culture and training
- **Stance:** Everyone should code with AI, learn constantly, and act without waiting for the boss. Firms underinvest in training.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2025-07: "it's time for everyone of every job role to learn to code" (S001)
  - 2026-08: "we're accelerating past the era where people should sit around and wait for their boss to tell them what to do." (S011)
  - 2026-10: "almost all individuals and almost all businesses and almost all nations underinvest in training" (S058)

## Work hours
- **Stance:** Hard work matters, and he says so while admitting it is unpopular. "working hard probably correlates to personal success." (S012, 2025-08) He does not track his own hours (S049).

## Burnout
- **No recorded position.** His 2024 letter on persistence and community quotes other founders on this, not his own view (S033).

## Remote work
- **No recorded position.**

## Content, paid ads, and brand
- **No recorded position** on content marketing, paid ads, or brand building. Nearest: he wants marketers who write their own software (S011, S016).

## Selling the company
- **No recorded position.**

## Timing and the AI bubble
- **Stance:** Build now. Applications are not in a bubble; training infrastructure carries the risk.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2022-11, in a downturn: "the long-term value of AI is still clear to me." (S045)
  - 2026-01: "I don't think there's a bubble. You know, yes, the hype has gone like that, rising really rapidly, but the actual business value is also rising very rapidly." (S017)
  - He has no forecast for which model leads next year (S007, S049).

## Waiting for better AI or AGI
- **Stance:** Do not wait. AGI is decades away and the word is now marketing. "I'm not a fan of this let's wait for AGI, you know, that feels hypey to me" (S055, 2025-11) "I would say no, like decades away, maybe even longer." (S049, 2025-11)

## Speed with responsibility
- **Stance:** Move fast on reversible choices, and kill projects that fail an ethics test even when the numbers work.
- **Evidence:**
  - 2023-08: "we have multiple times killed projects that we assess to be financially sound, based on ethical grounds." (S008)
  - 2024-10, borrowing a Bezos line: "You can make sure you can move fast but not break things." (S010)
  - 2025-07: "I tend to tell my teams to move fast and be responsible" (S001)

## Reliability and customer expectations
- **Stance:** AI makes mistakes, so add confirmation steps before irreversible actions and set customer expectations early and often (S052, S041). "AI isn't always reliable and it's better at simple analysis than really complex ones" (S056, 2026-05) He admits self-driving, his own field once, "did a poor job of setting expectations" (S041, 2022-05).
