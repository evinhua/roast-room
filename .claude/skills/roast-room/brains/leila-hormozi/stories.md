# Stories: Leila Hormozi

Built from the 20 batch notes over 107 video sources (2021 to 2026, plus 59 undated uploads). 20 business and leadership stories she reuses, most-told first, with the lesson she draws. Personal and family history is left out on purpose except where she uses it to explain a leadership rule. Source ids in parentheses; where her numbers differ between retellings, the versions are listed and none should be treated as exact.

1. **The boss who left, and the boss who replaced him.** At her first gym job, Mike gave clear expectations, one-on-ones, and encouragement, and the team led the region. His replacement yelled, threatened, and was unpredictable; top trainers left and she sat in her car trying not to cry before shifts. Retellings differ on the drop: mediocre within about three months (S035), down to the bottom 30th percentile (S089). She tells it at team meetups to teach praise over punishment (S091) and calls it the reason she built a company where people want to work (S085). Lesson: "the reason that a lot of people don't like work is because they don't like their boss" (S035). A sales-team echo: switching from a fear-based manager to a clear, encouraging one moved close rate from 30% to 75% in six weeks (S080).

2. **The missing talent funnel.** Gym Launch stalled around $400,000 a month because sales capacity could not keep up. Her mentor Bob asked about her talent acquisition funnel; she built one that mirrored the client funnel and went to $4M a month in about 20 months without changing the offer or the sales funnel (S037, S095). She now runs the internal talent funnel while Alex runs the client one (S089). Her first 30 hires all came from her network, and she later saw she had settled for lower talent because her options were small (S095). Lesson: "we've always been really one hire away from all the growth we wanted all the leverage we needed all the sanity that we thought we lost in building the business" (S037).

3. **Motels to Gym Launch, with moving numbers.** She and Alex lived in motels for about 16 months while iterating; the first thing that worked, showing up at gyms for free, took a year to find (S019). The flying-to-gyms model was "basically just like eating [ __ ]" for 18 months until they switched to licensing and education (S008); gym owners charged back $250,000 before the pivot, and the day eight owners paid $6,000 each to learn the system showed the system was the product (S097). Growth figures vary: zero to $7M in six months and $27M in two and a half years after two years of losing money (S066); $7M to $27M in under two years (S075). At $300,000 a month she worked from 4 a.m. to collapse until caffeine stopped working (S019). Lesson: busy is not growth, and growth outran her skills (S019, S075).

4. **Hiring her friend, 0 for 23.** Her first sales hire at Gym Launch was a high school friend who went 0 for 23 in his first four days while other reps did fine (S045); a later retelling says 0 for 30, and she fired him and cried in her car (S058). She saw she had made a hiring mistake and a training mistake at once, because she had never done the job herself (S045). Lesson: "it is completely my fault because either I don't have the skill of picking the right person for that role or I misunderstood what I was looking for for the role" (S001). She calls it her first floppy pancake (S058).

5. **The best month she hated.** During fast growth she handed hiring to managers and stopped taking last interviews; within about five months the culture crumbled, and after a record $4.5M month she hated showing up (S039). In another telling, at $50M a year the operator quit, top salespeople left, and big clients churned at the same time (S056). She felt she had carried 120 people over the line alone; her mentor told her "If it's your business, then it's your fault," which started a leadership overhaul (S059). Lesson: "it starts with the hiring process" (S039), and "growth actually does not fix broken systems or broken culture, it actually multiplies all of those things." (S056)

6. **Same revenue, half the team.** Gym Launch peaked at about 120 full-time staff doing about $4.6M a month, and the culture was miserable (S102). She fired nearly the whole leadership team and told them it was her fault for being inexperienced (S102); an earlier telling says she hired 30 people in six months and replaced the entire leadership team at month 12 because they patched problems and normalized mediocrity (S058). After accountability systems went in (end-of-week reports, communication cycles, utilization metrics), the same revenue ran on 55 people (S102) or 75 (S084), with fewer complaints and lower churn. The trigger was an employee who said "my boss has literally given me no work i take two calls a day and you're paying me 20 an hour" (S102). Lesson: "you can have the same revenue with half the team and the reason for that is just the standards change." (S102)

7. **Overhiring and the layoff she owned.** For the Prestige Labs supplement launch she staffed to a projection borrowed from a different business: 20 customer service reps when six were needed after three months (S032), or 35 to 40 hires before launch and 15 to 25 laid off, with Glassdoor falling from 5 to 2.2 (S058). A rep called to say she had answered one email in seven hours and could not ethically keep working (S032, S058). She shut the department, let the manager go first, met everyone one by one, overpaid severance, wrote letters of recommendation, lined up interviews, and cried while owning the mistake on the call; the team later called themselves "survivors of the purge" (S030). She still felt like a terrible CEO afterward (S041). Lesson: do not solve short-term pain with permanent hires: "a full-time employee is a one-size fits-all solution to a very intricate problem" (S032).

8. **Prestige Labs, the business she is least proud of.** She launched Prestige Labs beside Gym Launch despite stretched resources (S026). It made gym owners $16M in commissions, which gave the team a sense of impact (S038), but physical-goods margins ran 10% to 40% against Gym Launch's service margins (S027) and the split attention cost both. She says both grew, yet the combined result would have been two to three times larger with focus (S075): "it is a completely different business and it was very hard to do both" (S072). Lesson: "that was probably the biggest thing I learned from Prestige was the value of focus" (S097).

9. **Software and meals: ventures she argued for, against, and killed.** Alex wanted to build software; she thought it would cost millions. They argued for three days and agreed to shut it down if it was not profitable within four months; she says it worked and sold, but it lacked a product person from day one (S008). The Allen lead-nurture tool fit the short-term plan but not the strategy and cost 18 months of linear growth (S060); she hired a big-tech CTO on his resume and the mistake cost about $300,000 (S069); a beta that missed 30% of leads led her to go live on Facebook, take the blame, and offer full refunds (S078); and it was built too fast on the wrong technology (S097). A 2026 telling says two software products over about two years and neither worked out (S017), which does not match the 2022 telling. Separately, she and Alex spent about a year and over a million dollars on a meals business and killed it (S016, S017). Lesson: set a kill date, and "The market doesn't care" how long you worked on it (S016); she counts cutting fast as an advantage (S017).

10. **Two shocks: the Facebook slap and COVID.** When Facebook ad costs quadrupled overnight, half her leadership charged toward the new direction and half cowered; she changed pricing, marketing, sales, product, and customer base, and half the leaders left (S081). An earlier version of the warning: cost per lead crept from $0.50 to over $2 and she stayed quiet until the community revolted (S029). Then COVID hit three businesses that all served physical locations (S061). She cut to the bone and told the team she was pausing birthdays, anniversaries, and kudos for three to four months (S031), froze raises and promotions so people would still have jobs in a year (S093), and kept a "be neutral" note on her computer (S020). She could not promise jobs but could show certainty: "I could let them borrow my sense of certainty" is how she frames it (S029). Lesson: "you don't get tested when times are easy. You get tested when shit's hard." (S070)

11. **The Gym Launch sale.** Before going to market she proved growth with a big-box program for franchise chains and closed six deals; in diligence her team answered questions she could not, and buyers read that as proof the business ran without the founders (S027). She told the team two weeks before an expected close, the close slipped six weeks, and most spent two months job hunting; she told the next team after close, which was "200 times better" (S101). She let advisors drive decisions because she felt unsophisticated, and later saw she had given up her role as decision maker (S101). Numbers: valued at $150M or more before COVID, sold for about $50M (S006). She felt nothing when the money landed (S066, S071), and the payoff she remembers is writing team members checks that paid off homes (S012). Lesson: sell when you do not need to, and with her current skills she would not have sold (S006, paraphrase).

12. **Firing in public view.** Growing fast, she fired two newly hired leaders with strong resumes because they did not treat people well, told the team why, and watched less experienced leaders repeat the decision for years (S016). A charming hire with an impressive resume called a coworker names: "This person does not have unimpeachable character." (S069). When she fired three people who siphoned customers, their friends inside turned on her, and she took being called names rather than break confidentiality (S070). A new hire who used Slack to ask Alex for personal financial advice was fired on the spot (S098). Lesson: a leader's decision becomes a template, and "I will never break confidentiality. I will never humiliate somebody else." (S070)

13. **The $9 million dumb tax.** She and Alex unwound their three worst financial decisions and found about $9M of avoidable cost, about half from one wrong person in the wrong role for about a year (S044). Her worked example: an underperforming marketing director, run through her four-step framework, ends in firing and hiring someone better (S044). Lesson: avoid dumb decisions before chasing brilliant ones; people decisions carry the biggest dumb tax (S044).

14. **Betting on horses, then on jockeys.** Early Acquisition.com investments followed other people's rules instead of first principles, and she divested several (S007). The founders who failed had rated themselves higher than she rated them; the ones who succeeded rated themselves lower (S007). She put close to $10M into a company that became a thorn in her side and did not change her process because of one result (S014). She walked away from an agency whose 50-some clients turned out to be six, two of them about to cancel (S027). The divested founders could not change or face hard things (S049). Lesson: "I'm going to bet on the jockey I'm done betting on horses and if I can't find a jockey that I like no jockey is better than the wrong jockey" (S026).

15. **The operator ceiling in portfolio companies.** A certification business stuck at $400,000 a month had the founder's best friend as operator; a proper recruit took it to $1.2M a month in six months (S037, S095). A course business down 60% at $1.8M a month filled five missing executive roles and reached $12M a month 18 months later (S037, S095). An operator spent two hours telling her the founder was not the real CEO; once he was removed the business tripled (S099) or went from $300,000 to $1.3M a month (S098). A CEO at $2.9M bragged he had outsourced everything to an operator she judged a disorganized micromanager; a month later he wrote "i just had a huge blowout i fired my operator you were right" (S105). A $15M company that added a head of sales, marketing director, controller, head of development, and head of people reached $70M in three years (S076). Lesson: "if your operator's not a 10 out of 10 your business isn't going to be a 10 out of 10" (S099).

16. **Same team, new leader.** A sales manager who drilled his team without caring about them was replaced and production doubled within a week (S082). A director who called his team stupid was replaced by one who believed people are trainable; a year and a half later those people were leaders (S082). The same six-person finance team delivered month-end financials in 30 days under one director and three days under a new CFO (S082). Under fear, a finance director hid a $9M tax bill until the weekend before it was due (S089). Lesson: "they're not stupid you just don't know how to teach you just don't know how to train you just don't know how to reinforce behaviors" (S082), and "scared people hide problems" (S089).

17. **Promotions she got right and wrong.** Sean, a top rep, hated sales management within three weeks because he lost the instant reward of closing; Mel could not give hard feedback without conflict; Maggie became a real leader once she saw her team as followers, not employees; Kale moved from short-term thinking to an annual vision talk that made her cry (S043). An admin assistant who went to events manager to customer success manager in 14 months beat outside candidates for VP of Customer Success and became her best head of CS (S076). She promoted loud Dietra, saw quiet Leia doing the real work, reversed it publicly, and Leia became one of her best leaders (S076). A humble hire turned arrogant after a title until she pointed it out (S032); young Gym Launch leaders got "drunk with power" and team ratings fell from the 99th percentile to below the 50th in eight months (S083); a strong operations manager became the bottleneck after 4x to 5x growth and became a star again once someone was hired above him (S026). Her EA Yasmine became director of operations through seven years of proximity (S028). Lesson: "to spot a high potential leader you look for rate of progress not rate of performance" (S076).

18. **The leader sets the standard without saying a word.** Her team looked sloppy on video calls until she noticed she did too; she started doing her hair and makeup and within weeks they followed (S061, S082). She picked sprinkles off the floor and days later two team members cleaned up after everyone without being asked (S053). A leader's treadmill desk spread to half the team in two months (S002), and she stopped drinking for three and a half years to hold the standard she asked of others (S060). Lesson: "what you do sets the standard for what's tolerable and acceptable for the rest of the team because people don't listen to what you say they follow what you do" (S082).

19. **Stepping away so the team steps up.** When a former employee cloned the business and took clients, she gave her leadership team until Monday to bring a plan and they ran it with weekly check-ins (S059). Two weeks out for surgery, she came back to find a Facebook shutdown and two departures already handled, and saw she had been robbing them of the chance to step up (S060). An IVF surgery on an event day put two team members on stage for the first time; both became top-rated speakers within weeks (S028, S030). A direct report who could not speak in meetings went to running half an event in eight months because Leila praised each attempt (S010). Lesson: "Codling your team robs them of the opportunity to develop those skills themselves" (S079).

20. **Acquisition.com 3.0, Chiron, and the chairwoman seat.** After headcount doubled in under a year, she told the team their talent had been covering for missing process and that her own style had to change first: "this version of Leila doesn't scale this company and I have to set the standard for how everybody else here operates." (S021). With 15 direct reports and every division rolling up to her, she was white-knuckling the business and brought in Chiron as president and partner (S013, S023), skeptical at first and convinced by their fourth meeting (S023). The company went from 87 to 175 people in about a year (S015). Six months pregnant in 2026, she moved from CEO to executive chairwoman, calling it the right person in the wrong season rather than a failure (S015). Lesson: in her own company, the person who had to change first was her (inferred from S021, S023, S015).
