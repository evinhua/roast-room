# Stories: Ray Kurzweil

20 stories and cases he reuses, most-used first, with the lesson he draws. Built from the batch notes on all 43 sources. Source ids in parentheses.

1. **The Genome Project at 1%.** Seven years in, only 1% was sequenced and critics called it a failure. He said it was nearly done, because 1% is seven doublings from 100%. It finished on time. "1% is only seven doublings from 100%." (S037) Lesson: count doublings, not current progress; a small early number on an exponential is not failure (S002, S009, S015, S034, S035, S037, S038, S039).
2. **The rice on the chessboard.** An inventor asks for one grain doubled on each square. The first half looks harmless; the second half ruins the emperor. He says computing is now in the second half. Lesson: linear thinkers cannot picture late doublings (S001, S014, S044).
3. **Predicting the web from ARPAnet.** In the 1980s, when the defense budget could link a few thousand scientists, he read the yearly doubling and predicted a worldwide web by the mid to late 1990s. People thought it was crazy. Lesson: read the curve, not the current size (S014, S037, S039, S041).
4. **OCR in the 1970s, speech in the 1980s.** He built his computation charts to time his own projects: character recognition needed little compute so he did it first; speech recognition needed more so he waited. Lesson: pick the project the curve can support at launch (S032, S033).
5. **Deep Blue and Kasparov.** In the 1980s he predicted a computer would win the world chess title by 1998, and that people would then dismiss chess as unimportant. Kasparov scoffed and lost in 1997. Lesson: machines creep up and then soar past experts (S015, S035, S041, S044).
6. **The Stanford conference.** After his 1999 call of AGI by 2029, several hundred AI experts at Stanford put it at 100 years, some at never. The consensus has since moved toward his date. Lesson: trust the curve over expert intuition (S002, S010).
7. **The blind man on the plane.** He had a font-independent OCR system with no use for it, sat next to a blind man who said the one thing he could not do was read print, and built the reading machine, scanner, and text to speech to fit. Lesson: take a solution to the people who need it most (S012, S039).
8. **The Today Show demo.** In 1976 the only working prototype died two hours before going live on NBC. After rebuilding it backstage it still failed, until his chief engineer slammed it down and it worked. Lesson: ship under pressure with what you have (S012).
9. **Stevie Wonder and the Kurzweil 250.** Stevie Wonder saw the reading machine, called, and asked for a synthesizer that sounded like real instruments. That became Kurzweil Music Systems and the Kurzweil 250. Lesson: a demanding user will hand you the next product (S012).
10. **Speech recognition pricing.** At his speech company, the previous version sold for about a quarter of the current price, and versions two generations old could not be given away. Lesson: in an information technology market, value decays fast, so keep shipping the newest (S014).
11. **The dot com boom and bust.** Wall Street said the internet would change every business model, crashed when it did not happen overnight, and came back as adoption kept doubling. Lesson: a capital markets timing error is not a technology failure (S025, S034, S038, S041).
12. **Google and Facebook from dorm rooms.** Two Stanford students and two Harvard students built giant companies on cheap laptops. Lesson: falling costs put world-scale tools in everyone's hands, and nobody predicts the applications (S039, S041).
13. **Moderna's vaccine in two days.** Moderna simulated billions of mRNA sequences and had its formula in two days, against 5 to 10 years normally. Lesson: once a field becomes information technology, simulation replaces slow trials (S008, S012, S033).
14. **Rosenblatt and the perceptron.** He visited Frank Rosenblatt in the 1960s and asked whether he had tried stacking layers. Rosenblatt had not; he died without trying, and layered nets later took over the field. Lesson: the right idea early is not enough without execution and timing (S034).
15. **Larry Page buys Patterns Inc.** He pitched Larry Page for a venture investment two weeks after starting Patterns. Page said Google had far more compute and data and offered to buy the company outright. Lesson: in AI, who owns the compute and data changes the deal (S004).
16. **Self-driving cars.** His 1999 forecast of cars without a driver was wrong. He blames the extra margin society demands before it accepts a machine in place of a person. Lesson: technical readiness is not adoption; plan for the acceptance bar (S002, S003, S006).
17. **Dragon Dictation and the translating phone.** In late 2009, speech recognition and phone translation went from futuristic to top iPhone apps within months. Lesson: once the last enabling factor lands, status flips almost overnight (S015).
18. **The music industry versus publishers.** The music business clung to a model from his father's childhood and had a rough transition; publishers who embraced ebooks did better. Lesson: ask what job you do for customers and let the new technology do it better (S040).
19. **Microsoft's paranoia.** He credits Microsoft's stability through the dot com crash not to its monopoly but to Gates's fear of relying on any one business model. Lesson: plan for the lifespan of your model (S025).
20. **The stock prediction fund.** He built a pattern recognition system that predicted short-term stock moves better than chance and traded real money. He compares it to a casino house that wins over many bets. Lesson: you need better-than-chance odds at volume, not certainty (S038).
