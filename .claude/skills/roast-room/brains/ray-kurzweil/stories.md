# Stories: Ray Kurzweil

20 stories and cases he reuses, most-used first, with the lesson he draws. Built from the batch notes on the full deep corpus. Source ids in parentheses.

1. **The Genome Project at 1%.** Seven years in, only 1% was sequenced and critics said 700 more years. He said it was nearly done, because 1% is seven doublings from 100%, and it finished on time. "1% is only seven doublings from 100%." (S037) Lesson: count doublings, not current progress; a small early number on an exponential is not failure. His most told story (S002, S009, S015, S035, S037, S038, S039, S045, S049, S051, S073, S086, S091, S097, S100, S102).
2. **The rice on the chessboard.** An inventor asks for one grain doubled on each square. The first half looks harmless; the second half ruins the emperor. He says computing is now in the second half. Lesson: linear thinkers cannot picture late doublings (S001, S010, S014, S044).
3. **Predicting the web from ARPAnet.** In the early 1980s, when the defense budget could link a few thousand scientists, he read the yearly doubling and predicted a worldwide web by the mid to late 1990s, plus the need for search engines. People thought it was crazy. "that's the power of exponential growth." (S039) Lesson: read the curve, not the current size; it predicts categories, not winners (S014, S037, S039, S041, S046, S095).
4. **The expert consensus that moved to him.** After his 1999 call of AGI by 2029, a Stanford conference of several hundred AI experts said 100 years, some never. Later polls said 50 years, then 20 to 30. "I've technically gone from being an optimist to a pessimist without changing my prediction at all." (S084) Lesson: trust the curve over expert intuition (S002, S010, S081, S084, S085, S087).
5. **OCR in the 1970s, speech in the 1980s.** He built his computation charts to time his own projects: character recognition needed little compute so he did it first; speech recognition needed far more so he waited. Lesson: pick the project the curve can support at launch (S033, S076, S078).
6. **The blind man on the plane.** He had a font-independent OCR system with no use for it, sat next to a blind man who said the one thing he could not do was read print, and built the scanner, text to speech, and reading machine to fit. It did not catch on until the early 1980s, and he calls it his most gratifying invention. Lesson: take a solution to the people who need it most, and expect a gap between invention and adoption (S012, S039, S090, S103).
7. **Deep Blue and Kasparov.** In the 1980s he predicted a computer would win the world chess title by 1998, and that people would then dismiss chess as unimportant. Kasparov scoffed and lost in 1997. "they crept up on them and then suddenly soared past" (S044) Lesson: machines creep up, then pass experts fast; critics then move the goalposts (S015, S035, S041, S044, S053).
8. **Google and Facebook from dorm rooms.** Two Stanford students and two Harvard students built giant companies on cheap laptops. Social networks had existed years earlier, but "the price performance wasn't there" (S101). Of search: "there were 30 projects to do that." (S102) Lesson: the right idea wins when the curve enables it, and nobody can predict which team (S039, S041, S090, S101, S102).
9. **Moderna's vaccine in two days.** Moderna simulated several billion mRNA sequences and had its formula in two days, against years normally. Lesson: once a field becomes information technology, simulation replaces slow trials and discovery time collapses (S008, S012, S033, S076, S078, S081).
10. **Rosenblatt and the perceptron.** As a teenager he visited Frank Rosenblatt and asked whether he had tried stacking layers. Rosenblatt had not, and died in 1971 without trying. Minsky's book then froze neural net funding for about 25 years; before he died, Minsky told Kurzweil he now thought neural nets were the future. Lesson: the right idea early is not enough without the tools, timing, and execution; one narrow proof can freeze a field (S034, S084, S093, S094).
11. **The dot com boom and bust.** Wall Street said the internet would change every business model, crashed when it did not happen overnight, then came back as Google, Amazon, and others while adoption kept doubling. Lesson: a capital markets timing error is not a technology failure; 3D printing ran the same cycle (S025, S034, S038, S041, S088).
12. **Speech recognition pricing.** At his speech company, the previous version sold for about a quarter of the current price, and versions two generations old could not be given away. Over 15 years a $5,000 package became a $50 product with a far larger vocabulary. Lesson: value decays fast on the curve, so keep shipping the newest (S014, S045, S046).
13. **Self-driving cars.** His forecast of cars without a driver came years too early. He blames regulation and the extra margin society demands before it accepts a machine in place of a person. Lesson: technical readiness is not adoption; plan for the acceptance bar (S002, S003, S006, S087, S092).
14. **The e-reader company's parameter sheet.** At his e-reader startup the team wrote down every technical parameter the business depended on (display resolution, network speed, mobile compute) and tracked where each would be in one or two years. After 30 years of doing this he is still surprised. Lesson: do the curve audit explicitly every time, because intuition stays linear (S090).
15. **Larry Page buys Patterns Inc.** He pitched Larry Page for a venture investment two weeks after starting Patterns. Page said Google had far more compute and data and offered to buy the company outright; he joined Google to get that scale. Lesson: in AI, who owns the compute and data changes the deal (S004, S101).
16. **Stevie Wonder and the Kurzweil 250.** Stevie Wonder saw the reading machine on television, called, and asked for a synthesizer that sounded like real instruments. That became Kurzweil Music Systems and the Kurzweil 250. Lesson: a demanding user will hand you the next product (S012).
17. **Solar doublings for a prime minister and Larry Page.** He showed solar's two-year doubling to Israel's prime minister, who asked whether there was enough sunlight; after seven more doublings from 1% the world would use one part in 10,000 of it. He also steered Page toward solar because it had a 25-year exponential record and geothermal did not. Lesson: back the technology with a long measured curve, and a small share is not a small future (S088, S089, S101).
18. **Incumbents: music, publishers, Microsoft.** The music business clung to a model from his father's childhood and had a rough transition; publishers who moved to ebooks early did better. He credits Microsoft's survival of the dot com crash to Gates's fear of relying on any one business model. Lesson: move to the new model early and plan for your model's lifespan (S025, S040).
19. **Sony and the transistor radio.** American inventors saw the transistor as a hearing aid part; Sony licensed it, rethought the radio as a device for one person, and had a hit. Lesson: marketing creativity, rethinking what a technology is for, can matter as much as the invention (S070).
20. **The stock prediction fund.** He built a pattern recognition system that predicted short-term stock moves better than chance and traded real money, and compares it to a casino house that wins over many bets. Lesson: you need better-than-chance odds at volume, not certainty (S038).
