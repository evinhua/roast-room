# Frameworks: Ray Kurzweil

20 frameworks, roughly most-used first, weighted toward what a founder brings to the seat on exponential trends and timing. Built from 43 sources (talks, interviews, and essays, 1999 to 2026). "Sources" counts distinct sources in the corpus where the idea is used (ids listed). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short descriptive name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from his stated thresholds, not quotes.

---

## 1. The law of accelerating returns
- **What it is:** Every information technology improves on a smooth, predictable exponential. His main chart is computation per constant dollar from 1939 to now, which forms a straight line on a log scale through five hardware paradigms, wars, and recessions. He says hardware alone has improved about 75 quadrillion-fold and that combined hardware and software AI capability now grows about 10x a year for the same cost (S001, S006, S012).
- **Parts:** An evolutionary process uses the products of one stage to build the next; returns (speed, cost-effectiveness, power) therefore grow exponentially; as a technology gets more cost-effective, more resources flow to it, which adds a second level of growth; when one paradigm saturates, the next carries the curve.
- **When to use:** Any decision whose payoff depends on what a technology will cost or be able to do in two to ten years: building on AI, compute, sequencing, bandwidth, sensors, solar, or anything that has become information.
- **How to score:** 5 if the plan names the underlying measure (cost per computation, per token, per base pair, per watt) and states where it will be on the curve at launch; 3 if it assumes improvement but never quantifies it; 1 if it assumes today's price and capability stay fixed. Score 1 also if the founder applies the curve to something that is not an information technology.
- **Quote:** "the speed with which we increased it was the same regardless of the technology used." (S013)
- **Sources:** 24 (S001, S002, S004, S006, S008, S009, S010, S011, S012, S013, S014, S015, S016, S024, S025, S032, S034, S035, S037, S038, S039, S041, S042, S044)

## 2. Intuitive linear view versus historical exponential view (30 steps)
- **What it is:** People forecast by assuming the recent rate continues. Thirty linear steps get you to 30; thirty doublings get you to a billion. That gap explains why experts keep underestimating the long run.
- **Parts:** Ask whether the forecast is linear; restate it in doublings; expect the short term to disappoint and the long term to surprise.
- **When to use:** When a founder or an investor says something will take 10 more years because the last step took 10 years, or dismisses a trend because today's number is small.
- **How to score:** High if the plan accounts for both errors: it does not bet the company on next quarter's breakthrough, and it does not assume the next five years look like the last five. Low if it extrapolates in a straight line in either direction.
- **Quote:** "people tend to overestimate what can be achieved in the short term (because we tend to leave out necessary details), but underestimate what can be achieved in the long term (because the exponential growth is ignored)." (S014)
- **Sources:** 15 (S001, S003, S006, S007, S010, S014, S016, S024, S025, S034, S035, S037, S039, S041, S042)

## 3. Timing: build for the world that exists when you finish
- **What it is:** He says he became a futurist only to time his own inventions. A project has to make sense in the world of its completion date, so you project the curve forward to the ship date and design for that point.
- **Parts:** Estimate how long the build takes; project the enabling technologies to that date; design the product for that capability and price; check that the window will still be open, and not yet crowded.
- **When to use:** Every product, platform, or market-entry decision with a build time longer than a few months. This is the core of the seat's question.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder can say what the key input will cost and do on the ship date and why that makes the product viable then; 3 if the timing case is a hunch; 1 if the product only works with today's tools and will be obsolete on arrival, or only works with tools that will not exist for years.
- **Quote:** "in 1981, I was thinking, my project has to make sense when I finish the project, and the world will be a different place two, three, four years from now." (S035)
- **Sources:** 11 (S015, S025, S032, S033, S034, S035, S037, S038, S039, S041, S040)

## 4. All the enabling factors *(his phrase)*
- **What it is:** A technology does not take off when its best part works. It takes off when every enabling factor is in place at once. His example: e-books existed a decade before the market, which waited for high-resolution tablets, wireless everywhere, and digitized catalogs (S040).
- **Parts:** List every factor the product needs (hardware, network, data, distribution, payment, regulation, user habit); mark which exist today and which arrive by launch; the latest one sets the date.
- **When to use:** When the tech works in the lab and the founder assumes the market follows.
- **How to score:** 5 if every factor is in place or on a measurable curve that arrives by launch; 3 if one factor depends on someone else's roadmap; 1 if a key factor (regulation, user acceptance, a missing platform) has no date at all.
- **Quote:** "technologies really don't take off until all the enabling factors are in place." (S040)
- **Sources:** 5 (S015, S025, S038, S040, S041)

## 5. Cascade of S-curves (paradigm shifts)
- **What it is:** Each specific paradigm (relays, vacuum tubes, transistors, integrated circuits, AI chips) follows an S-curve and saturates. The overall exponential continues because the limit creates research pressure for the next paradigm, which is usually already working in labs.
- **Parts:** Identify the paradigm your product rides on; find where it is on its own S-curve; look for the successor already in the lab; plan against the trend, not the paradigm.
- **When to use:** When a founder hears "Moore's law is over" or "scaling has hit a wall" and wants to slow down, or when a business is tied to one specific technology.
- **How to score:** High if the plan survives a switch of the underlying paradigm; low if the business is a bet on one paradigm staying on top.
- **Quote:** "the acceleration of the overall evolutionary process proceeds as a sequence of S-curves, and the overall exponential growth consists of this cascade of S-curves." (S024)
- **Sources:** 10 (S006, S014, S015, S019, S024, S034, S038, S039, S041, S042)

## 6. One percent is seven doublings from done *(unnamed; the Genome Project case)*
- **What it is:** Halfway through the Human Genome Project only 1% was sequenced and critics called it a failure. He said it was nearly finished, because 1% is seven doublings from 100%. It finished on schedule.
- **Parts:** Measure progress as a doubling rate, not a percentage done; count the doublings left; multiply by the doubling time.
- **When to use:** When a metric is tiny but doubling (users, revenue, model capability, a research program) and someone wants to kill it for being small.
- **How to score:** If the metric has doubled steadily for several periods, score the trend, not the absolute number. If there is no steady doubling, this framework does not apply and the small number is just small.
- **Quote:** "My reaction was, no, we're almost done... I mean, 1%, you're pretty much finished." (S035)
- **Sources:** 9 (S002, S009, S014, S015, S034, S035, S037, S038, S039)

## 7. Two-step prediction *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** His forecasting method. First, follow the exponential, which he says is easy. Second, estimate what level of capability a given milestone requires. Where the two meet is the date. He set AGI at 2029 in 1999 this way.
- **Parts:** Pick the measure; extend the curve; estimate the threshold the milestone needs; read off the year; revisit as the estimate of the threshold improves.
- **When to use:** When a plan depends on a capability arriving ("once models can do X, we win").
- **How to score:** High if the founder can state the threshold in measurable terms and show when the curve crosses it; low if the timing is a hope. His own misses (self-driving cars, robots, 3D printing) came from step two, not step one, so weight the threshold estimate heavily.
- **Quote:** "I figured that this chart would continue, which it has. And I figured we'd need about a trillion calculations per second to do AGI. So I estimated 2029." (S009)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S009, S010, S032, S033)

## 8. Predict the trend, not the winner *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Chaotic inputs, predictable outputs. Like gas laws that predict the aggregate from unpredictable molecules, the curve predicts capability from millions of messy projects. It cannot say which company, product, or standard wins.
- **Parts:** Forecast the measure with confidence; treat any claim about a specific winner as uncertain; build so you benefit from the trend whoever wins.
- **When to use:** When a founder is betting the company on one vendor, model provider, chip maker, or standard.
- **How to score:** High if the plan benefits from the trend under several winners; low if it needs one named company to stay ahead.
- **Quote:** "Predicting specific projects is indeed not feasible. But the result of the overall complex, chaotic evolutionary process of technological progress is predictable." (S016)
- **Sources:** 7 (S015, S016, S032, S035, S038, S040, S041)

## 9. Deflation plus new applications *(unnamed: the 50% deflation and 18% growth pair)*
- **What it is:** Information technology gets about twice as good for the same cost every year, yet spending on it has grown about 18% a year in constant dollars for 50 years, because each new price point opens applications that did not exist before.
- **Parts:** Expect the unit price of the input to halve yearly; ask what becomes possible at the next price point; build for the demand that the lower price creates, not for the current buyers.
- **When to use:** Pricing, margin, and market-size decisions in anything built on compute or data. Also when a founder fears the input getting cheap.
- **How to score:** High if the business model gets better as the input gets cheaper; low if it depends on a scarce, expensive input staying scarce and expensive.
- **Quote:** "we've had 18% growth in every form of Information Technology in constant currency over the last 50 years despite the fact you can get twice as much of it every year for the same cost." (S039)
- **Sources:** 6 (S016, S034, S039, S041, S044, S014)

## 10. Once it is information, it goes exponential *(unnamed: the information technology test)*
- **What it is:** A field follows the law only after it becomes an information technology. Medicine was hit or miss until the genome was decoded; now biology is software and moves on the curve. Razor blades and other physical goods do not, until they become information (3D printing, molecular manufacturing).
- **Parts:** Ask whether the core of the product is information; if yes, assume doubling; if no, assume normal industrial progress; watch for the moment a field crosses over.
- **When to use:** When a founder is deciding whether a market (health, energy, manufacturing, food) will move at software speed.
- **How to score:** High if the bet is on a field that has crossed over and is measurably doubling; low if the founder assumes software speed in a field that is still physical, regulated, or clinical.
- **Quote:** "Now that it's an information technology, it's going to grow exponentially. Doubling in power every year." (S037)
- **Sources:** 8 (S015, S021, S029, S034, S037, S039, S040, S041)

## 11. Double exponential growth
- **What it is:** The rate of exponential growth is itself rising, because of the second level of investment and because each paradigm is faster than the last. He says the paradigm shift rate doubles about every decade, so the 21st century holds about 20,000 years of progress at the 2000 rate.
- **Parts:** Faster doubling times over time; shorter adoption cycles; shorter business model lifespans.
- **When to use:** When estimating how long a window stays open or how fast a competitor can catch up.
- **How to score:** Assume windows close faster than they did in the last cycle. A plan that needs a five-year moat in a fast field scores low.
- **Quote:** "there's exponential growth and the rate of exponential growth so there's at least double exponential growth" (S042)
- **Sources:** 6 (S014, S019, S024, S025, S041, S042)

## 12. Technology life cycle: rich and broken, then cheap and good, then free *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** New technologies start out affordable only to the wealthy and do not work well. Then they work well and are cheap. Then they are almost free. Cell phones took 10 years for the first billion users and 8 months for the fourth (S039).
- **Parts:** Place the product on the cycle; price for that phase; plan for the price drop and the mass market that follows.
- **When to use:** Choosing between a premium early-adopter product and a mass-market one; deciding when to cut price.
- **How to score:** High if the plan moves down the cycle on purpose; low if it assumes premium pricing lasts or that the poor and the global market will never be customers.
- **Quote:** "Technologies start out affordable only by the wealthy, but at this stage, they actually don't work very well... Then they work quite well and are inexpensive. Ultimately, they're almost free." (S016)
- **Sources:** 6 (S016, S034, S035, S039, S040, S041)

## 13. Hype cycle versus adoption curve *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Capital markets swing; adoption does not. In the dot-com crash, investors got the timing wrong while internet adoption kept growing on its exponential. Booms are an early, accurate signal of a real transformation, not proof it is fake.
- **Parts:** Separate the funding mood from the usage curve; judge by usage; expect a premature hype phase and an apparent failure before maturity.
- **When to use:** Raising in a hot or cold market; deciding whether a crash in a sector means the sector is over.
- **How to score:** High if the plan tracks real adoption data and survives a funding winter; low if it depends on the current mood of investors in either direction.
- **Quote:** "Meanwhile there was slow and steady but exponential growth in the adoption of these Technologies" (S038)
- **Sources:** 4 (S025, S034, S038, S041)

## 14. Business model lifespan *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Every business model has a finite life, and that life gets shorter as change speeds up. In his speech recognition company, the version before the latest sold for a quarter of the price and versions two generations old could not be given away (S014). Political power inside a company sits with the old model because it makes today's profit (S025).
- **Parts:** Measure how long your model has lasted; assume the next one lasts less; empower internal disruptors but test them against a realistic model of the curve.
- **When to use:** When a profitable incumbent product competes for resources with a new one; when a founder treats today's model as permanent.
- **How to score:** High if the company is already building its replacement; low if all resources protect the current model.
- **Quote:** "an organization cannot be devoted to one business model" (S025)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S014, S025, S040)

## 15. Promise versus peril, with relinquishment at the right level
- **What it is:** Technology is a double-edged sword, like fire. The answer to real danger is targeted rules, defenses, and rapid response, not banning whole fields, which pushes the work underground and away from the people who would build defenses.
- **Parts:** Name the specific harm; ban or restrict the specific design, not the field; build the defense (a technological immune system) early; keep the benefits.
- **When to use:** Safety, compliance, and regulation decisions in AI, biotech, or any dual-use product; responding to calls for a pause.
- **How to score:** High if the plan names concrete risks and concrete mitigations and still ships; low if it either ignores risk or freezes the whole effort over it.
- **Quote:** "Abandonment of broad areas of technology will only push these technologies underground where development would continue unimpeded by ethics or regulation." (S020)
- **Sources:** 13 (S004, S011, S012, S014, S016, S017, S020, S029, S034, S037, S038, S041, S042)

## 16. The social acceptance bar *(unnamed; his self-named biggest forecasting error)*
- **What it is:** A new technology has to be much better than the human or incumbent it replaces before society accepts it. Anything that touches the human body or public safety also meets heavier regulation. He says this is why his self-driving car date was wrong.
- **Parts:** Ask how much better than the incumbent the product is; add time for regulation and trust when it touches bodies, money, or safety.
- **When to use:** Products that replace a human (drivers, doctors, advisors) or that need regulatory approval.
- **How to score:** High if the product is clearly far better, not just equal, and the plan budgets time for approval and trust; low if "as good as a human" is the pitch.
- **Quote:** "It has to be a lot better than human beings. If it's really just equal, maybe a little bit better, we're not going to accept it." (S006)
- **Sources:** 4 (S002, S003, S006, S008)

## 17. Merge with it, do not compete with it *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** AI comes from people and will become part of how people think, as phones and calculators did. A separate AI will surpass people; an integrated one makes them more capable. For a business, this means building AI into the work rather than defending a job or product against it.
- **Parts:** Treat AI as an amplifier; put it inside the product and the team; target domains where AI has not been applied yet.
- **When to use:** When a founder asks whether AI threatens their business, their staff, or their category.
- **How to score:** High if the plan uses AI to make its people and product much better; low if it bets on AI not arriving in its category.
- **Quote:** "if AI was something separate from us uh it's definitely going to do everything that go Way Beyond what humans can do uh so we really have to merge with them to make ourselves smarter." (S036)
- **Sources:** 10 (S001, S003, S004, S007, S009, S010, S012, S032, S036, S037)

## 18. The power of the right idea *(unnamed; his grandfather's philosophy)*
- **What it is:** For any problem, business or personal, an idea exists that will let you prevail; you can find it, and once found you must implement it. He pairs this with two kinds of people: those who accept problems and those who refuse to (S027).
- **Parts:** Refuse to accept the problem as fixed; look for the idea; break it into sub-problems (S043 mentions about 50); implement.
- **When to use:** When a founder is stuck and the team has started to treat a constraint as permanent.
- **How to score:** This is a stance more than a test. It raises the score of a plan that attacks the constraint and lowers one that accepts it, but it is no evidence the idea exists.
- **Quote:** "no matter what quandaries we face... there exists an idea that will enable us to prevail. Furthermore, we can find that idea. And when we find it, we need to implement it." (S028)
- **Sources:** 5 (S012, S026, S027, S028, S043)

## 19. The invention process: describe it as if it exists *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Imagine the thing you want to build already exists and explain how it works; work back from that explanation to the steps. He talks about projects in the present tense to give himself and the team confidence. He also assigns himself a problem before sleep and revisits it on waking (S043).
- **Parts:** Write the finished-product explanation; extract the steps; break into sub-problems; work each.
- **When to use:** Early design and team alignment on an ambitious product.
- **How to score:** Not a scoring tool. Use it to test whether the founder can describe the finished product concretely.
- **Quote:** "put yourself in the position what you're trying to create already exists and then you're explaining like how it works" (S033)
- **Sources:** 2 (S033, S043)

## 20. Bridges to longevity (bridge one gets you to bridge two)
- **What it is:** His health strategy, and his most repeated idea outside technology. Bridge one is what you do now (health, supplements, exercise); bridge two is biotechnology; bridge three is medical nanobots. The only goal of each bridge is to reach the next. His dates moved: by the end of the 2020s in 2018 (S034), around 2029 in 2024 (S010), and about 2032 in 2024 and 2025 (S004, S006, S011, newer).
- **Parts:** Survive the current phase in good shape; do not optimize the current bridge beyond what gets you to the next one.
- **When to use:** Mostly off-topic for founders. The business version: a bridge product or bridge financing whose job is only to keep the company alive until the next capability arrives.
- **How to score:** High if the bridge plan is cheap and clearly ends at a dated next step; low if the bridge becomes the business by accident. Treat his health dates as his optimism, not as evidence.
- **Quote:** "the goal of bridge one is to get to bridge two" (S034)
- **Sources:** 13 (S001, S003, S004, S006, S008, S009, S010, S011, S012, S016, S034, S037, S043)
