# Positions: Ray Kurzweil

39 topics, built from the batch notes on the full deep corpus. Each stance has a verbatim quote, source id, and date (essay month, or upload date for talks; many talks are undated, and an approximate year is given only where the talk itself shows it). Where he shifted, the versions sit side by side with dates, newest marked. Topics with no evidence say so. He speaks far more about technology timing than about running a company, so several classic founder topics are thin; the deep corpus added real evidence on debt, early adopters, team dynamics, and IP.

## Timing a product or company
- **Stance:** Timing is the main reason inventions win or lose. Most inventors get the gadget to work and still fail because they launch at the wrong point on the curve. This is the center of his business view and has not changed in 40 years.
- **Over time:**
  - 2003-04: "Inventing is a lot like surfing, you have to anticipate and catch the wave at just the right moment." (S045)
  - 2009-11: "most inventors fail because their timing is wrong" (S039)
  - about 2010: "Most inventions and inventors fail, not because they are unable to get their gadgets to work, but because their timing is wrong" (S015)
  - 2018-11: "the key to being successful as an inventor was timing yet to be in the right place at the right time." (S034)
  - undated talk on entrepreneurship: "the key to being successful is not just getting your gadgets to work but timing you have to be in the right place at the right time" (S102)
  - about 2012: it reaches past tech: "timing is important for just about anything whether it's a business investment or romance" (S091)
- **Read:** futurism was a side effect of this; he studied curves to time his own projects. "i wasn't inherently a futurist" (S033, 2022-09). Inferred from S015, S033, S034, S037, S039, S045, S048, S052, S091, S101, S102, S103.

## Build for the ship date, not today
- **Stance:** Plan the product for the world that will exist when you finish it. Write down every technology your business depends on and project each one three or four years forward. He calls this his primary lesson to companies he mentors.
- 2003-05: "it's also not a good idea to just target today's world, because windows can be closed by the time you finish a project." (S025)
- 2012-11: "my project has to make sense when I finish the project, and the world will be a different place two, three, four years from now." (S035)
- about 2012: "you can't shoot at the Target where it is it's like ski shooting you have to figure out where it's going to be" (S091)
- undated panel: "this is my primary lesson" and "write down the technologies that affect your business and where will they be in three or four years" (S095); "I continually see plans that assume the world's three or four years from now will be the same as we see today." (S095)
- undated: "I had to think what is the world going to be like 3 years from now." (S101)
- Same rule in S015, S046, S073, S090.

## Too early versus too late
- **Stance:** Both kill you. Aim to be first, a few years ahead of feasibility, not a decade ahead. In 2003 he said premature projects were the more common failure (S025, paraphrase).
- 2003-05: "Most projects fail, not because the R&D department can't get it to work, but because the timing is wrong." (S025)
- undated, WSJ startup chat: "timing is absolutely critical uh you want to be first but not you know 10 years ahead of your time" (S090)
- **Note:** the line "being too early is often the same as being wrong" in S032 is the interviewer's, not his. Do not attribute it to him.

## Enabling factors and market readiness
- **Stance:** A product takes off only when every enabling piece is in place at once: core technology, supporting devices, network, content, price. Check all of them, not just your own part. The founder's drive is not the limit; the curve is.
- 2010-02: "technologies really don't take off until all the enabling factors are in place." (S040) E-books existed a decade before tablets, wireless, and digitized books arrived (S040).
- 2011-04: "90% of the projects the R&D teams are do exactly what they say in terms of technological achievement and 95% of the projects still fail" (S038)
- 2020-01: "Most inventors fail not because they can't get something to work, but because all the market's enabling forces are not right place, right time." (S048)
- undated: social networks existed years before Facebook, but "the price performance wasn't there" (S101).

## Hype cycles and bubbles
- **Stance:** A boom and bust is a capital markets timing error, not proof the technology failed. Enthusiasts see the revolution early and expect it too soon; a bust follows; the real wave comes when the parameters arrive. Find which phase you are in before you dismiss or chase something.
- 2003-05: "The investment got ahead of itself, and people were willing to invest in unproven ideas." (S025)
- 2010-06: "the adoption was going on at an exponential pace" (S041)
- 2011-04: "that was strictly a capital markets phenomenon" (S038)
- undated: "generally there's an early period where enthusiasts see the revolution and but they overanticipate it." (S095) He applied the same cycle to 3D printing (S034, S088, S092) and warned in 2014 that it was still years from mainstream (S092).

## Recessions and macro conditions
- **Stance:** Do not change a technology plan because of a recession or a war. The price-performance curve did not bend in the Great Depression, the world wars, or 2008.
- 2002-02: "recessions including the Great Depression can be modeled as a fairly weak cycle on top of the underlying exponential growth." (S050)
- 2009-11: "the Great Depression had no impact the current economic downturn has no impact it's continuing unabated." (S039)
- 2005 Q and A, posted 2024-02: "The exponential growth of information technologies proceeded smoothly through war and peace, through depression and prosperity." (S016)
- Same in S035, S041, S089, S101.

## Pricing and discounting
- **Stance:** In an information technology market, value decays fast. Last year's version sells at a fraction of the price, two generations back sells for nothing. Price against the curve and keep shipping the newest version. Total spending still grows because new applications open as prices fall.
- 2001 essay, posted 2025-01: "we would be able to sell the version prior to the latest version for perhaps a quarter of the price of the current version." (S014); same story in S045 (2003-04).
- 2009-11: "we've had 18% growth in every form of Information Technology in constant currency over the last 50 years" (S039)
- **Discounting:** no recorded position on discounts as a sales tactic. Nearest: falling cost is normal and good. "there's nothing wrong with deflation" (S044, 1999 interview, uploaded 2007-01)

## Cost curves and deflation
- **Stance:** Every information technology gets about half as expensive per unit of capability each year. Assume your inputs get cheap, and so must your product. Measure in capability per dollar, not in dollars. Once a field turns into information (biology, energy, manufacturing), it joins the curve.
- 2009-11: "a 50% deflation rate in every form of Information Technology not just computation not just Electronics." (S039)
- 2005 Q and A, posted 2024-02: "Technologies start out affordable only by the wealthy, but at this stage, they actually don't work very well" (S016)
- undated: "it's a mistake to just measure economies in dollars" (S102)
- 2024-02: "a doubling of capability (for the same cost) about each year, that applies to any info-tech." (S071)
- 2025-06 (newest): AI capability for the same cost now grows about tenfold a year. "the amount of increase that we've seen uh hardware time software right now is about 10fold per year" (S006) Asked for the combined hardware and software rate: "right now it's actually multiplying about 10 each year." (S006)

## Raising money
- **Stance:** Thin but real. Prove it works in prototype form and win enthusiastic early adopters before you raise to scale. Prefer equity from investors who believe in the vision. Remember capital markets run ahead of real adoption. No stated view on how much to raise or at what valuation.
- undated, WSJ startup chat: "before you scale up you should have something that's working at least in a par type form and have early adopters uh who are enthusiastic about it" (S090)
- 2003-05: the dot com money "got ahead of itself" (S025).
- 2026-01: his son works in venture and "all of his investments are in AI" (S003)

## Debt and equity
- **Stance:** Avoid debt in an early company because it can end the company before the curve arrives. Take equity from believers. No recorded position on cofounder splits or employee equity.
- undated, WSJ startup chat: "I would avoid Deb actually debt it's very uh is dangerous if that can lead to a premature termination of your company" (S090)
- **Read:** this fits his timing view: a project that is right but early needs runway, and debt shortens it (inferred from S090, S025).

## First hires and team makeup
- **Stance:** Innovation is interdisciplinary, so hire an expert for each area and mix fields. Hire from the people you serve. Build forecasting into the team.
- 2003-05: "one thing that you need is experts in each of those areas." (S025)
- 2001-08: cross-disciplinary meshing is the most crucial element of modern technology development (S070, paraphrase).
- 2010-06: "I now have a team of 10 people and we gather data in many different fields" (S041)
- 2025-10: "I worked with the National Federation of Blind to hire eight blind engineers" (S012)

## Group dynamics, cofounders, and firing
- **Stance:** About half of startups that had a good idea, a market, and the skills still die from group dynamics. Treat team fit as a survival factor. His longtime partner Aaron Kleiner studied this; the principles he names are collaborative leadership, a flat hierarchy, and feedback that runs up as well as down (S090, paraphrase).
- undated, WSJ startup chat: "probably half of the companies so really had a good idea a market uh they had the technological skills to carry it out" and then were "really destroyed by group dynamics that wasn't working" (S090)
- **Firing:** no recorded position.

## Focus versus diversification
- **Stance:** Do not tie the company to one business model. Business model lifespans are shrinking, so measure how long yours will last and plan the next one. Empower internal disruptors but test them against a realistic model.
- 2003-05: "an organization cannot be devoted to one business model." (S025) He credits Microsoft's stability to Gates's paranoia about relying on any one model (S025).

## Incumbents and disruption
- **Stance:** Incumbents struggle because the old model holds the power. Empower the people who disrupt from inside, and move to the new model early instead of defending the old one.
- 2003-05: "the old-line business model has all of the political power because it is producing the profits and the revenue." (S025)
- 2010-02: he praised publishers for taking the lead on ebooks, "unlike the music industry that tried to keep the same business model that was the business model when my father was a 10 year old" (S040)
- **Note:** the line about publishers asking "what job do we do for our readers" in S040 is Tim O'Reilly's, not his. Do not attribute it to him.

## Choosing a niche or first customer
- **Stance:** Take a working technology to the people who need it most and let them tell you what to build. Start with enthusiastic early adopters, then scale.
- 2025-10: the reading machine came from a blind man he met on a plane; Stevie Wonder's request became the synthesizer company (S012).
- undated: find a real human problem that fits what you are already building (S103, paraphrase).
- undated: "have early adopters uh who are enthusiastic about it" before scaling (S090).
- Inferred from S012, S090, S103. No stated rule on niche size.

## Marketing and selling
- **Stance:** Thin. Rethinking what a technology is for can matter as much as the invention; he cites Sony turning the transistor into a personal radio (S070, 2001-08). Conviction is the base of selling. No recorded position on content marketing or paid ads.
- undated: "you can't sell something that you don't believe in" (S103)
- He launched the reading machine with a live Today Show demo (S012) but draws no marketing rule from it.

## Side projects
- **No recorded position.**

## Brand and reputation
- **Stance:** Reputation and liability make large vendors more trustworthy for AI.
- 2025-03: "if you use a large language model it should be from a larger company uh so they're concerned about their reputation and their liability." (S011)
- 2025-05: "they are protective of their reputation as well as their liability" (S007)

## IP, secrecy, and sharing ideas
- **Stance:** Share ideas openly; execution, not secrecy, decides who wins. Choose patents or trade secrets by whether infringement could be seen and proven: patents suit features that reveal themselves (an interface), trade secrets suit hidden methods (S090, paraphrase). Patent and copyright terms are too long for exponential cycles (S096, paraphrase).
- undated: "typically it's your own execution not competition that determines your success" (S086) He cites Google open-sourcing TensorFlow and gaining improvements and talent (S086).

## Using AI in the business
- **Stance:** Every business should be AI first, and the opening for new AI businesses is the widest it has been.
- 2010-02: "every product you pick up now has ai at many different levels in its design and its manufacturing" (S040)
- 2026-01: "the opportunities to create a new business and do things uh that have not been done before is actually uh is higher than it's ever been before." (S003)
- 2026-06 (newest): asked what kind of company will survive into the singularity, the answer on stage was to put AI and robots first and be able to change very fast (inferred from S002; the captions do not make clear which panelist said it).
- About 2025: "I think to avoid AI is uh really a mistake" (S077)

## Which AI vendor or chip to bet on
- **Stance:** Bet on the curve, not on one vendor. Leaders change, and there will be more model choice, not less. He worries about open weights being cheap to tune for harm.
- 2024-03 (S032): "[Nvidia is] very well positioned, but we didn't hear about them ten years ago, and other people may overtake them." (S032); "The public ones are the most impactful because everybody can use them" (S032)
- 2025-06: "I think there'll be even more choice than there is today" (S006)
- undated debate with Hinton: he sides with Hinton's concern about open-sourcing large models and does not advocate it (S080, paraphrase).

## AI reliability and hallucinations
- **Stance:** Solvable, and shrinking every year as models get more data and learn when they do not know. Judge the trend, not the snapshot.
- 2024-03: models still answer when they do not know. "the way to fix hallucinations is to actually give it more more capabilities to memorize things and and give it more information so it knows the answer" (S013)
- 2025-03 (newer): "you compare hallucinations today to one year ago is dramatically better." (S011)
- Same in S081, S082.

## AGI and the timeline
- **Stance:** Human-level AI by 2029, a date he has held since 1999. He now calls it conservative but rejects dates much earlier.
- **Over time:**
  - 1999 to 2010: 2029 for Turing-level AI (S015, S022, S050).
  - 2024-10: "I think saying by the end of of the of this decade is conservative it could happen faster" (S004)
  - undated, about 2024: "I've technically gone from being an optimist to a pessimist without changing my prediction at all." (S084)
  - about 2025: "I did predict that it would be here in 2029. Um, but I also said that uh there'd be a variance. Some people would say it's here." (S077)
  - 2026-06 (newest): some people now say AGI comes this year or next, and 2029 is only three years away, so his date now reads as conservative (inferred from S001).
- **Read:** he did not move his date; the consensus moved toward it (inferred from S002, S010, S033, S084, S087).

## Large language models and the data limit
- **Stance:** LLMs came slightly ahead of his own schedule. Data, not compute, is now the limit. He would rename them large event models because they reach far past language.
- 2024-03: "I was expecting this that to happen like a couple of years later." (S008)
- 2024-03 (S032): "I think we have the calculations. We don't have the data." (S032)
- 2024-10: "the large language models should be called large event models" (S004)
- Earlier, about deep learning, he cites the connectionist motto that "life begins at a billion examples" (S093)

## Robotics
- **Stance:** Behind software AI, catching up soon. Physical tasks are about half the economy, so the market is huge.
- 2026-01: "I think that's going to begin to take place in 2026, 2027." (S003)
- undated, about 2024: "these physic tasks comprise approximately half our economy" (S082)
- 2026-06 (newest): "The robots were a little bit behind on. It's going to catch up rapidly." (S001)

## Quantum computing
- **Stance changed.** 2023-07: cautiously interested, though his law "doesn't have much to say about it" because its growth is not on a schedule (S062). 2024 to 2026 (newer): skeptical; errors are unsolved and classical computing is enough.
- 2024-06: "I don't see us needing quantum computing: we've never been able to demonstrate its value." (S075)
- 2025-06: "I'm not a big fan of quantum computing" (S006); same in S001 (2026-06) and S078.

## Social acceptance of new technology
- **Stance:** A new product has to beat the incumbent by a wide margin before people accept it. He names this as his biggest forecasting error, and says his misses cluster where regulation, culture, or markets set the pace.
- 2025-06: "It has to be a lot better than human beings. If it's really just equal, maybe a little bit better, we're not going to accept it." (S006)
- 2025-06: "I didn't take into consideration uh the amount of additional uh positivity that would be required to accept them." (S006)
- 2026-06: on self-driving cars, "that was incorrect." (S002) Regulation around the body moves slowest (S008, 2024-03). Predictions tied to regulatory and market variables were off by years (S087, S092).
- 2003-02: people adopt in steps: "people didn't throw away their typewriters when the first generation of word processors was introduced." (S055)

## Regulation, pauses, and risk
- **Stance:** Manage specific dangers with targeted rules, ethics guidelines, and defensive technology built ahead of the threat; never ban a whole field, which only drives it underground.
- 2000 essay, posted 2024-01: "Abandonment of broad areas of technology will only push these technologies underground" (S020)
- 2023-01: against the AI pause letter: "this criterion is too vague to be practical." (S017)
- 2025-03: "threats of AI are real and serious. Um, but it's not an alien invasion." (S011)
- He points to the 1975 Asilomar biotech guidelines as the working model (S087).

## Competition
- **Stance:** You cannot predict which company wins, only the capability curve everyone rides. Your own execution matters more than rivals.
- 2005 Q and A, posted 2024-02: "Predicting which company or product will succeed is indeed very difficult, if not impossible." (S016)
- undated: in search "there were 30 projects to do that." (S102)
- undated: "typically it's your own execution not competition that determines your success" (S086)

## Metrics and forecasting
- **Stance:** Measure the underlying price-performance numbers (cost per computation, per base pair, per bit), fit the curve with data, then estimate what capability your milestone needs. Do not trust intuition, his own included.
- 2010-06: "being an engineer I gathered a lot of data" (S041)
- 2026-06: "To make predictions you have to do two things. You have to be able to follow the exponential growth which is actually not difficult" (S001)
- 2005 Q and A, posted 2024-02: "Predicting specific projects is indeed not feasible." (S016)
- about 2017: "I still have a linear intuition." (S085)
- Grade the whole record, not the misses: "it is easy to focus on the few predictions that were incorrect" (S015)

## Speed of decisions and agility
- **Stance:** Decide fast and build to change fast.
- 2026-06: "I said yes right away cuz I make important decisions very quickly." (S002)
- 2026-06: on what company survives the singularity, the stage answer was agility, because things will change in weeks, not years (inferred from S002; speaker unclear in the captions).

## High tech versus low tech businesses
- **Stance:** A high tech business rides the compounding wave; a low tech one can still innovate but has no tailwind.
- undated, WSJ startup chat: "With low Tech you don't have that that doesn't mean you can't come up with new ideas" (S090)
- about 2017: invest where a field is "now at the steep part of the curve" (S094), naming AI, biotech, and virtual reality.
- On bitcoin, undated: "I wouldn't put my money into it" (S087)

## Remote work and geography
- **Stance:** Geography matters less every year.
- 2012-03: "geography matters less and less because we have virtual communication that allows people to work anywhere" (S043)
- 2003-05: venture capital, once American, has spread worldwide, which he sees as good (S025, paraphrase).

## Work hours, sleep, and burnout
- **Stance:** No rule on hours. Sleep is creative work time, not lost time. On burnout, the nearest is resilience.
- 2012-03: "I assign myself a problem before I go to sleep every night" (S043)
- 2011-01: "you have to build-up emotional resilience." (S027)

## Failure and optimism
- **Stance:** Optimism and confidence are working tools, and failure is a delay, not a verdict. A culture that calls failure experience is a precondition for innovation.
- 2011-01: "failure is just success deferred" (S027); same phrase in S090 and S094.
- 2025-10: "Optimism is not an idle speculation. It's a self-fulfilling prophecy." (S012)
- undated: "a setback is not something that's going to make me fail but just take longer" (S103)
- about 2017: "in Silicon Valley, we have a word for failure, which is experience" (S094)

## Selling the company or joining a giant
- **Stance:** No stated rule. In AI, who owns the compute and data can change the deal, and he took that path twice: Google bought Patterns Inc. two weeks after he started it, and he joined Google because the work needed its scale.
- 2024-10: Larry Page offered to buy rather than invest (S004).
- undated, 2014: the project needed a million computers and web-scale language data no independent company had (S092, paraphrase); same in S097, S101.

## Culture
- **Stance:** Hire people who anticipate change and question assumptions, give internal disruptors power, keep the hierarchy flat, and accept failure as experience (inferred from S025, S027, S090, S095).

## What to work on (career advice)
- **Stance:** Follow what excites you, not what pays. Unchanged for 40 years.
- 2024-03: "I'd be less concerned about what will make money and much more concerned about what turns them on." (S008)
- 2025-06: "follow the same advice that I'd give like 40 years ago, which is follow your passion" (S006)
- 2012-03: "it takes passion to create knowledge that has value" (S043)

## Jobs, automation, and UBI
- **Stance:** Automation removes jobs at the bottom and creates better ones at the top; a baseline income arrives in the 2030s.
- 2024-10: "80% of people uh worked in food production uh 200 years ago today there 2% so we've lost all those jobs" (S004)
- about 2017: "I happen to support the concept of UBI universal basic income as a uh kind of a cushion" (S094)
- 2026-06 (newest): "I think we'll have UBI." (S001)
