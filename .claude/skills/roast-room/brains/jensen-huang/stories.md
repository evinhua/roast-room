# Stories: Jensen Huang

Built from the extraction notes on 104 sources. 20 stories and cases he reuses, with the lesson he draws. Most retold first. Source ids in parentheses. Keynote retellings repeat the same few stories many times; the counts show which ones he leans on.

1. **The DGX-1 nobody bought.** In 2016 he built the first deep learning supercomputer, announced it to silence and confusion, found no buyers, and hand-delivered the first unit to a small San Francisco nonprofit called OpenAI after Elon Musk said he could use one. Every OpenAI model since has trained on Nvidia. Lesson: the market rarely sees a platform shift at launch; build it anyway, and the first customer is often the one whose mission needs it to exist. "I hand delivered the very first dgx-1 to a startup located in San Francisco called open AI" (S043) "And so we built it anyway." (S098) (S003, S005, S034, S039, S040, S043, S044, S047, S049, S073, S074, S077, S095, S098)
2. **CUDA on GeForce.** In 2006 and 2007 Nvidia put CUDA on every consumer card. No customer asked for it, it ate the gross margin, and the market value fell to a few billion dollars. He toured universities pitching it for seismic work, molecular dynamics, and chemistry, one field at a time, for about ten years until AlexNet. He thought it would be an overnight success; it took almost twenty years. Lesson: a platform bet means years of cost before the market sees it, so build the install base and hold on. "we risked everything to pursue deep learning" (S035) (S001, S003, S011, S016, S035, S038, S039, S040, S041, S043, S073)
3. **AlexNet and how far it can go.** Hinton, Krizhevsky, and Sutskever trained AlexNet on two GeForce gaming cards and beat decades of hand-coded vision work. Andrew Ng, Hinton, and LeCun had already reached out for GPU help, and Nvidia went looking for every AI researcher on the planet and built cuDNN for them, though they had no money. He asked not how good it was but how far it could scale, and rebuilt the company's whole stack around the answer. Lesson: watch the people doing important work at the edge, and judge a breakthrough by whether it scales, not by its current quality. "we asked ourselves you know, how far can AlexNet go?" (S005) (S004, S005, S017, S021, S040, S043, S059, S064, S071, S099, S102, S105)
4. **The radiologist prediction.** A leading AI scientist (Hinton) said radiologists would be obsolete within five years. Now AI is in nearly all radiology and there are more radiologists, because they read more scans and hospitals take more patients. He adds that the prediction hurt recruiting into the field for nothing. Lesson: automating a task is not eliminating a job when the purpose of the job grows; understand the purpose before forecasting the job. (S001, S009, S018, S019, S022, S025, S026, S066, S067, S072)
5. **Sega and the wrong architecture.** Nvidia spent about two and a half years building on the wrong architecture (curved surfaces, forward texture mapping, no Z-buffer) while under contract for Sega's next console. Finishing would have taken Sega down the wrong path too. Jensen flew to Japan, told Sega's CEO the truth, asked to be let out of the contract and still be paid, and Sega agreed. The money built the Riva 128. Lesson: admit the mistake early and ask for help; a strategic retreat saved the company. "Intellectual honesty and humility saved our company and how a retreat a strategic retreat was one of our best strategies." (S034) (S002, S003, S034, S035, S036, S050, S070)
6. **The founding pitch.** In 1993 the plan needed technology, a market, and an ecosystem that did not exist, and 3D games were close to a zero market. Asked about the killer app, he named Electronic Arts; Don Valentine had backed EA and said its CTO was 14. Asked about market size, he said every human would be a gamer someday. Wilf Corrigan of LSI Logic told Valentine to fund him anyway. He put the opportunity at about $300 million. Lesson: your track record does the selling, and the best markets are the ones you badly undersize at the start. "I wouldn't fund myself today." (S029) (S017, S028, S029, S041, S073)
7. **Leaving the phone market.** Nvidia's Tegra phone chips grew to about a billion dollars, then the 3G to 4G shift made the modem decide the winner, Qualcomm owned the modem, and Nvidia was shut out. In 2023 and 2024 he told it as a chosen retreat into robotics, "to retreat from a giant phone market to create a zero billion dollar robotics Market" (S035). In the undated Stanford talk he goes further: entering was the mistake, because he could have seen he would be shut out after a couple of years. The expertise went into Thor, the robotics chip. Lesson: before entering, forecast who controls the piece that decides the winner (S034, S035, S071, S073).
8. **The Riva 128 tape-out.** With months of cash and dozens of rivals, he spent half the remaining money on an emulator from a company going out of business, tested the whole chip and its drivers in software, and sent it straight to production without a prototype. It worked. Lesson: when failure is the default, take the big bet, but simulate the downside away first. "If I could start the production, I might have a chance." (S003) (S003, S004)
9. **Reinventing at the peak.** While Hopper sold at its prime, Nvidia bet the next system on NVLink 72, which partners called too hard; the first full-size NVLink design years earlier had been too big, and he kept the idea and reworked it. Earlier, in 2018, RTX reinvented the GPU, the software, and the algorithms at once: "I think it's safe to say that it was worth it to bet the company." (S103) Lesson: reinvent while you are winning, and keep an architecture idea that is right even if the first build is wrong (S015, S037, S038, S096, S103).
10. **The programmable shader gap.** Nvidia saw graphics would be commoditized by integrated chips unless it opened the canvas, so it invented programmable shaders. Applications had not caught up, so the chip cost too much for two painful quarters. "you created something for the future, which means that the current applications don't benefit. Until you have new applications, your chip is just too expensive." (S047) Lesson: building for the future opens a margin gap before the market fills it; plan to survive the gap (S047, S048).
11. **Self-driving took far longer than planned.** Nvidia started building self-driving cars around 2017, not because customers asked but to learn what robotic systems would need from chips, infrastructure, and models. He expected two or three hundred engineers; seven years in he had 3,000 and saw at least four more years to basic deployment. Lesson: a long platform bet costs more people and years than you plan, and you build the full stack yourself to earn the right to help others build it (S078, S097).
12. **Intel left Nvidia alone, and warehouse computers looked silly.** Intel watched for years and rejected parallel computing because its CPU business was so successful. While the industry moved to smaller devices, Nvidia went the other way: "instead of building smaller and smaller devices, we made computers the size of warehouses." (S033) Lesson: success blinds incumbents, and being ignored is time to build the ecosystem. "people left us alone for a long time" (S024) (S024, S033)
13. **The market's doubts.** At the 1998 IPO, at about a $300 million valuation, the only question investors asked was when Nvidia would go out of business. After the financial crisis it lost about 80 percent of its value, and he went back to work the next day the same way: "you go back to just doing your job, woke up at the same time, prioritize my day in the same way." (S017) In early 2023 analysts expected about $26 billion in revenue; Nvidia did $60 billion. On BG2 it was host Brad Gerstner, not Jensen, who called that miss one of the worst forecasting failures ever; Jensen's own view is that Nvidia has no trouble beating the numbers (inferred from S007, S008) Lesson: anchor on first principles; analysts model straight lines and cannot model compounding platforms (S007, S008, S017, S074).
14. **Write-offs and hangovers.** After the 2018 crypto bust, channel inventory took two quarters to clear, not one, because buyers froze while prices fell, so Nvidia stopped shipping into the channel. In 2022, after Ethereum mining collapsed, he delayed Ada, repriced, and protected partners through two horrific quarters. In 2025, export rules banned the H20 and Nvidia wrote off $5.5 billion: "no company in history has ever written off that much inventory." (S014) Lesson: outside shocks hit without warning; take the hard medicine fast and build the company to survive them (S014, S024, S048, S057).
15. **Mellanox, Arm, and Hugging Face.** He saw that a data center is set apart by its networking, not its processor, and bought Mellanox; he spent years shaping belief inside the company first, so the call got full buy-in on the day. He tried for years to buy Arm, was blocked, paid the breakup fee, and still calls it a great idea: "We can always go make more money. It was worth a try." (S047) In 2026 he agreed to buy Hugging Face because open models matter too much to Nvidia's business to leave them in the wrong hands. Lesson: buy the piece the whole system needs next, and prepare people long before the announcement (S001, S004, S015, S047, S051, S060, S061, S065, S067, S076).
16. **The investments he missed, and the one he made.** He was invited into OpenAI early and put in too little, passed on Anthropic because he assumed VCs would fund it, and years before told Sequoia not to back Yahoo because he could not see how free search made money. But he invested in Keyhole with no revenue because he believed in 3D navigation and nobody else would fund it; it became Google Earth. Lesson: deep insight in one domain does not make you right in others, and on a platform forming once in a generation the usual error is investing too little (S002, S007, S020, S054).
17. **The textbook fix.** Facing the wrong technology and dozens of competitors, he bought the Silicon Graphics or OpenGL books, handed one to each architect, and said to read them and save the company. "And we implemented the OpenGL pipeline like nobody had ever implemented the OpenGL pipeline." (S017) Lesson: start from the best known art, then rethink it from first principles (S017, S020).
18. **Supply locked up years early.** He started working with memory suppliers when Nvidia was still a chip company, which Wall Street found puzzling. By 2026 Nvidia had used its balance sheet to secure wafers, packaging, memory, and even capacitors, so a request for a few gigawatts got the answer no problem. "I got all the memories, I got all the wafers, I got all the CoWoS, I got all the packaging" (S074) Lesson: lock in supply years before you need it (S048, S054, S074).
19. **Misuse as demand.** Researchers at Mass General were tricking graphics cards into CT reconstruction by framing it as a graphics problem. That helped inspire CUDA. Lesson: watch how people misuse your product; it shows the real demand (S005).
20. **The moss gardener.** At a Kyoto temple he met a gardener picking dead moss with bamboo tweezers in a huge garden. The gardener said he had cared for it for 25 years and had plenty of time. He repeats the phrase often. Lesson: prioritize properly and do the most important work first, and you will have time for a long project (S034, S070).
