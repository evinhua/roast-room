# Frameworks: Jensen Huang

Built from six batches of notes on 45 sources (interviews, podcasts, keynotes, commencement speeches, and earnings calls, 2011 to 2026). 24 frameworks, roughly most-used first. "Sources" counts distinct sources in the corpus where the idea is used (ids listed). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short descriptive name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from his stated thresholds and stories, not quotes.

---

## 1. The install base flywheel
- **What it is:** A computing platform is defined by how many machines already run it. Install base attracts developers, developers make breakthroughs, breakthroughs open new markets, new markets grow the install base. Nothing else, including chip elegance, substitutes for it.
- **Parts:** Get the architecture into as many hands as possible; keep every generation compatible so software written once runs everywhere; let each software gain lift the whole fleet; let the ecosystem's richness become the switching cost.
- **When to use:** Any platform, marketplace, developer tool, or product whose value grows with the number of users or builders on it. Also when a founder is tempted to chase a slicker product that breaks compatibility with what customers already run.
- **How to score:** 5 if the plan grows a base that others build on and every new customer makes the product better for the rest; 3 if there is usage but nothing compounds; 1 if each sale stands alone and a rival could take the customer with a better spec sheet.
- **Quote:** "Install base defines an architecture. Not... Everything else is secondary." (S001)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S002, S004, S008, S011, S013, S018, S028, S038)

## 2. The zero billion dollar market (market maker, not share taker)
- **What it is:** Aim at a market that does not exist yet rather than fight for share in one that does. No customers today means no competitors and nobody watching, which buys the years needed to build the platform. He says NVIDIA never talks about market share; the only question is whether the mission is necessary.
- **Parts:** Find a need that has not emerged yet; reason from first principles that it will; build the technology and the market at the same time; accept a long stretch with tiny revenue; be the first one positioned when the market forms.
- **When to use:** A founder choosing between a crowded proven market and an empty new one, or between a share-grab and a category bet.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder is creating a category with a reasoned case that it will exist and a plan to survive until it does; 3 if it is a new angle on an existing market; 1 if the pitch is to take a small slice of a big existing market with no reason the incumbents cannot respond.
- **Quote:** "I'd rather be a market maker, market creator, than a market taker." (S016)
- **Sources:** 8 (S004, S008, S016, S017, S024, S034, S035, S038)

## 3. Five-layer cake
- **What it is:** AI is a stack of five layers: energy (land, power, shell), chips, infrastructure, models, and applications. Each layer depends on the one below. He uses it to locate any AI question and to argue that you have to win every layer, not just the model.
- **Parts:** Name the layer the decision sits in; check the bottleneck in the layers below; check who captures value in the layers above; invest where a dollar unlocks many dollars of ecosystem.
- **When to use:** Any AI or infrastructure business: where to build, where the bottleneck is, which layer will be commoditized, where margins land.
- **How to score:** High if the founder knows which layer they own and what they depend on below; low if the plan assumes power, chips, or model access will simply be there.
- **Quote:** "AI is actually essentially a five layer cake." (S026)
- **Sources:** 9 (S002, S009, S014, S018, S021, S022, S025, S026, S031)

## 4. AI factory and tokens per watt
- **What it is:** The data center stopped being a warehouse for files and became a factory: energy and data go in, tokens come out, and revenue is tokens produced per watt times the price tier. Power is the binding constraint, so the quality of compute per watt decides revenue.
- **Parts:** Treat the whole system as the unit, not the chip; measure throughput and token speed; segment output into price tiers; plan land and power years ahead; never let the most expensive resource sit idle.
- **When to use:** Any compute-heavy or capacity-constrained business: unit economics, capex, pricing tiers, what to optimize when a hard input is capped.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder knows the scarce input and maximizes revenue per unit of it; 3 if they track cost but not output per constrained unit; 1 if they price inputs without asking what the factory produces.
- **Quote:** "I call them AI factories they're AI factories because it has one job and one job only generating these incredible tokens" (S037)
- **Sources:** 9 (S001, S013, S016, S023, S024, S028, S034, S037, S038)

## 5. Task versus purpose of a job
- **What it is:** Every job has a purpose (why it exists) and tasks (the work done to serve it). AI automates tasks. Where the purpose is bigger than the task, demand for the job grows; where the task is the whole job, it shrinks. His proof is radiology: AI reads scans and there are more radiologists.
- **Parts:** Name the purpose; list the tasks; ask which tasks AI removes; ask whether lower cost raises demand for the purpose.
- **When to use:** Hiring plans, headcount cuts "because AI", product positioning for tools that automate work, any claim that AI will kill a role or a market.
- **How to score:** High if the plan uses AI to serve more of the purpose (more patients, more customers, more ideas); low if it treats task automation as automatic job removal.
- **Quote:** "There's a fundamental difference between the tasks that we do in our job versus the purpose of our job." (S021)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S003, S009, S021, S022, S025, S026, S036)

## 6. Core beliefs, gut-checked daily *(unnamed; also "first principles")*
- **What it is:** Reason a long bet from first principles (physics, deep industry knowledge, or science), write down the core beliefs, and check them every day. If the beliefs have not changed, the bet does not change, whatever the stock price or the critics say. If they have, change course.
- **Parts:** State the beliefs; name the evidence that would break them; gut-check daily; ignore noise that does not touch the beliefs; course-correct when a belief breaks.
- **When to use:** A long bet under pressure: a down quarter, a skeptical board, a competitor launch, a falling valuation.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder can state the beliefs and what would falsify them; 3 if conviction is real but unstated; 1 if the bet rests on mood, momentum, or "investors like it".
- **Quote:** "Have a core belief. Gut-check it every day. Pursue it with all your might, pursue it for a very long time." (S017)
- **Sources:** 7 (S003, S005, S016, S017, S021, S034, S006)

## 7. Fund the platform with a volume application *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A new architecture has a chicken-and-egg problem. Solve it with one application that both needs the architecture and sells in enough volume to spread it. For NVIDIA that was 3D games: GeForce paid for CUDA and put it on hundreds of millions of machines before anyone used CUDA.
- **Parts:** Pick the killer application; make sure it has real demand and real volume; ride it to build the install base; let the next market (science, then AI) arrive on a base that already exists.
- **When to use:** A founder with a platform vision but no near-term buyer for the platform itself. Asks: what pays the bills while the bigger market forms?
- **How to score:** 5 if a large, paying use case funds the long bet and spreads the base; 3 if there is a paying use case but it does not spread the platform; 1 if the long bet must be funded by investors alone with no volume engine.
- **Quote:** "how do we find an application that would, on the one hand, need this architecture, on the other hand, be sufficiently high volume to cause this architecture to proliferate" (S021)
- **Sources:** 6 (S003, S004, S005, S021, S028, S038)

## 8. Do as much as needed, as little as possible
- **What it is:** Do only the work that would not get done without you; partner for everything else. This is why NVIDIA does not build fabs, does not become a cloud, and does not pick model winners. He also uses it as a work rule: reject and defer work as long as possible.
- **Parts:** For each piece of work ask: if we do not do it, will anyone? If yes, partner. If no, it is ours. Go up the stack only as far as developers need.
- **When to use:** Build versus partner, vertical integration, new business lines, scope creep.
- **How to score:** High if the founder only builds what nobody else would and leans on excellent partners for the rest; low if they are rebuilding what others already do well.
- **Quote:** "We should do as much as needed, as little as possible." (S002)
- **Sources:** 6 (S002, S011, S013, S015, S017, S028)

## 9. Extreme co-design *(full-stack acceleration)*
- **What it is:** When one component stops improving on its own (Moore's Law ending), gains come from optimizing every layer at once: algorithm, chip, system, networking, software, power. Speeding one step of a pipeline yields little (Amdahl's Law); speeding the whole cycle yields large gains.
- **Parts:** Map the whole pipeline; find every step on the critical path; optimize them together; treat the system, not the part, as the product.
- **When to use:** A founder whose product is one component in a bigger workflow, or who is chasing a 10% gain in one step while the bottleneck sits elsewhere.
- **How to score:** High if the plan improves the customer's whole cycle time; low if it polishes one step the customer barely notices.
- **Quote:** "Extreme code design means that you have to optimize the model, algorithm, system, and chip at the same time." (S007)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S007, S008, S015, S023, S038)

## 10. Org design as a computing stack, with information equity *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Design the company like the machine it builds, not like the military. He keeps 40 to 60 direct reports, no one-on-ones, no status meetings, only problem meetings where everyone hears the same reasoning at the same time.
- **Parts:** Ask what the input, output, and environment are; organize around that; flatten so information is not "mutilated" by layers; give feedback in the open so everyone learns; reserve the CEO for problems nobody else can move.
- **When to use:** Org charts, management layers, meeting cadence, how the founder spends time.
- **How to score:** High if the structure lets the right experts see every key decision fast; low if information flows through layers and power comes from who knows what.
- **Quote:** "the architecture of the company to me is a computer with a Computing stack with um uh people managing different parts of the system" (S004)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S004, S008, S011, S016, S017)

## 11. Choose work that is hard, new, and yours *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** His test for what to work on: something never done before, insanely hard (the difficulty discourages others and buys you time), something you are somehow destined to do, and something you love enough to endure the pain. NVIDIA looks for problems that are barely possible or impossible on ordinary computers.
- **Parts:** Is it new? Is it hard enough to keep others out? Do you have the perspective or skill that makes it yours? Will you still want it after years of suffering?
- **When to use:** Picking an idea, a new product line, or a pivot.
- **How to score:** 5 if all four hold; 3 if it is hard but anyone could do it; 1 if it is easy, crowded, or chosen for the money.
- **Quote:** "the person who's willing to to to suffer the longest wins" (S028)
- **Sources:** 6 (S011, S013, S016, S020, S024, S028)

## 12. Prefetch the risk before you bet the farm *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Bold bets are fine, but pull the future risks into the present first, through simulation, emulation, or testing, so that when you push all the chips in you already know it will work. The Riva 128 was simulated end to end on an emulator, then taped out once.
- **Parts:** List what could kill the bet; find a cheap way to test each now; spend on tools that compress time; commit fully only after the downside is simulated away.
- **When to use:** Any all-in decision: a big launch, a large contract, a single production run, a major hire or acquisition.
- **How to score:** High if the founder has tested the main failure modes before committing; low if the bet is bold and untested.
- **Quote:** "When you bet the farm you're saying I'm going to take everything in the future all the risky things and going to pull it in advance." (S004)
- **Sources:** 3 (S003, S004, S037)

## 13. Strategic retreat
- **What it is:** Deciding what to give up is core to success. NVIDIA walked away from the Sega console contract, then from a phone chip market where it was doing well, to focus on the mission and later robotics.
- **Parts:** Check each business against the mission; leave the ones that do not fit even if they work; confront mistakes early; reinvest the freed focus in the next platform.
- **When to use:** A founder spread across too many lines, or defending a working but off-mission business.
- **How to score:** High if the founder is willing to cut something that works to protect the main bet; low if every opportunity is kept.
- **Quote:** "strategic retreat sacrifice deciding what to give up is at the core the very core of success" (S035)
- **Sources:** 4 (S020, S034, S035, S003)
- **Note:** In S035 (2023) the phone exit is told as a choice; in S034 (2024) he says NVIDIA was "kicked out" of mobile and other markets. The choice framing is cleaner than the record.

## 14. Early indicators of future success
- **What it is:** Long bets show no financial result for years, so track leading signs that you are doing the right thing and decouple them from results. Gross margin is a result, not a KPI. For CUDA the signs were researchers using it in CT reconstruction, seismic, and molecular dynamics, then Andrew Ng's cat and AlexNet.
- **Parts:** Name signals that come before revenue (who is using it, for what important work); track them; keep enduring while they point the right way.
- **When to use:** Judging a bet that is years from paying off; deciding whether to keep funding it.
- **How to score:** 5 if the founder names specific early signs and they are moving; 3 if there are signs but no tracking; 1 if the only evidence offered is revenue that has not come or hope.
- **Quote:** "early indicators of future success... you want to decouple the result from evidence that you're doing the right thing." (S017)
- **Sources:** 4 (S005, S016, S017, S035)

## 15. Narrow enough to win, broad enough to fund R&D *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** NVIDIA walks a fine line every generation. Too general and you become an undifferentiated general-purpose processor; too narrow and the market cannot pay for the R&D. Cover enough domains to fund research while staying far faster than the general option in each.
- **Parts:** Pick domains where you are many times better than the default; add domains to grow customers and R&D; stop before you are just another generalist.
- **When to use:** Niche versus broad, horizontal versus vertical product, how many segments to serve.
- **How to score:** High if each segment gets a big advantage and together they fund the roadmap; low if the niche is too small to fund itself or the breadth erases the edge.
- **Quote:** "the flywheel of number of domains expanding the number of customers expanding the number of markets expanding the the sales which creates larger R&D" (S028)
- **Sources:** 4 (S005, S011, S020, S028)

## 16. Fungible and durable beats specialized (TCO, not price)
- **What it is:** Judge infrastructure by total cost of operation over its life, not the sticker price. Systems that run any workload and keep improving through software carry the lowest risk and best return. A specialized system that is worse per unit of the scarce input loses even if it is free.
- **Parts:** Count land, power, operations, and lifetime; ask if it runs every workload; ask if software keeps making it better; compare output per constrained unit.
- **When to use:** Pricing against a cheaper rival, buying equipment, choosing a stack, positioning a premium product.
- **How to score:** High if the founder wins on lifetime value per scarce unit; low if the pitch is "cheaper" with nothing else.
- **Quote:** "Specialization is the enemy of fungibility. Specialization is the enemy of durability." (S006)
- **Sources:** 6 (S005, S006, S007, S014, S018, S038)

## 17. Conviction with agility
- **What it is:** Believe two things equally hard at once: that you are right about the direction, and that you could be wrong about the path. Conviction alone is stubbornness; agility alone is drifting. He also runs two modes in the company at once: a refined, precise core and a shape-shifting skunkworks.
- **Parts:** Hold the direction; stay open on method; keep one part of the company refining and one part exploring.
- **When to use:** A founder who is either too rigid or pivoting every month.
- **How to score:** High if the direction is stable and tactics change on evidence; low if both are fixed or both keep moving.
- **Quote:** "you have to believe both equally hard" (S011)
- **Sources:** 3 (S003, S005, S011)

## 18. Go forward in time, look backward *(position near the tree)*
- **What it is:** Plan from the future end state back to today, not forward from current limits. You do not need to predict the exact moment; position the company near where the opportunity will fall so you are first to pick it up.
- **Parts:** Picture the end state; reason back to what must be true; position near it; be ready for the diving catch.
- **When to use:** Timing a market, deciding how early is too early, long-range roadmaps.
- **How to score:** High if the founder is positioned near a reasoned future without betting on an exact date; low if the plan needs perfect timing or only extends today's trends.
- **Quote:** "I never looked forward from where I am, I go forward in time and look backwards." (S017)
- **Sources:** 2 (S004, S017)

## 19. A unifying theory, and shaping belief before announcing it *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** The CEO's job is to hold one theory that explains why everything the company does fits together. Before any big move (going all in on deep learning, buying Mellanox) he spends months or years laying stepping stones with teams and partners, so that by the time he announces it, people wonder why it took so long (inferred from S001, S015, S037). Roadmaps shown years ahead do the same for the supply chain.
- **Parts:** State the theory; share partial reasoning early and often; brief partners and suppliers on the size and timing of the opportunity; announce once belief is in place.
- **When to use:** A big pivot, a major acquisition, a long-horizon bet that needs partners to invest alongside you.
- **How to score:** High if the team and partners already understand why; low if the founder plans a surprise memo.
- **Quote:** "The reason why Nvidia can move so fast is because we always have a unifying theory for the company, and that's my job." (S015)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S002, S015, S037)

## 20. Vertically integrated, horizontally open
- **What it is:** Build the whole stack together so it works best, then sell every layer separately so customers and partners can take any piece. Go to market through partners for most customers.
- **Parts:** Co-design the full system; disaggregate it for sale; serve customers however they want to buy; let 80% go through partners.
- **When to use:** Bundling versus unbundling, platform versus product, channel strategy.
- **How to score:** High if the founder controls the hard integration but does not force customers to buy everything; low if it is a closed bundle or a loose kit with no integration edge.
- **Quote:** "We create a thing vertically and then we open it horizontally and so everybody could use whatever piece they would like." (S015)
- **Sources:** 4 (S013, S014, S015, S038)

## 21. Cannibalize yourself; reinvent at the peak
- **What it is:** If you do not cannibalize your own product, a competitor will. NVIDIA ships a new architecture every year and bet on a hard new design while the old one was at its prime. In a fast industry, a company that is not reinventing itself is slowly dying.
- **Parts:** Plan the replacement before decline; ship on a steady rhythm; accept that the new generation will make the old one hard to sell.
- **When to use:** A founder protecting a winning product, or delaying the next version to milk the current one.
- **How to score:** High if the founder is building what replaces their best seller; low if they are guarding it.
- **Quote:** "if you don't cannibalize it someone will and it surely will be cannibalized" (S020)
- **Sources:** 3 (S020, S037, S038)

## 22. Speed of light
- **What it is:** Benchmark every plan against the physical limit, not against last year's result. Strip the process back to zero, ask what physics allows, then find the real gap. Related rule: never do twice what you can do once.
- **Parts:** Ask what the limit is (time, cost, energy, steps); ask why the current number is so far from it; rebuild from zero toward the limit.
- **When to use:** Timelines, cost structures, any claim that a task simply takes as long as it has always taken.
- **How to score:** High if the founder knows the theoretical best and is closing the gap; low if they improve 10% on an inherited process.
- **Quote:** "The speed of light is my shorthand for what's the limit of what physics can do." (S001)
- **Sources:** 2 (S001, S004)

## 23. Marginal cost to zero opens new markets *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** When the cost of a resource falls by orders of magnitude, demand rises and new uses appear that were irrational before. Cutting compute cost a million times did not shrink the market; it created AI. He expects token cost to fall about a billion times in ten years.
- **Parts:** Find a cost that is collapsing; ask what becomes rational at near-zero cost; build for that use, not the old one.
- **When to use:** Pricing a falling-cost product, sizing a market after a cost drop, fear that cheaper means smaller.
- **How to score:** High if the founder bets on the new uses cheap supply enables; low if they assume demand is fixed.
- **Quote:** "You wouldn't ever consider doing it unless the marginal cost of computing was zero." (S018)
- **Sources:** 2 (S009, S018)

## 24. Scaling laws of compute *(pre-training, post-training, test-time, agentic)*
- **What it is:** His reason demand for compute keeps rising. Each new scaling law (pre-training, post-training, reasoning at inference time, then agents) was dismissed before it arrived, and each multiplies compute per use on top of rising usage. He uses it to argue against bubble talk.
- **Parts:** Usage exponential times compute-per-use exponential; each new law stacks on the last.
- **When to use:** Sizing AI infrastructure demand, judging whether a compute-heavy market is a bubble.
- **How to score:** Guidance only for AI-compute questions. Note his incentive: he sells the compute, and he admits the whole industry, him included, underestimated reasoning demand by about 100x in 2024 (S037).
- **Quote:** "intelligence is gonna scale by one thing, and that's compute." (S001)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S007, S037)
