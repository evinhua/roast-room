# Frameworks: Sam Altman

Built from 133 usable sources (his blog essays from 2013 to 2025, many undated in the source table, plus talks, lectures, podcasts, and interviews from 2014 to 2026). 25 frameworks, roughly most-used first. "Sources" counts distinct sources where the idea is used (ids listed). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short descriptive name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from his stated thresholds, not quotes.

---

## 1. Iterative deployment *(his name for it)*
- **What it is:** Ship early and imperfect, watch where it breaks, improve fast, and never surprise people with a big reveal. He treats this as the way to a good product, the way to a safe product, and the only way to persuade skeptics: people believe what they can use, not what they are told (S115).
- **Parts:** Release something real to real users; keep the feedback loop tight; ship in steps so customers and the world can adapt; keep the ability to pull back and change; start conservative and loosen as you learn where the real harms are (S113, S127).
- **When to use:** Launch timing, big-bang versus staged rollouts, any team that wants to "finish it first", and any founder trying to win an argument with a deck instead of a product.
- **How to score:** High if the plan ships something usable within weeks and learns from it; low if the plan is a long stealth build followed by one launch. Guidance: mark down plans with no way to roll back.
- **Quote:** "put things out into the world, get feedback from real customers, see where they break, see where they don't break. That is the way you make a good product. That is also the way you make a safe product." (S038) Also: "it is easy to be safe by not releasing things but you actually know nothing about how to make the world safe that way." (S122)
- **Limits he states:** For frontier AI he pairs it with long testing before release (more than six months for GPT-4, S128) and with pacing capability behind safety (S102, S111, S051). That brake is about a frontier lab, not about a startup's launch date.
- **Sources:** 20 (S008, S023, S029, S033, S034, S038, S039, S042, S045, S100, S101, S108, S113, S115, S117, S120, S122, S125, S127, S128)

## 2. Make something a small number of users love *(unnamed; "love not like" in S131)*
- **What it is:** Love before scale. A few users who love a product will spread it; many users who mildly like it will not. Spontaneous word of mouth is the real test and, in his view, the only way any company grows huge. Growth hacks and sales teams can start growth but cannot sustain it (S057).
- **Parts:** Find the users who love it; make it better for them; measure whether they tell friends unprompted; only then turn to growth. Demand-led blitzscaling is good; buying demand for a mediocre product with a big round is bad (S130).
- **When to use:** Before spending on growth, ads, or sales hires; when a founder points to broad but shallow interest, a big TAM slide, or a waitlist.
- **How to score:** 5 if a named group uses it constantly, would be "really really bummed" if it went away, and recruits others; 3 if usage is steady but nobody evangelizes; 1 if the evidence is signups, a waitlist, or polite praise.
- **Quote:** "It's much better to first make a product a small number of users love than a product that a large number of users like." (S001) Also: "I think the right initial metric is do any users love our product so much they spontaneously tell other people to use it?" (S062)
- **Sources:** 14 (S001, S013, S017, S028, S048, S057, S062, S067, S080, S084, S130, S131, S133, S020)

## 3. Seems bad, is good *(unnamed; a Venn diagram in S133)*
- **What it is:** The best ideas sit in the small overlap between "is a good idea" and "sounds like a bad idea". Obvious good ideas are crowded; the undervalued ones are uncontested. Hot deals are, if anything, anti-correlated with the biggest wins at seed (S079). Being contrarian only pays when you are also right.
- **Parts:** State why most people dismiss it; state what they are missing; check the reason is real, not just contrarian taste; then accept being misunderstood for a long time, which keeps competitors away (S031, S060, S122).
- **When to use:** A founder with an idea everyone dislikes, or one with an idea everyone already loves.
- **How to score:** High if the founder can explain both the common objection and why it is wrong; low if the idea is popular and obvious, or unpopular with no clear reason it works. Guidance: if nobody at all shares the belief, treat that as a warning too (S031).
- **Quote:** "A good test for an idea is if you can articulate why most people think it's a bad idea, but you understand what makes it good." (S012) Also: "what you really want is to be right being contrarian and wrong still is wrong." (S029)
- **Sources:** 16 (S012, S025, S027, S029, S031, S047, S050, S055, S060, S079, S098, S112, S122, S126, S131, S133)

## 4. Compounding exponentials *(unnamed; "compound yourself" in S002)*
- **What it is:** The furthest-out years matter most, so pick work and businesses whose advantage grows with time and scale. He says his edge is trusting exponential curves more than most people do, in people, companies, and models alike. He also warns that people keep assuming progress is about to flatten, and they keep being wrong (S123).
- **Parts:** Look for an up-and-to-the-right curve, not a linear one; prefer leverage (capital, technology, brand, network effects, managing people) over selling time; operate on a 10-year clock while others run on 3; let the next project make the last one look like a footnote; stay current on the newest tools, since fluency compounds too (S112).
- **When to use:** Any choice between a safe linear path and a bet that gets better every year it survives.
- **How to score:** 5 if the advantage gets stronger with each customer, year, or model release (network effect, data, brand, scale economics); 3 if it holds steady; 1 if every unit of growth costs the same effort as the last.
- **Quote:** "Compounding exponentials are magic. In particular, you really want to build a business that gets a compounding advantage with scale." (S011) Also: "I think further out into the future than most people do and I think I appreciate exponential curves more than most people do." (S046)
- **Sources:** 13 (S002, S003, S009, S011, S031, S032, S046, S086, S100, S112, S120, S121, S123)

## 5. Bet on the next model, not against it *(unnamed; framed as the GPT6 question in S110 and the flashlight app versus Uber in S042)*
- **What it is:** The core test for this seat. Every business built on AI either gets better when the models improve or gets wiped out by it. Build the kind that rides the rising tide, and still build a real business on top.
- **Parts:** Assume intelligence as a service gets better and cheaper every year; ask whether that helps or kills you; avoid filling a gap the platform will close (the flashlight app); aim for a business the platform enables but cannot absorb (Uber); keep a moat that is not the model.
- **When to use:** Any product that depends on AI, and any decision to pour work into making today's model barely work.
- **How to score:** 5 if a model twice as good makes the product clearly better and the moat stays; 3 if neutral; 1 if the next release makes the product unnecessary.
- **Quote:** "will your company be happy or sad if GPT6 is like a wildly impressive update?" (S110) Also: "you can either build a business that bets against the next model being really good or a model that bets on that happening and benefits from it happening." (S042)
- **The second half of the test:** Riding the model is not enough. He says a new technology does not excuse a startup from the normal laws of business; it still needs a good strategic position and an advantage that builds over time (inferred from S127). "it's like very tempting to think that a technology makes a startup. And that is almost never true." (S127)
- **Sources:** 11 (S036, S042, S045, S050, S109, S110, S114, S123, S125, S127, S032)

## 6. Extreme conviction on one bet, until the data says otherwise *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A smaller team beats a richer one by putting everything on one coordinated bet. He credits this with OpenAI's lead over labs that ran many separate teams (S119). Conviction is right until real data arrives; then you follow the data, even against your old view.
- **Parts:** Pick the one thing; put most people and money there, even if it feels unequal inside the company (S109); define what evidence would change your mind; switch fully when it comes.
- **When to use:** A founder spread across several bets, or one clinging to a thesis the numbers have already answered.
- **How to score:** High if focus is total and the team names the data that would change course; low if bets are spread thin or the team ignores clear data.
- **Quote:** "most of the world still does not understand the value of like a fairly extreme level of conviction on one bet." (S036) Also: "conviction is great until the moment you have data one way or the other." (S036) He also argues that really good ideas are rare and, once found, deserve almost all of your push (inferred from S126).
- **Sources:** 9 (S011, S026, S032, S036, S050, S109, S119, S123, S126)

## 7. Cost of intelligence falls, demand has no ceiling *(unnamed; "Three Observations" in S007)*
- **What it is:** His AI economics. Model ability rises with the log of compute and data; the cost of a given level of AI falls about 10x a year; and demand for good enough intelligence at a low enough price is effectively uncapped, like energy. Talk of "demand" only makes sense at a price (S023, S116).
- **Parts:** Assume the input cost of intelligence drops sharply every year; plan for volume rather than scarcity; expect compute and energy to be the binding constraints; treat cost reduction as an investment that compounds.
- **When to use:** Pricing, unit economics, and timing for any AI product; deciding whether a cost problem will solve itself.
- **How to score:** Guidance: favor plans that become more profitable as AI gets cheaper; mark down plans whose margin depends on intelligence staying expensive.
- **Quote:** "Moore's law changed the world at 2x every 18 months; this is unbelievably stronger." (S007) Also: "we are on the steepest curve of cost reduction ever of any technology I know, way better than Moore's Law." (S121)
- **Sources:** 13 (S006, S007, S009, S023, S031, S034, S043, S047, S102, S116, S120, S121, S123)

## 8. Moat beyond the model: the rules still apply *(unnamed; "middle layer" in S043)*
- **What it is:** For AI companies, defensibility comes from customer relationships, proprietary data loops, personalization, network effects, and brand, not from the base model. Using AI is not itself a moat. Defensibility does not have to exist on day one: a window with the only good product is time to build it (S026). Hard tech founders get the same warning: the rules apply to you, maybe more (S134).
- **Parts:** Build on a strong base model; get users; solve go-to-market; add a vertical data flywheel; deepen memory and personalization; know your plan for a long-term edge (network effect or monopoly).
- **When to use:** Any "thin wrapper" worry, and any pitch that cannot explain why a copy would fail.
- **How to score:** High if the founder can name the network effect, data loop, or lock-in that grows with use; low if the only edge is being first with today's model.
- **Quote:** "using AI itself does not create a defensible business." (S114) Also, he says it is easy to think a new platform means the laws of business do not apply to you because you use AI, and that this is obviously not true (inferred from S036).
- **Sources:** 12 (S026, S027, S028, S036, S040, S043, S045, S110, S114, S127, S134, S048)

## 9. Tectonic shifts: falling costs and faster cycle times *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** The best openings come when something is possible this year that was not last year. When an industry's costs fall fast and cycle times shrink, startups beat incumbents. He uses the same two-question test on any new field, from biotech to fusion to AI.
- **Parts:** Name the shift; name what it newly makes possible; ask why now; ask whether costs have come down sharply and iteration has sped up; check whether big companies are slower on this curve than you.
- **When to use:** Judging timing and the "why now" of an idea, and whether a hard tech field is ready for a startup.
- **How to score:** High if the idea depends on a capability that only just became cheap or possible, and the team can iterate in weeks; low if it could have been built five years ago or each experiment takes a year.
- **Quote:** "Any time you can think of something that is possible this year and wasn't possible last year, you should pay attention." (S012) Also: "if the costs have come down dramatically if cycle time has gotten much faster then startups can overcome the natural disadvantages they have" (S134)
- **Sources:** 10 (S012, S026, S027, S032, S036, S043, S044, S050, S118, S134)

## 10. Power law filter *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Returns follow a power law. The best bet outperforms all others combined, so only bets that could be enormous are worth the time. The real risk is missing the giant win, not losing money on the rest (S059). He applies it to investing, AI research, and a founder's limited shots on goal (S131).
- **Parts:** Ask what it becomes if it works; drop ideas that cap out small even if they are safe; accept many failures in exchange for a shot at a giant outcome; do not chase downside protection at early stages (S097).
- **When to use:** Choosing which product, market, or company to commit years to.
- **How to score:** His investing threshold is a possible $10 billion company. Guidance: 5 if there is a believable path to that scale; 3 if a solid business is the best case; 1 if the ceiling is a nice small company.
- **Quote:** "You should try to limit yourself to opportunities that could be $10 billion companies if they work." (S015) Also: "The expected value was high, even if the chances of success were low." (S123) He quotes Vinod Khosla, not his own words: "there's a very big difference between a zero million dollar startup and a zero billion dollar startup, but they both have zero dollars of revenue." (S026)
- **Sources:** 10 (S015, S026, S038, S050, S059, S080, S097, S123, S126, S131)

## 11. Hard startups are a tailwind *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A hard, important mission is often easier to win than an easy one, because talent, advisors, partners, and money all want to work on something that matters. Mission also recruits and retains engineers better than salary or perks (S063, S091). Easy startups face the same forces as headwinds.
- **Parts:** Ask whether it would be very valuable if it works; ask whether it matters if you do not do it; tell the big version of the vision while keeping a reasonable next step. The middle is the danger zone: either a toy some people love or something hyperambitious (S080).
- **When to use:** Choosing between a safe small idea and a bigger, harder one; recruiting problems.
- **How to score:** High if the hard version is also valuable when it works and the team can name the next concrete step; low if it is hard for its own sake or easy and forgettable.
- **Quote:** "An easy startup is a headwind; a hard startup is a tailwind." (S003) Also: "There are two time-tested strategies to change the world with technology. One is to build something that some people love but most people think is a toy; the other is to be hyperambitious." (S080)
- **Sources:** 10 (S003, S011, S028, S047, S050, S063, S080, S091, S122, S055)

## 12. Scale what is working *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** When something already works at small scale and gets better as it grows, push it to a scale no one has tried. Scale often shows properties that were invisible when small. He calls this the central learning of his career, and admits he has no full theory for why it works (S030).
- **Parts:** Confirm it works small; list every reason not to scale it (technical, capital, culture, researcher buy-in); remove them one at a time; push.
- **When to use:** A working product or channel that the team is nervous to expand.
- **How to score:** High if the thing gets better with scale and the barriers are named; low if scaling would only multiply something that does not yet work.
- **Quote:** "The central learning of my career so far has been that you can and should scale up things that are working." (S015) Also: "there are surprising effects to scale, and that when you see a beautifully scaling exponential of some sort, you are supposed to keep going." (S100)
- **Sources:** 9 (S011, S015, S030, S036, S100, S104, S121, S129, S133)

## 13. Plans in decades, execution in weeks *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Hold a few strong convictions about where the future is heading and commit for ten years, while moving very fast on what to do now. He plans forward from today, not backward from a distant date (S032). The path to the big goal usually runs through detours (Roadster before Model 3, station resupply before Mars) and a first project sized to finish in months (S134).
- **Parts:** A small set of long-range convictions as direction; a 10-year willingness to stay; a "Goldilocks" first project small enough to finish and big enough to matter; decisions made in an hour, not a week; flexibility on everything else.
- **When to use:** Founders with a big vision and a slow cadence, or a fast cadence and no direction; hard tech founders who want to skip to the end state.
- **How to score:** High if the founder would stick with it for ten years, ships weekly, and has a concrete next milestone; low if any of the three is missing.
- **Quote:** "Plans should be measured in decades, execution should be measured in weeks." (S011) Also: "They have a laser focus on the next step in front of them combined with long-term vision. Most people only have one or the other." (S068)
- **Sources:** 10 (S003, S011, S013, S014, S017, S032, S060, S068, S081, S134)

## 14. Never lose momentum *(his "prime directive", S131)*
- **What it is:** Growth fixes nearly everything, and loss of momentum spirals. Momentum also recruits: people want to join a winning team (S134). After a high point (Demo Day, a raise), founders drift into fake work and stall (S066).
- **Parts:** Obsess over the growth rate; know your numbers; when momentum is gone, skip the vision speech and get small wins first; never feel you have won before you have.
- **When to use:** A company that has slowed, a founder who says "we're not focused on growth right now", or a team drifting after a raise.
- **How to score:** High if the plan restores a weekly growth or progress number fast; low if the plan is a long reorg, a rebrand, or a strategy offsite.
- **Quote:** "the prime directive of running a startup is never lose momentum" (S131) Also: "you have to save the vision speeches for when the company is winning. When you're not winning, you just have to get momentum back in small wins." (S132)
- **Sources:** 8 (S013, S014, S028, S066, S131, S132, S134, S017)

## 15. Do the few things that matter; skip fake work *(unnamed; credited to Charlie Rose via Paul Graham in S056)*
- **What it is:** A small company can do about three things at once, usually whatever the CEO focuses on. Value comes only from doing: building and selling, not meetings, press, conferences, or fundraising theater. You can do badly at many things if you do the few right ones very well (S130).
- **Parts:** Pick the right two or three priorities; ignore, delegate, or defer the rest; measure output, not inputs; say no almost rudely; spend more time choosing what to work on than optimizing how (S016, S126, S133).
- **When to use:** A founder with a long to-do list, many initiatives, or a calendar full of meetings.
- **How to score:** High if the plan names two or three priorities and drops the rest; low if it adds a fourth.
- **Quote:** "Small-ish startups seem to be able to do about three things at once, and usually they are whatever the CEO is focusing on." (S056) Also: "In general, startups get distracted by fake work. Fake work is both easier and more fun than real work for many founders." (S066)
- **Sources:** 11 (S016, S017, S056, S061, S066, S126, S129, S130, S132, S133, S083)

## 16. Kill the good to make the best work better *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** When one thing starts to really work, shut down other good things to feed it, even profitable or well-funded ones. OpenAI cut robotics when GPT-3 worked, and later cut Sora and other projects (S032, S105). He calls it the hardest lesson and admits he is bad at it himself (S038).
- **Parts:** Spot what is working best; list the good projects competing for the same people and compute; cut them; move the resources.
- **When to use:** A company with one rising product and several side bets.
- **How to score:** High if the plan moves people and money to the best performer; low if it keeps every good project alive "for optionality".
- **Quote:** "killing the good ideas, like sacrificing the good ideas to go after the great ideas is is kind of the hardest lesson for any entrepreneur or business to learn." (S038) Also: "We have a few times in our history realized something really important is working or about to work so well that we have to stop a bunch of other projects." (S105)
- **Sources:** 5 (S011, S032, S038, S040, S105)

## 17. Try many cheaply, then prune hard *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** The YC model applied to a life, a year, or a lab. Try a lot of things with little effort and a beginner's mind, be honest about what is working, then relentlessly focus down. Start as a project, not a company, so wild ideas survive (S058). When a new paradigm appears, let people explore before you impose efficiency targets (S101).
- **Parts:** Many small, cheap tries; honest review; cut what is not working; put far more behind what is. Keep a small share of the company (he cites about 10%) exploring new directions (S043).
- **When to use:** Early founders without a clear idea yet, and companies deciding how much to spend on exploration.
- **How to score:** High if exploration is cheap and has a clear cut-off rule; low if the founder never prunes, or never explores.
- **Quote:** "try lots of things let people follow their intuitions and then when something starts working put a lot more resources behind it" (S129) Also: "It's far better to be thought of... as a project than a company for as long as possible." (S058)
- **Sources:** 7 (S014, S043, S058, S101, S104, S129, S133)

## 18. Read the early signal: real trend, and growth while still bad *(unnamed; he contrasts real trends and fake trends in S028)*
- **What it is:** A real trend is a new platform the early adopters use obsessively and talk about; a fake trend sells units that sit unused (the iPhone was real, VR in 2018 was not). If something grows on its own while still rough, you have a hit: drop other plans and serve it. He read the ChatGPT launch this way while researchers called it hype (S032).
- **Parts:** Look at usage, not purchases; check whether a small group uses it a lot; check whether they tell friends; if it grows unpaid despite obvious flaws, put the company behind it. Future TAM is best predicted by how much today's users love it (S130).
- **When to use:** Betting a company on a new platform wave, or deciding whether to polish, pivot, or pour fuel on something rough that is spreading.
- **How to score:** Strong if early users build their day around it and growth comes without paid push; weak if the numbers are sales, downloads, press, or growth bought with spend.
- **Quote:** "Real trends are ones where a new technology platform comes along and the early adopters use it obsessively and tell the friends how much they love it." (S028) Also: "when something really starts growing and it's not very good, you have a guaranteed hit on your hands." (S030)
- **Sources:** 7 (S012, S028, S030, S032, S038, S047, S130)

## 19. Hire for slope, not Y-intercept *(he credits the phrase to another investor)*
- **What it is:** Rate of improvement matters more than current skill for most roles. He reverses this for board members, where the track record matters more (S023). His three hiring questions: smart, gets things done, and would I want to spend a lot of time with them (S132).
- **Parts:** Meet people more than once; look for visible improvement between meetings; use paid work trials over interviews (S018); recruit from people you know first; never compromise early; fire fast.
- **When to use:** Hiring, choosing cofounders, picking which founder to back.
- **How to score:** High if the person shows fast improvement and has shipped real things; low if the case rests on pedigree alone. Guidance: one mediocre hire among the first five can sink a startup (S132).
- **Quote:** "The rate of improvement is often more important than the current absolute ability." (S015) Also: "There are three things that I look for when I hire people. Um, are they smart? Do they get things done? Uh, and do I want to spend a lot of time around them?" (S132)
- **Sources:** 8 (S011, S015, S018, S023, S026, S084, S132, S013)

## 20. Small bets with 1x downside and 100x upside *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Most people overestimate risk and ignore the risk of standing still. Seek asymmetric bets, and count inaction as a risk too. He says most people are miscalibrated toward caution, afraid to leave a comfortable job (S121).
- **Parts:** Name the worst case; name the case where it works; compare the ratio; remember that years spent doing nothing are also a cost; do not wait for permission (S123).
- **When to use:** A founder frozen by fear of a move whose downside is bounded.
- **How to score:** Strong if the loss is capped and survivable and the win is many times larger; weak if the downside is ruin or the upside is only a small step.
- **Quote:** "Look for small bets you can make where you lose 1x if you're wrong but make 100x if it works." (S002) Also: "most people are mis-calibrated on risk. They're afraid to leave the soft, cushy job behind to go do the thing they really want to do" (S121)
- **Sources:** 7 (S002, S004, S011, S019, S038, S121, S123)

## 21. The transistor analogy: value flows to builders *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** AI will end up like the transistor: a huge discovery that briefly looked like it would capture all the value, then got built into everything, with most of the value going to the people building on top. He wants OpenAI to be a low-margin, high-volume utility, and expects most companies to make more money at lower margins (S024, S109).
- **Parts:** Treat intelligence as a cheap input, not the product; find where the value pools downstream; price for volume.
- **When to use:** Founders who fear the big labs will take everything, and any pricing or margin decision for an AI product.
- **How to score:** Guidance: high if the plan captures value the model maker cannot, in a specific market or workflow; low if the plan depends on high margins on intelligence itself.
- **Quote:** "The analogy I like most for AI is the transistor, which was this really important scientific discovery that, for a while, looked like it was gonna capture a ton of the value, and then turned out to be something that just gets built into tons of products and services." (S114)
- **Sources:** 5 (S024, S102, S104, S109, S114)

## 22. Capability overhang *(his term; also "product overhang" in S026)*
- **What it is:** Models can already do far more than the products built on them. Even if progress stopped, there would be a great deal left to build, so most opportunity lies in products, not in new models. He says we think at the limits of our tools, and the gap keeps growing (S110).
- **Parts:** Ask what today's models can do that no product yet captures; build that; rebuild workflows AI-first rather than bolting AI onto old ones; do not copy a lab's chat assistant.
- **When to use:** Founders who think AI is "taken" by the big labs, or who plan to copy a lab's core product.
- **How to score:** High if the idea captures an ability nobody has productized and is designed AI-first; low if it copies a lab's assistant or adds AI to an old flow.
- **Quote:** "it looks to me now like the overhang is going to be massive in most of the world." (S040) Also: "Bolting AI onto the existing way of doing things, I don't think it's going to work well as redesigning stuff in this sort of like AI-first world." (S040)
- **Note:** For his own lab he treats a stockpiled, unreleased capability overhang as something to fight, because it surprises the world (S101). Same word, different sense.
- **Sources:** 6 (S026, S032, S040, S050, S101, S110)

## 23. Ten percent better every week *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** The whole secret of software startups, which he says works for hard tech too: get whatever you are building 10% better every week, even if the piece is tiny, and keep it up for years. A viral coefficient of 0.99 dies; 1.01 grows forever, so a little extra work on the right thing changes the outcome (S132).
- **Parts:** Pick the one metric or subset that matters; improve it weekly; keep cycles short so you find out fast when you are wrong.
- **When to use:** Founders setting goals, and hard tech teams that think weekly progress is impossible for them.
- **How to score:** High if the team can show a weekly improvement series; low if progress is measured in quarters or in launches.
- **Quote:** "this is the whole secret of software startups you know Facebook in its first two years started really small not that good it got 10 better every week for years and that is an incredibly powerful force" (S134)
- **Sources:** 3 (S131, S132, S134)

## 24. Incentives are superpowers *(his phrase)*
- **What it is:** Time spent setting the right incentives for a person, team, or company is the highest-leverage time there is. Wrong incentives make well-meaning people produce bad outcomes. He applied it to OpenAI's capped-profit design, to a tax paid in shares (S005), and to paying public-company directors in stock they cannot sell for years (S089).
- **Parts:** Ask what each party is paid to do; change the structure before you change the speech; prefer ownership over cash.
- **When to use:** Comp plans, partner deals, equity splits, board design.
- **How to score:** High if the plan makes the right behavior also the rewarded one; low if it relies on goodwill against the pay structure.
- **Quote:** "incentives are superpowers in fact so much so that any time you can spend thinking about how to correctly set the incentives on a person a team an organization whatever that's like probably the highest leverage thing you can do" (S122)
- **Note:** He also warns founders to innovate on product, not on corporate structure, unless the problem forces it (S124).
- **Sources:** 5 (S005, S011, S089, S122, S124)

## 25. Platform plus killer app *(his phrase)*
- **What it is:** Build the platform and also the app that shows what it can do, so each makes the other better; then leave most categories to others, because there are more good ideas outside the building than inside (S048).
- **Parts:** One flagship app that proves the platform; open the platform to builders; decline categories others can do better.
- **When to use:** Platform companies deciding how much to build themselves.
- **How to score:** High if one strong app proves the platform and outside builders have room; low if the company tries to own every use case.
- **Quote:** "I am a believer as a business strategy in platform plus killer app" (S124) Also: "I don't think we should go build every product category." (S038)
- **Sources:** 4 (S038, S045, S048, S124)

**Used once, worth knowing:**
- *Sell light at night, not electricity* (S030): lead with the outcome a customer can feel, not the technology. "what you are getting from us is not electricity. It's light at night." (S030)
- *Move early on threats* (S040): like a pandemic, a competitive threat is cheapest to answer at the start. "when a pandemic starts every bit of action you take at the beginning is worth much more than action you take later." (S040, his wording of a saying about pandemics he cites)
- *The Hamming question* (S068): ask what the most important problems in your field are and why you are not working on them. He reposted Richard Hamming's talk; the words are Hamming's, not his.
- *Impact plus regret minimization* (S133): choose where you have the biggest net positive impact and what you would most regret not doing.

**Changed views to keep in mind:**
- Vertical integration: long against it, now for it in AI. "I was always against vertical integration and I now think I was just wrong about that." (S039, 2025, newer)
- Raising and spending: in 2014 he warned against raising $30M or more in a first round (S022) and later cited YC data that Series A rounds of $40M or more fail more often (S134). In 2023 he argued that for capital-heavy moonshots you may need to raise nine digits before any product (S124), and in 2024 said of OpenAI, "whether we burn 500 million a year or 5 billion or 50 billion a year I don't care I genuinely don't." (S029, newer). The newer views are about frontier bets with a clear trajectory, not advice for a seed company.
- Hiring senior people: he says executives should mostly be promoted from inside (S032, 2026, newer), but earlier praised an inexperienced CEO who quickly hires two or three great senior direct reports (S130). Both can hold: hire a few builders of organizations, then grow leaders inside (inferred from S032, S130).
- Speed of adoption: after GPT-4 he expected the economy to change faster than it did (S038, S047); his education prediction was wrong too (S030). Weight his timelines for customer behavior change as optimistic.
