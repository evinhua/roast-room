# Books: Sam Altman

Built from 133 usable sources (his blog essays from 2013 to 2025, many undated in the source table, plus talks, lectures, podcasts, and interviews from 2014 to 2026). He has written no books. His thinking lives in essays, one long online guide, two lecture series, and an annual letter, grouped below. Summaries are in my own words.

## Startup Playbook (2015)
- **Core thesis:** A free online guide to starting a company: idea, product, team, and execution, with making something users love as the step no one can skip.
- **Key frameworks:** a small group who love the product beats a large group who like it; founders do sales and support themselves; keep burn low and hire for aptitude and belief in the mission; founders set the culture early.
- **Taught in sources:** S001.

## How to Start a Startup, Stanford lecture series (2014)
- **Core thesis:** The course he ran. Lecture 1: you need a great idea, product, team, and execution, and the idea and product come first. Lecture 2: cofounders, hiring, and execution as focus plus intensity.
- **Key frameworks:** explain the company in one sentence; the best ideas look bad at first; answer "why now"; plan for an eventual monopoly in a large market; relentlessly resourceful cofounders; three hiring questions (smart, gets things done, want to spend time with them); hire late, fire fast; pick the two or three priorities; never lose momentum; generous employee equity.
- **Taught in sources:** S027 (Lecture 1, shared with a second speaker), S132 (Lecture 2); the same themes in S013, S014, S028, S131.

## Startup advice essays (2013 to 2015 where dated; many undated)
- **Core thesis:** Short lists of rules for founders: love before growth, growth fixes most problems, momentum matters, real work beats fake work, speed is your edge over big companies, and only start something you would work on for ten years.
- **Key frameworks:** growth rate as the obsession; decide in an hour; painkillers over vitamins; start as a project, not a company; a startup can do about three things at once; word of mouth is the only way to grow huge; make more than you spend per user; the post-raise slump into fake work; learn to code rather than hunt for a technical cofounder; toy-or-hyperambitious as the two paths to impact.
- **Taught in sources:** Startup Advice (S013), Startup advice, briefly (S014), Super successful companies (S017), Advice for ambitious 19 year olds (S019), Startups, Role Models, Risk, and Y Combinator (S020), How things get done (S056), The Only Way to Grow Huge (S057), Projects and Companies (S058), Value is created by doing (S061), Before Growth (S062), The Post-YC Slump (S066), Unit Economics (S067), Researchers and Founders (S068), Stupid Apps and Changing the World (S080), Non-technical founder? Learn to hack (S092), The Worst Part of YC (S098).

## Hiring, equity, and team essays (How to hire is 2013; the rest undated)
- **Core thesis:** A company is the team the founders build, so hiring deserves a third to a half of a founder's time after product-market fit, and early employees deserve far more equity than they usually get.
- **Key frameworks:** paid work trials over interviews; hire from personal networks first; learn a role before you hire for it; never compromise early; fire fast; retain with mission, rocketship growth, and a great team rather than perks or high salaries; give about 10% to the first 10 employees; long option exercise windows; always state the fully diluted percentage.
- **Taught in sources:** How to hire (S018), Employee Retention (S063), Employee Equity (S064), The Engineer Crunch (S091); echoed in S011, S026, S132.

## Fundraising and investing essays (2014 and 2020 where dated; many undated)
- **Core thesis:** Startup returns follow a power law, so investors should only back companies that could be enormous and founders should keep rounds simple, competitive, and not too large.
- **Key frameworks:** the $10 billion filter; upside risk over downside protection; rate of improvement over current ability; scale what is working; decide quickly; hot seed rounds are a poor signal; set up competition and avoid down rounds; anything but a term sheet is a no; simple term sheets focused on ownership and pro rata; one lead investor over a party round; founders want money, not advice; in a bubble, act as if this is the last money you will raise.
- **Taught in sources:** How To Invest In Startups (S015), Valuations (S022), Upside risk (S059), Fundraising Mistakes Founders Make (S065), Board Members (S075), Party rounds (S076), The separation of advice and money (S077), Black Swan Seed Rounds (S079), Bubble talk (S081), 'We're in a Bubble' (S082), What to do if a bubble is starting (S083), The Tech Bust of 2015 (S085), A founder-friendly term sheet (S097); the power law applied to research in S038.

## How To Be Successful (2019) and related career essays
- **Core thesis:** Habits for an exponential career: compound yourself, believe in yourself almost to delusion, own equity, take risks, focus, work hard, and care about opinions on a long time scale.
- **Key frameworks:** compound yourself; small bets with 1x downside and 100x upside; long-term thinking as the edge; be internally driven; get good at sales; own things that rise in value; allocate your year, not your day; trade short-term low status for long-term high status.
- **Taught in sources:** S002; related essays The days are long but the decades are short (S004), Productivity (S016), The Strength of Being Misunderstood (S060).

## Hard Startups (2020), Idea Generation (2020), and hard tech
- **Core thesis:** Hard, important startups attract talent and help that easy ones do not; good ideas come from watching big shifts and from ideas most people think are bad; hard tech still follows the normal startup rules.
- **Key frameworks:** a hard startup is a tailwind; a 10-year horizon beats a 3-year one; scan for what is newly possible this year; the bad-seeming but good idea; falling costs and faster cycle times as the test for a field; a "Goldilocks" first project; detours on the way to the big goal; 10% better every week.
- **Taught in sources:** S003, S012, Hard Tech is Back (S055), the MIT hard tech talk (S134).

## 2017 YC Annual Letter (2017)
- **Core thesis:** An update to the YC community and the questions he used to judge companies in the era of huge, network-effected tech firms.
- **Key frameworks:** four questions (will lots of people really love it, is it easy to copy, will the founders become forces of nature, is the mission clear and important); the "hyperscale" era of network effects.
- **Taught in sources:** S084.

## What I Wish Someone Had Told Me (2023)
- **Core thesis:** Lessons from running OpenAI in one page: optimism, concentration, compounding, and speed.
- **Key frameworks:** plans in decades, execution in weeks; concentrate on few high-conviction bets; compounding advantage with scale; inaction is risk; incentives are superpowers; outcomes over process.
- **Taught in sources:** S011.

## AI essays (2021 to 2025 where dated; the early machine intelligence posts are undated)
- **Core thesis:** Early, undated essays treat machine intelligence as both the biggest opportunity and the biggest risk and call for regulation and safety research; the dated 2021 to 2025 ones argue deep learning gets predictably better with scale, the cost of intelligence keeps falling fast, and abundant intelligence and energy will make most goods far cheaper.
- **Key frameworks:** machine intelligence as a double exponential; the merge of humans and machines; Moore's Law for everything; intelligence rises with the log of compute while cost falls about 10x a year; progress is one smooth exponential curve; iterative deployment; compute as the binding constraint; democratize AI and keep power from concentrating; judge a product by whether users' lives are better after six months (Sora 2).
- **Taught in sources:** Machine intelligence parts 1 and 2 (S052, S053), The Merge (S054), AI (S095), Moore's Law for Everything (S005), The Intelligence Age (S006), Reflections (S008), Three Observations (S007), The Gentle Singularity (S009), Abundant Intelligence (S010), GPT-4o (S093), Sora 2 (S094), and an untitled post on his core AI beliefs (S096).

## Policy and progress essays (Moore's Law for Everything is 2021; the rest undated)
- **Core thesis:** Growth is what makes democracy work, technology turns differences in ability into exponential differences in wealth, and policy should push for growth and spread ownership.
- **Key frameworks:** taxing capital rather than labor and an American Equity Fund (2021); seven policy levers for growth; target a real GDP growth rate ("you build what you measure"); the 2x2 of short versus long term and software versus physical, with too little money for long-term physical work; open culture as a condition for innovation; density as the reason Silicon Valley works.
- **Taught in sources:** S005, The Software Revolution (S070), What happened to innovation? (S071), Why Silicon Valley Works (S072), Please Fund More Science (S074), Technology and wealth inequality (S086), American Equity (S087), Growth and Government (S088), Policy for Growth and Innovation (S089), E Pur Si Muove (S090). These sit outside a founder's usual decisions; use them for his view of where costs and markets are heading.

## Books and texts he cites
- **The Beginning of Infinity (David Deutsch):** he says he is rereading it in light of AI, for its idea that progress means understanding more of the world (S038). Not his book.
- **You and Your Research (Richard Hamming):** he reposted the talk on his blog and uses its question about working on the most important problems in your field (S068). The text is Hamming's.
- **Pandemonium:** his favorite book one year, on how people met the first machines and thought about automation, which he ties to the AI moment (S130). Author not named in the source.
- **The Way to Love (Anthony de Mello):** a short book of meditations he read on Naval Ravikant's recommendation (S129).
