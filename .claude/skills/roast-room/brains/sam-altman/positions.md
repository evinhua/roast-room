# Positions: Sam Altman

Built from fifteen batches of verified notes over 133 usable sources (essays 2013 to 2025, undated blog posts, talks and interviews 2014 to 2026). 40 topics. Each stance has a verbatim quote, source id, and date (essay month, or upload month for video; see `sources.md`). S052 to S134 carry no date in the table except S084 (2017-01); they are marked undated, and where a title names a year it is given as "title year". Where he changed his mind, both views sit side by side with dates and the newer one is marked. Topics with no evidence say so.

## Ambition and hard startups
- **Stance:** Pick the hard, important thing. Hard startups get a tailwind of talent and support that easy ones never get, and the big thing is not much harder than the small one.
- "An easy startup is a headwind; a hard startup is a tailwind." (S003, 2020-02)
- "It is easier for a team to do a hard thing that really matters than to do an easy thing that doesn't really matter; audacious ideas motivate people." (S011, 2023-12)
- "I am a believer that if you're going to like work really hard at something, you may as well pick the big thing cuz it's not that much harder." (S103, undated)
- 2026-07: "Do something that matters. Like do something that is important and if you don't do it, if your company doesn't succeed, might not happen." (S047)
- On hard tech, undated: less competition is a structural advantage, and missionaries join where mercenaries will not (S134).
- **Read:** consistent from 2013 to 2026, and the core of this seat. Inferred from S003, S011, S028, S047, S103, S134.

## Choosing an idea
- **Stance:** The best ideas sound bad to most people and are big if they work. Never take your idea from what is fashionable.
- "A good test for an idea is if you can articulate why most people think it's a bad idea, but you understand what makes it good." (S012, 2020-05)
- "all of these were ideas that seemed bad but turned out to be good, and this is the magic formula for major success." (S058, undated)
- "I love the unpopular ideas. I I I have always liked ideas that are big if they work, difficult to get to work, and unpopular." (S050, 2026-03)
- Caveat he adds: "what you really want is to be right being contrarian and wrong still is wrong." (S029, 2024-05)
- A check on lone conviction: "if you can't find anybody else that shares your belief, you should pay attention to that." (S031, 2026-07)
- Ideas alone are cheap: "Ideas by themselves are not worth anything." (S132, title 2014)

## Market size and growth
- **Stance:** Care about how fast the market grows and how big it gets, not today's size. As an investor, only back things that could be huge.
- "What you really want to do is identify a market that's going to grow every year and be able to ride that up elevator." (S028, 2018-08)
- "You should try to limit yourself to opportunities that could be $10 billion companies if they work." (S015, 2020-01)
- 2015-02: prefers a fast-growing market to a large slow one, especially one others dismiss (S014).
- On AI demand, 2026-07: "the demand for sufficiently high quality intelligence at a sufficiently low price is effectively uncapped." (S031)

## Timing and why now
- **Stance:** Startups win when the ground shifts fastest. He calls the current AI shift the best time ever to start.
- "Any time you can think of something that is possible this year and wasn't possible last year, you should pay attention." (S012, 2020-05)
- "when the clock cycle of the industry changes this much, startups almost always win." (S026, 2025-06)
- "This is the best fucking time ever in the history of technology, ever, period, to start a company." (S026, 2025-06)
- "you have probably the biggest option space of any startup of any of startups that have at any time in history." (S050, 2026-03)
- Downturns: 2023-01, good for builders because tourists leave (S044); undated, "Good companies can make incredible strides during a bust." (S083)
- The catch, undated: "to do anything at this point on a 10-year time horizon requires a real suspension of disbelief." (S109)

## Building on AI models
- **Stance:** Build something that gets better when the next model ships. Do not build a patch for a gap the next model will close, and do not mistake using AI for a business.
- "you can either build a business that bets against the next model being really good or a model that bets on that happening and benefits from it happening." (S042, 2024-05)
- "will your company be happy or sad if GPT6 is like a wildly impressive update?" (S110, undated)
- "it's like very tempting to think that a technology makes a startup. And that is almost never true." (S127, DevDay 2024)
- "using AI itself does not create a defensible business." (S114, undated)
- 2025-12: "Bolting AI onto the existing way of doing things, I don't think it's going to work well as redesigning stuff in this sort of like AI-first world." (S040)
- 2026-07: "a startup of today still looks mostly like a startup of 10 years ago, cuz that's what the received wisdom says you're supposed to do" (S032)
- His wrapper warning, undated: early App Store flashlight apps got absorbed into iOS, while Uber used the phone without being captured by it (S114).

## Defensibility and moats
- **Stance:** Every great business needs a plan for long-term advantage, but the moat can come after launch. Software is a temporary edge you spend building a real moat.
- "Compounding exponentials are magic. In particular, you really want to build a business that gets a compounding advantage with scale." (S011, 2023-12)
- "software itself has never been a super strong moat. You use it to get a temporary advantage to build up one of the traditional moats of a business." (S100, undated)
- 2023-01, for AI startups: "build like deep relationships with customers a product they love and some sort of moat that doesn't have to be technology." (S045)
- 2025-06, on OpenAI itself: "We didn't have any defensibility strategy. We just, like, had the only good thing out there." (S026)
- 2025-12: "personalization is extremely sticky. People love the fact that the model gets to know them over time" (S040)
- Undated: traditional moats still work (customer relationships, network effects, data, infrastructure scale), and scale of infrastructure is OpenAI's biggest (S103). One of his four YC questions is whether the company will be easy to copy (S084, 2017-01).

## Competition and copying
- **Stance:** Do not copy competitors or build what everyone else is building. Ignore competitors until they beat you with a shipped product.
- "If you compete with other people, you end up in this mimetic trap, and you sort of play this tournament, and if you win, you lose." (S025, 2019-02)
- "Don't worry about a competitor at all um until they're actually beating you with a real shipped product. Uh press releases are easier to write than code." (S132, title 2014)
- "I really believe it's something like 70 or 80 of the market is chasing the current fad" (S134, undated)
- "We have a lot of people that I think are skating to where the puck was, and we're going to where the puck is going." (S121, undated)
- "if you're starting the same startup as everybody else, it's like you get a lot of hype and you can raise a lot of money" (S031, 2026-07), but those are rarely the big outcomes.
- He still respects threats: "If Google had really decided to take us seriously in 2023, let's we would have been in a really bad place." (S040, 2025-12) And he likes the fight: "And I expect to win." (S035, 2025-07)

## Focus versus diversification
- **Stance:** Concentrate on a few high-conviction bets and kill good projects to feed the best one. He admits he is bad at this himself.
- "Concentrate your resources on a small number of high-conviction bets; this is easy to say but evidently hard to do. You can delete more stuff than you think." (S011, 2023-12)
- "When you combine extreme focus and great teams, magic happens." (S056, undated)
- "sacrificing the good ideas to go after the great ideas is is kind of the hardest lesson for any entrepreneur or business to learn." (S038, 2026-08)
- Earlier exception, 2022-09: let about 10 percent of the company explore new directions (S043).
- Self-correction, undated: OpenAI lost ground on coding and pretraining by spreading across a browser, Sora, and other side quests (S102, S113).
- **Read:** focus on one bet plus a small explore budget. Inferred from S011, S032, S038, S043, S102.

## Conviction versus data
- **Stance:** Hold extreme conviction on one bet until the data arrives, then follow the data. Being misunderstood is fine if you are right.
- "most of the world still does not understand the value of like a fairly extreme level of conviction on one bet." (S036, 2024-11)
- "conviction is great until the moment you have data one way or the other" (S036, 2024-11)
- "as long as you are right, being misunderstood by most people is a strength not a weakness." (S060, undated)
- "it's really hard to have a lot of conviction and unpopular idea as a founder or an investor" (S131, undated)
- His own blind spot, undated: "every time I make a major investment in a company um I am convinced it's going to work." (S129)

## Scaling what works
- **Stance:** When something is already working at small scale, push it further than anyone has tried, and watch for a steeper curve.
- "The central learning of my career so far has been that you can and should scale up things that are working." (S015, 2020-01)
- "when in doubt if you have something that seems like it's getting better with scale I think you should scale it up." (S036, 2024-11)
- 2026-06: the most interesting results of his career came from "emergent properties that scale or scale continuing to provide returns far beyond what the consensus thinks will work." (S030)
- The caution, undated: "we followed one scaling law that kept being good without without really internalizing. There was a new even steeper scaling law" (S104)

## Growth and momentum
- **Stance:** Growth is the main job. Never lose momentum, and never let fake work replace real work.
- "Growth solves (nearly) all problems." (S013, 2013-06)
- "Momentum is everything in a startup. If you have momentum, you can survive most other problems." (S066, undated)
- "Fake work is both easier and more fun than real work for many founders." (S066, undated)
- "One of the most important jobs you have as a founder is to never lose momentum." (S028, 2018-08)
- Signal he trusts: "when something really starts growing and it's not very good, you have a guaranteed hit on your hands." (S030, 2026-06)

## Product and niche
- **Stance:** Start with a small group who love it, not a big group who like it. Word of mouth is the test.
- "It's much better to first make a product a small number of users love than a product that a large number of users like." (S001, 2015-11)
- "the degree to which you're successful approximates the degree to which you build a product that is so good people spontaneously tell their friends about it." (S028, 2018-08)
- "The only way to generate sustained exponential growth is to make whatever you're making sufficiently good." (S057, undated)
- 2026-09: "you got to like make something people want." (S048)
- Undated: product love, such as people in a poor country guarding their iPhones above all else, predicts a market better than a sizing spreadsheet (S130).

## Shipping and speed
- **Stance:** Ship before you are comfortable, learn from real users, and get new things done every week. He applies this to safety too.
- "this is the one thing that we've learned best predicts the success of founders in YC. If every time we talk to a team, they've gotten new things done, um that's the best predictor we have" (S132, title 2014)
- "it does kind of suck to ship a product that you're embarrassed about but it's much better than the alternative." (S029, 2024-05)
- "put things out into the world, get feedback from real customers, see where they break, see where they don't break. That is the way you make a good product." (S038, 2026-08)
- Exception for frontier research, undated: OpenAI waited 4.5 years for a first product and broke standard YC advice, which he does not recommend for most companies (S121). "the great majority of making a good product is doing good research." (S113)

## Raising money
- **Stance:** Raising is not the hard part. Raising too much early is a known bad sign. Create competition among investors, take market terms, and get back to work.
- 2014-06: resist raising and spending too much; companies that raise very large first rounds have a bad track record (S022).
- Undated: "if you look at the track record of companies that have raised 40 million or more in their series a... they usually fail like way more often than the average series a company fails" (S134)
- "you generally need to set up a competitive environment to get a good outcome in fundraising (or, for that matter, any big deal)." (S065, undated)
- "In a sense, VCs sell advice, but founders want to buy money." (S077, undated)
- 2019-05: "I would not have YC companies raise the amount of money they raise or the valuations they do" (S046)
- For capital-heavy missions he raises without limit; undated, he backs raising nine digits before any product for long, hard bets (S124). See Burn and spending.

## Valuation and terms
- **Stance:** Do not chase above-market valuations. Never set up a down round. Keep terms simple.
- "trying to get really high valuations is a mistake." (S065, undated)
- "the prime directive of fundraising strategy: set things up so that you never do a down round." (S065, undated)
- "Great companies often look like bad ideas at the beginning" so a seed round that looks great is likely overpriced (S079, undated).
- As an investor: "I have an allergic reaction to complex deal structures, as they invariably end up with all sorts of unintended consequences." (S097, undated)
- 2023-01: Stripe was his second investment ever and his best multiple, at a valuation that would look tiny today (S044).

## Metrics, boards, and investors
- **Stance:** Know your growth numbers cold. A lead investor and a board help by forcing a monthly review. Party rounds let companies drift.
- "It turns out to be really good for a company to have a board -- it focuses the company if everyone knows they have to present the key metrics to outsiders once a month." (S022, 2014-06)
- "In a typical party round, no single investor cares enough to think about the company multiple times a day." (S076, undated)
- "Good investors stay out of the way when you don't need them and help you a lot when you do." (S076, undated)
- "The companies that have had the biggest impact and created the most value have had excellent board members (and executives). I don't believe this is a coincidence." (S075, undated)
- 2024-03, on directors: "For a board member, I do care much more about the Y-intercept." (S023)
- Undated: at YC he worked out with Ginkgo how to get 10 percent better every week, and every hard field has an incremental milestone (S134). He runs a December 31 review of what worked and what did not (S129).

## Burn and spending
- **Earlier (2013 to 2015, plus undated essays):** keep burn low and stay frugal. Personal burn low is a standing rule (S004, S019), frugal companies keep a long-term mindset (S022, 2014-06), and "there is something culturally important to us about frugality because we want that reflected in our startups" (S130, undated). Treat money in the bank as the last you will raise (S083, undated).
- **Unit economics (undated):** "Most great companies historically have had good unit economics soon after they began monetizing, even if the company as a whole lost money for a long period of time." (S067) "Burn rates are scary when you scale the business up and the model doesn't look any better." (S067)
- **Newer (2024 to 2026), for compute-bound bets:** "whether we burn 500 million a year or 5 billion or 50 billion a year I don't care I genuinely don't" (S029, 2024-05). "I definitely badly undershot on the compute investments." (S032, 2026-07) On the compute commitment, undated: "we might screw it up like this is the bet that we're making" (S116)
- **Read:** frugal by default; spend without limit only when scale itself is the advantage, the unit economics improve with scale, and the demand is visible. Inferred from S022, S029, S032, S040, S067.

## Debt
- **Stance:** On a startup's own debt: no recorded position. On AI infrastructure, he called debt reasonable because data centers are real assets; his worry is model progress stalling, not the debt (S040, 2025-12).
- Undated, on late-stage rounds with heavy preferences and caps: "This is hardly an equity instrument at all." (S085) He reads such terms as debt in disguise.

## Pricing and margins
- **Stance:** Expects margins to fall and is fine with it: more money at lower margin, huge volume, and prices that fall every year.
- "I think that most companies like OpenAI will make more money at a lower margin." (S024, 2025-11)
- "I'd be happy for us to be like a forever low margin as long as we can be huge and grow in fast business" (S109, undated)
- "AI is going to be massively deflationary." (S110, undated)
- Undated: at $20 a month ChatGPT Plus already delivers well above its price, and the cost of intelligence will head toward zero (S121).
- On discounting or specific pricing tactics: no recorded position.

## Advertising and monetization
- **Stance:** Dislikes ads, and holds that paid placement must never change the answer.
- "I kind of hate ads just as an aesthetic choice" (S023, 2024-03)
- Undated (2023 talk): "Ads plus AI is sort of uniquely unsettling to me." (S123) He calls advertising a misalignment of company and user incentives most of the time (S122, title 2023).
- "if ChatGPT were accepting payment to put a worse hotel above a better hotel, that's probably catastrophic for your relationship with ChatGPT." (S024, 2025-11)
- He thinks the real money is elsewhere: "the way to monetize the world's smartest model is certainly not hotel booking." (S024, 2025-11)

## Paid acquisition and marketing
- **Stance:** Prefers organic growth; growth hacks only amplify a product people already love. Paid ads: no recorded position. When marketing a new technology, sell the outcome, not the technology.
- "It's the critical ingredient for companies that do really well ... and if you don't figure it out, no amount of growth hacking will make you into a great company." (S062, undated)
- Undated: refer-a-friend programs work only if the product is already good enough to recommend (S057).
- "what you are getting from us is not electricity. It's light at night." (S030, 2026-06)

## Content and personal brand
- **Stance:** No recorded position on content marketing. He values founders who can sell and evangelize, and expects real human presence to gain value as AI content floods in.
- 2018-08: at least one founder must recruit, sell, talk to press, and raise money (S028).
- "All great careers, to some degree, become sales jobs." (S002, 2019-01)
- "being a real person in a world of unlimited AI content will increase in value." (S114, undated)

## Hiring and firing
- **Stance:** Hiring decides the company. Hire slowly, from your network, for slope over current ability. Fire fast.
- "If you don't hire very well, you will not be successful -- companies are a product of the team the founders build." (S018, 2013-09)
- "Spend more time recruiting. Take risks on high-potential people with a fast rate of improvement." (S011, 2023-12)
- "hire for slope, not Y intercept. And I think that's just like unbelievably great advice." (S026, 2025-06) He credits the slope line to Paul Buchheit; the endorsement is his own.
- 2014-01: only hire when there is no other way to do what you need (S017). Undated: the best startups hire the fewest people and wait the longest; Airbnb took five months to hire its first employee (S131, S132).
- Retention is the hidden failure: "A reasonably common failure mode for startups is to do a great job recruiting the first ten or so employees and then have many of them leave after 18 months." (S063, undated)
- **Firing:** every new founder waits too long. "I have never met a newbie founder that fires fast enough." (S018, 2013-09)
- 2013-06: hire slow, fire fast (S013). "no matter what I say here, this is not going to prevent anyone from doing it wrong." (S132, title 2014)

## Small teams and one-person companies
- **Stance (newer):** AI lets tiny teams build huge companies, and that spreads economic power.
- "I think it is probably possible now to start a company that is a oneperson company that will go on to be worth like more than a billion dollars." (S104, undated)
- "it is a legitimate single-person billion-dollar company as far as I can tell." (S105, undated)
- Undated: in recent months the power of a one to three person company has become extraordinary (S113); zero-person companies are the most underrated opportunity (S112).

## Senior executives
- **Earlier (undated, Masters of Scale):** hire two or three senior people who are great at scaling early; an inexperienced CEO with strong lieutenants does better (S130).
- **Newer (2025 to 2026):** promote from inside and do not start with an eminent senior administrator. 2025-06: he has not had success starting with a very senior administrator (S026). "I think most of the time executives at a company should be promoted internally, not hired externally." (S032, 2026-07)

## Equity and compensation
- **Stance:** Own equity, not time. Pay early staff a bit below market with generous equity. Founders are too stingy with employees and too generous with investors.
- "You get truly rich by owning things that increase rapidly in value." (S002, 2019-01)
- 2013-09: slightly below market salary, very generous equity (S018).
- "Founders certainly deserve a huge premium for starting the earliest, but probably not 100 or 200x what employee number 5 gets." (S064, undated)
- Undated: the YC companies most generous with early employee equity are in general the most successful (S132).
- His own OpenAI stake, two views: 2026-07, holding none is worth it: "I have a front row seat to the most exciting moment of human history. And like that is worth more to me than any amount of money." (S047) Undated, the regret: "This is why I wish I had just taken the stock." (S106) and "That that was truly one of the dumbest thing as I say this as someone who's made a lot of dumb decisions." (S113) The table cannot order these; he holds both: money did not drive him, and the optics cost trust.

## Co-founders and founder fit
- **Stance:** Co-founders should have known each other a long time. Founder fit with the company matters as much as product fit with the market.
- "one of the biggest predictors of success was had the co-founders known each other for a long time." (S109, undated)
- "If you're a solo founder and you can't hack, learn." (S092, undated)
- "I've come to believe that founder/company fit is as important as product/market fit." (S012, 2020-05)
- Undated: two or three founders is ideal, diversity of background is good but diversity of vision is fatal (S131); split equity near equal, early, with vesting (S132).
- "The rate of improvement is often more important than the current absolute ability." (S015, 2020-01)

## Remote work and location
- **Stance:** Against remote early on; expects the most important companies to be in person. The Bay Area is still the best place, with housing cost as the threat.
- "in the early days of a startup, uh where communication and speed outweigh everything else, um for whatever reason, video conferencing or calls just don't work that well." (S132, title 2014)
- 2013-09: do not hire remote employees while early culture is gelling (S018).
- 2023-01: skeptical of fully remote; the most important companies of the decade will be heavily in person (S045).
- 2019-02, against coworking for startups: "The single thing that has differentiated YC more than any other decision we've made is that we do not have a coworking space." (S025)
- Undated: the Bay Area is still best, but housing costs and short job tenures hurt hard tech (S131, S134).

## Work hours
- **Stance:** Extreme effort for a period of life is required, and it is fine to choose not to.
- "Work really hard. Everyone wants a secret to success other than this; if it exists, I haven't found it yet." (S014, 2015-02)
- "I think people who pretend you can be super successful professionally without working most of the time (for some period of your life) are doing a disservice." (S002, 2019-01)
- But hours are second to direction: "It is much more important to work on the right thing than it is to work many hours." (S002, 2019-01)

## Burnout and hard times
- **Stance:** Resilience is learnable. Talk about the low points, keep perspective, and reframe pain rather than avoid it. He admits fatigue.
- "a lot of founders end up pretty depressed at one point or another, and they generally don't talk to anyone about it." (S078, undated)
- "Startups are not life-and-death matters... it's just work." (S078, undated)
- "I just decided that I was going to like learn to love the hard parts" (S035, 2025-07)
- "the opposite of a bad experience is no experience." (S050, 2026-03)
- 2025-09: "I haven't had a good night of sleep since ChatGPT launched." (S049) 2026-07: "I'm tired. I don't know if so I've been doing this a long time. It's tiring." (S047)

## Culture and mission
- **Stance:** Founders set culture early and it is hard to change. A clear mission is the retention tool. Values are articles of faith.
- "I'd go so far as to say that a company that is not mission-oriented will have a hard time being really successful because of talent retention problem." (S063, undated)
- 2013-09: diversity of values inside a startup is bad (S018).
- 2025-06: "the special thing about OpenAI is we've managed to build a culture that is good at repeatable innovation." (S037)
- Undated: "To get the really good ideas, we need to tolerate really bad and wacky ideas too." (S090)

## Decision speed
- **Earlier (2015-02):** once in company mode, make most decisions fast, within about an hour (S014).
- **Newer (undated, recent OpenAI interview):** "at our scale. Now, I've learned that it's much better to spend a lot of time trying to get to the right decision." (S102)
- **Read:** fast at startup scale where mistakes are cheap; slow at the scale where one call moves billions. Inferred from S014, S102.

## Corporate structure
- **Earlier (2023):** OpenAI's capped-profit structure is not cosmetic; it shapes daily decisions (S122, title 2023). It was meant to let them act against shareholder interest when safety requires it (S051, 2026-09).
- **Newer (2024 to 2026):** do not innovate on legal structure. "I would heavily discourage any startup that was thinking about starting as a nonprofit and adding a for-profit arm later." (S023, 2024-03) "we made a mistake to try to innovate in our structure in the beginning" (S047, 2026-07)

## Vertical integration and platform
- **Vertical integration, earlier (undated; in 2025-10 he says he was "always" against it):** against it.
- **Newer (2025-10):** "I was always against vertical integration and I now think I was just wrong about that." (S039) For AGI he now wants to own chips, data centers, research, and product together.
- **Platform:** "A business strategy I've always really respected is like the platform plus killer app together." (S045, 2023-01) 2026-08: "I don't think we should go build every product category." (S038) 2026-09: "we really do love developers." (S048)

## Selling the company and IPO
- On selling a startup: no recorded position. Undated, he advises not mentioning potential acquirers in a seed pitch because it signals you are not trying to build a big company (S065).
- IPO, 2025-12: "am I excited to be a public company CEO? 0%." (S040) 2026-09: "I would say not 2026." (S051)
- What appeals to him, undated: letting ordinary people own shares: "that is probably the single most appealing thing about it to me." (S116) On a falling stock if mission comes first: "Then it falls. Maybe it falls a lot, I don't know." (S106)

## Speed of AI adoption
- **Earlier (2021 to 2023):** expected fast change; in 2021 he wrote that within five years programs would read legal documents and give medical advice (S005).
- **Newer (2026):** "Society and the economy will adapt more slowly." (S038, 2026-08) He calls his education forecast "a prediction error for me." (S030, 2026-06) And the whole field missed: a 2019 audience shown today's models would have expected the economy to be upended, and it has not been (inferred from S047). "anytime you're that wrong and that confident, which I think we were as a field" you have to update (S047, 2026-07)
- On the technology itself he has not moved: "the systemic mistake is always to assume that progress is about to S-curve off" (S123, undated)
- **Read:** the technology moved as fast as he said; society moved slower. Weight the newer view when judging go-to-market timing.

## Jobs and AI
- **Earlier (undated essay):** software would "create wealth but destroy jobs." (S070)
- **Newer (2025 to 2026):** not a jobs doomer, but honest that some jobs vanish. "I'm not a jobs doomer at all" (S050, 2026-03). "I think it's important to be honest about that there will be some jobs that totally go away" (S035, 2025-07)
- "human demand seems limitless. Our ability to imagine new things to do for each other seems limitless." (S115, undated)
- "And the entry-level people, I think, will be the people that do the best here. They're the most fluent with the tool to tools." (S115, undated)

## Regulation, safety, and power
- **Earlier (2023):** asked for a licensing agency: "we think that regulatory intervention by governments will be critical to mitigate the risks of increasingly powerful models" (S128, Senate hearing, undated).
- **Newer (2024 to 2026):** light touch below the frontier. "it' be a mistake to go regulate heavily regulate like current capability models" (S042, 2024-05). ## Risk
- **Stance:** Most people overrate risk and underrate the risk of doing nothing, especially when young.
- "Look for small bets you can make where you lose 1x if you're wrong but make 100x if it works." (S002, 2019-01)
- "Inaction is a particularly insidious type of risk." (S011, 2023-12)
- "when you're young and you have nothing to lose is absolutely the time to take risk" (S130, undated)
- "The most expensive investing mistake in the world to make is to be a pessimist." (S025, 2019-02)
- His own limit, undated: he says risk management was never his strong suit, and he once put half his savings into one company (S130).

## Side projects and explore mode
- **Stance:** Start as a project, not a company. Low stakes let you entertain big, odd ideas.
